01 Before Stephenson
Ask most people who gave us the railroad and you'll hear Vanderbilt, or if you ask in Britain, George Stephenson and his engine Rocket, which won a famous contest in 1829. But twenty-five years before Rocket, on February 21st, 1804, a Cornish engineer named Richard Trevithick ran a steam locomotive on rails at an ironworks in south Wales, and it pulled five wagons, ten tons of iron and about seventy men who climbed on for the ride, nearly ten miles down the line to Abercynon. The reason you've never heard of him is the track. The rails were cast iron and they kept cracking under the weight of the engine, so it proved the idea and then went nowhere as a railway. Trevithick died broke in 1833, and local workers paid for his funeral. The national museum of Wales calls him the real father of the railways.

02 The Doctor Boston Forgot
Every March 30th, America marks National Doctors' Day, and almost nobody knows why it's that date. On March 30th, 1842, a country doctor in Georgia named Crawford Long put a man named James Venable under ether and removed a tumor from his neck. The one everybody learns about came four years later in Boston, October 16th, 1846, when William Morton gave a public demonstration of ether, and that's the one that made the textbooks. The difference is Long didn't publish until 1849, so by the time anyone read about his operation, Boston already had the credit. Doctors' Day started in Winder, Georgia, in 1933 to honor Long's operation, and Congress made it national in 1990, so the date the whole country uses is his. And Long wasn't first either, a surgeon in Japan beat him by thirty-eight years.

03 The Pizza Letter
The story goes that in 1889 a pizza maker in Naples named Raffaele Esposito made a pizza for Queen Margherita with tomato, mozzarella and basil, the colors of the Italian flag, and the palace sent him a thank-you letter, and that letter is the proof everybody points to. In 2014 a historian named Zachary Nowak took a close look at it and found three problems. The seal is in the wrong place, the handwriting doesn't match the official who supposedly signed it, and it's addressed using his wife's last name, Brandi, instead of his own. Nowak thinks it was probably made up later, likely by the Brandi family, who ran the pizzeria from the 1930s, as marketing, and that's his argument, it isn't proven. But the pizza itself was already in print in 1858, when a Naples writer described pizzas topped with basil, thin slices of mozzarella and sometimes tomato, about thirty years before the queen's supposed visit.

04 Crapper Didn't
Thomas Crapper's name is on every toilet joke you've ever heard, and a lot of people will tell you he invented the thing, and he didn't. In 1596 Sir John Harington built a flushing toilet with a water tank for Queen Elizabeth the First and wrote up how it worked in a pamphlet called The Metamorphosis of Ajax. In 1775 Alexander Cumming patented the S-bend, the curve in the pipe that holds a little water so the sewer smell can't come back up. Crapper came along in the late 1800s, a real sanitary engineer with real patents on things like the ballcock, the float in the tank that shuts the water off, so he made toilets better, he just didn't invent them. And the word crap is older than he is. The idea that he invented the toilet got a push from a 1969 book called Flushed with Pride, which was partly a joke.
