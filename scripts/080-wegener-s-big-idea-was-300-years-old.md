## 01 Cold Open

Every kid notices that South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces, and so did a mapmaker in 1596. Alfred Wegener usually gets the credit for the idea that the continents move, and he deserves a lot of it, because he's the one who built the scientific case. But the idea that the Atlantic coasts used to be joined goes back more than three hundred years before him, to a Flemish mapmaker who looked at his own maps and saw it.

## 02 The Famous Version

So the version most of us got in school goes like this. In 1912 a scientist named Alfred Wegener put forward continental drift, the idea that the continents haven't always been where they are now, that they've moved.

And that's widely taught as where the idea started, right? Wegener and continental drift go together the way Edison and the lightbulb do. But the puzzle-piece part, the thing that makes the idea feel so obvious once you see it, other people saw a long time before 1912.

## 03 Ortelius, 1596

The first name is Abraham Ortelius, a Flemish mapmaker. In 1596, in a book called Thesaurus Geographicus, he suggested that the Americas had been torn away from Europe and Africa, and he blamed earthquakes and floods for it.

And here's the part that matters. He wrote that the vestiges of the rupture reveal themselves if you put a world map in front of you and look carefully at the coasts. So he's saying exactly what every kid says, put the map down, look at the coastlines, and you can see where they came apart. He saw it. He saw it in 1596, more than three hundred years before Wegener.

And this isn't just a nice story somebody dug up. The US Geological Survey tells it in its own history of the idea, and a peer-reviewed paper in the journal Nature in 1994 identified Ortelius as a forerunner of continental drift.

## 04 The Maps of 1858

And he wasn't the only one. Other people noticed the fit over the years too, but the next big one is Antonio Snider-Pellegrini. In 1858 he published a pair of before-and-after maps, one with the Americas and Africa joined together, and one with them pulled apart.

Now, to be fair, we don't know what Snider-Pellegrini thought pushed them apart, so we're not going to guess. What we do know is that in 1858, more than fifty years before Wegener, there was a published map showing the Americas and Africa joined together, and another showing them split, and that's the whole idea, drawn out on the page. And the US Geological Survey tells that part too, and so does a short history of plate tectonics from a research group called IRIS, which lists Ortelius, Bacon, Franklin and Snider-Pellegrini as people who noticed the fit.

## 05 What Wegener Actually Did

Now, to be fair to Wegener, and this is a big part of it, what he did was different. Ortelius and Snider-Pellegrini guessed from the shape of the coasts, and Ortelius blamed earthquakes and floods, and that's a guess, not a case, right? A matching shape on a map could be a coincidence, and the only way past that is evidence that comes from somewhere other than the shape.

Wegener was the first to build a full scientific case, and he didn't just use the coastlines, he used fossils, and rocks, and glaciers, and the edges of the continental shelves, the underwater edges of the continents. So the fair way to say it is that Ortelius and the others were first to notice, not first to prove, and Wegener was the first to build the full case. First to notice and first to build the case are two different things, and the second one is Wegener's.

## 06 Who Saw It First

So the plain version goes like this. Alfred Wegener put forward continental drift in 1912, and he built the first real scientific case for it, with fossils and rocks and glaciers and shelf edges. But in 1858 Antonio Snider-Pellegrini had already published maps of the Americas and Africa joined and then separated, and in 1596 Abraham Ortelius wrote that if you put a world map in front of you and look carefully at the coasts, the vestiges of the rupture reveal themselves.

Somebody did it first, and in this case it was Abraham Ortelius, in 1596, more than three hundred years before Wegener.
