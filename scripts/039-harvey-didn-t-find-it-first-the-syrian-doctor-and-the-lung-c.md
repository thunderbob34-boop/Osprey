## 01 Cold Open

Nearly four hundred years before William Harvey, a Syrian doctor had already worked out where your blood goes when it leaves the right side of your heart. Harvey gets the credit for discovering how blood circulates, and he did something really important that we'll give him full credit for. But the first person to describe how blood travels through the lungs was a doctor named Ibn al-Nafis, in the mid-1200s, and there was a second man who described it before Harvey too, and his book was burned along with him.

## 02 The Famous Version

The version most of us learned goes like this, in 1628 a doctor named William Harvey published a book called De Motu Cordis, and in it he discovered the circulation of the blood. And it stuck because Harvey really did show something huge, and because he's the name in the textbooks. If you learned about the heart in school, his is the name you got, right? But that version folds two different things together, and pulling them apart is the whole story here.

## 03 Two Loops

So here's the plain version of what your heart does. The right side of your heart pumps blood out to your lungs, and the left side pumps it out to the rest of your body. So there are really two loops, a short one through the lungs, and a big one through the whole body, right? And for a long time, the old teaching, going back to Galen, was that blood got from the right side of the heart to the left side through invisible pores in the wall between them.

## 04 Ibn al-Nafis

Now here's the part that matters, Ibn al-Nafis was a Syrian physician, born around 1210 to 1213, and he died in 1288. In a commentary he wrote, usually dated to about 1242, he described the lung circuit, the short loop. In his account, the blood goes from the right ventricle, which is the right pumping chamber of the heart, through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, it mixes with air there, and then it comes back through the pulmonary vein to the left ventricle.

And he said no to Galen's invisible pores, he rejected the idea that blood seeps through the wall of the heart. So that's the lung circuit, described correctly, by about 1242, and I say about because the exact date of that commentary isn't certain, so the safe way to put it is the mid-1200s.

## 05 Lost for Centuries

And then it disappeared, at least as far as Europe was concerned. His work wasn't translated in his own time, and Europe didn't know about it until his manuscript was rediscovered in 1924. That's nearly seven hundred years after he wrote it.

Now, to be fair, some scholars argue about whether any knowledge of his work reached Europe before that, and that question is still debated, nobody has settled it. But there's no known evidence that Harvey or Servetus, who we're about to get to, ever saw what Ibn al-Nafis wrote. So I'm not saying anybody copied him, I'm saying he got there first, right?

## 06 Servetus and the Burned Book

And there's more, because there's a second person who beat Harvey to the lung circuit. In 1553, a man named Michael Servetus described how blood passes through the lungs, on his own, independently of Ibn al-Nafis. Britannica describes it as the first accurate description of the pulmonary circulation in Europe.

But he didn't put it in a medical book, he put it inside a book about theology, called Christianismi Restitutio. And that same year, 1553, Servetus was burned at the stake in Geneva, and copies of that book were burned along with him. Only three original copies are known to survive today. So the first accurate description in Europe of how blood moves through the lungs was in a theology book, and only three copies of that book are known to have made it.

## 07 Fair Credit to Harvey

Now, to be fair to Harvey, what he did is a big deal, and it's different from what the other two did. Ibn al-Nafis and Servetus described the lung loop, the short one. Harvey proved the whole circuit, the big loop through the entire body, and he proved it by experiment and by measurement, in 1628.

So Harvey earned his place, and the fair way to say it is that Harvey proved the full circulation, and Ibn al-Nafis described the lung circuit centuries before him.

## 08 Who Did It First

So here's how it lines up. Around 1242, Ibn al-Nafis, a Syrian physician, described blood going from the right side of the heart through the lungs and back to the left, and he rejected Galen's invisible pores. Europe didn't know about it until 1924. In 1553, Michael Servetus described the same lung circuit on his own, inside a theology book that was burned with him, and only three copies survive. And in 1628, William Harvey proved the full circuit through the whole body.

Harvey gets the name, and he earned it for the whole circuit, but somebody did it first, and the lung circuit was described by Ibn al-Nafis in the mid-1200s.
