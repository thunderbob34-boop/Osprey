## 01 Cold Open

Eighteen centuries before Copernicus, a Greek astronomer put the Sun in the middle, and one of the main reasons we know about it is that Archimedes mentioned it in a book about counting grains of sand. Copernicus is the name everybody learns as the man who put the Sun at the center, and he did something enormous, and we'll give him full credit for it. But he wasn't the first person to say the Earth goes around the Sun, and here's the strange part, in his own handwritten draft, he named the man who was.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here's the story most of us got in school, in 1543 a book came out called De revolutionibus, by Nicolaus Copernicus, and it put the Sun in the middle and the Earth going around it, and that book is why Copernicus gets called the founder of the sun-centered model. And that's the version that stuck, because it's a great story, one man with one book changing the whole picture of the sky.

But the idea itself, the idea that the Earth moves around a Sun that stays put, didn't start with Copernicus. It was around a very, very long time before him, and it came from Greece. And it's worth stopping on how big an idea that is, because every day of your life you watch the Sun come up on one side of the sky and go down on the other, and nothing under your feet feels like it's moving. So saying the Sun is the one standing still and the Earth is the one moving goes against everything you see, and somebody said it anyway, a very long time ago.

## 03 Aristarchus of Samos

His name was Aristarchus of Samos, Samos being a Greek island, and he lived from about 310 to about 230 BCE. And Aristarchus proposed that the Earth goes around a Sun that doesn't move. Now here's the catch, his own writing on that is lost, we don't have it, so everything we know about his sun-centered idea comes to us secondhand.

And one of the people we get it from is Archimedes, the famous Greek mathematician. Archimedes wrote a book called The Sand-Reckoner, before 216 BCE, and it's a book about counting grains of sand, and in the middle of it he reports Aristarchus's theory. We also have it from Plutarch, a later Greek writer. So the oldest sun-centered model we know about only survived because other people bothered to mention it.

And if you do the math on the dates, that puts Aristarchus roughly eighteen centuries before Copernicus, which is how the Dictionary of Scientific Biography puts it too, eighteen centuries.

## 04 What Copernicus Actually Did

Now, to be fair to Copernicus, this matters a lot. Aristarchus had the idea, but he had no full mathematical system behind it, and Copernicus built one, he worked out a full mathematical system, put it in a book, and got it printed, and that is a lot more than a good idea, right? An idea is one sentence, but a system is something other astronomers can actually check and work with.

So the fair way to say it is that Aristarchus was first with the idea, as far as we know, and Copernicus was the one who built the idea into something that could change how people saw the sky. Both of those things are true at the same time.

## 05 The Crossed-Out Line

And now here's the part most people never hear. Copernicus's handwritten manuscript of De revolutionibus still survives, it's in the Jagiellonian Library in Kraków, in Poland. And in that manuscript, Copernicus actually mentioned Aristarchus by name.

Then the passage was crossed out, and when the book was printed in 1543, that passage isn't in it. Britannica notes it, and the University of Rochester's library has written about it too, the mention was there in the handwritten version and it was gone from the first printed edition.

Now, it would be easy to turn that into a story about Copernicus hiding where he got the idea, and we're not going to do that, because nobody knows who crossed it out or why. All we can say is that it was crossed out. So Copernicus had written Aristarchus's name into his own draft, and it didn't make it into print, and for the readers of that 1543 book, the Greek who had the idea first wasn't credited with it.

## 06 Who Did It First

So here's the plain version, around eighteen centuries before Copernicus, Aristarchus of Samos said the Earth goes around a Sun that stays still. His own writing is lost, and we know about it because Archimedes mentioned it in The Sand-Reckoner and Plutarch mentioned it later. Copernicus built the full mathematical system and published it in 1543, and in his handwritten draft he named Aristarchus, and that line was crossed out before the book was printed. The draft is still sitting in Kraków. Somebody did it first, and his name was Aristarchus.
