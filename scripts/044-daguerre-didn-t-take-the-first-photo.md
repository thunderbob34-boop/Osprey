## 01 Cold Open

The world's oldest surviving camera photograph wasn't taken by Daguerre, and it's sitting at the University of Texas. Daguerre is the name on the process, the daguerreotype, and his announcement in 1839 is commonly treated as the invention of photography. But more than ten years before that, a man named Nicéphore Niépce pointed a camera out of a window, and what he got is still around.

## 02 The Famous Version

The famous version is pretty simple. In 1839 Louis Daguerre announced the daguerreotype, and that's usually where the history of photography starts. And it stuck for an easy reason, his name is literally on the thing. You don't have to remember who made a daguerreotype, it tells you. And Daguerre's process was faster and practical, which matters, and we'll get to that.

But a name on a process is not the same as being first, right? And the earliest photograph we still have isn't a daguerreotype.

## 03 The View From the Window

In 1826 or 1827, a man named Nicéphore Niépce made a picture called View from the Window at Le Gras. It's the earliest surviving camera photograph in the world, and Britannica describes it as the first permanent photograph. He made it on a pewter plate coated with something called bitumen of Judea, which is a kind of natural asphalt, and he called his process heliography.

And here's the part that tells you how early this was. The exposure took at least eight hours. So this was not point and shoot. He set the camera up looking out of the window and just left it there, for hours.

Now, I keep saying surviving on purpose. Niépce made earlier attempts that were lost, so this isn't the first photograph he ever tried, it's just the earliest one that still exists. And it still exists in Texas. It's held at the Harry Ransom Center at the University of Texas at Austin, and they're the ones who own it.

## 04 Fair Credit to Daguerre

Now, to be fair to Daguerre, he didn't just take somebody else's picture and put his name on it. Britannica describes Daguerre building on what Niépce had done, and the process Daguerre ended up with was faster and practical. An exposure of at least eight hours is fine for a view out of a window, but it's not going to work for much else, and Daguerre's faster process is a big part of what made photography practical.

So the fair way to say it is that Niépce made the first photograph we still have, and Daguerre made photography practical. Those are both big deals, they're just not the same deal.

## 05 One More Name

Oh, one more thing. Daguerre and Niépce aren't the only names in this story. A man named Fox Talbot was working on photography at the same time, as a separate inventor, on his own track. So even the idea that photography came from one man in one place doesn't really hold up. A few people were working on the same problem, and the earliest result that survives happens to be Niépce's.

## 06 Who Was First

So who took the first photograph? The honest answer is the earliest one we still have was made by Nicéphore Niépce, in 1826 or 1827, a view out of his window at Le Gras, on a pewter plate coated with bitumen, exposed for at least eight hours. It's at the Harry Ransom Center at the University of Texas at Austin. Daguerre's process came out in 1839 and made photography practical, and his name stuck to it. Somebody did it first, and you can go and look at it in Texas.
