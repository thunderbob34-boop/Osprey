# Fulton Wasn't First: John Fitch's Steamboat Ran in 1790

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 5 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 756 spoken words.
- Tone is calm and matter-of-fact, and fair to Fulton throughout. This is not a story about a thief, so keep the voice level.
- Say Clermont as KLAIR-mont, Rumsey as RUM-zee, Bardstown as BARDZ-town, Livingston as LIV-ing-stun.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "first commercially successful", "advertised, regular passenger service", "the first profitable venture in steam navigation", "was unable to make it a commercial success", "short-lived", "we don't know" and "on the suggestion that" exactly as written, because they are what keeps the channel credible.
- Do not say "first steamboat" for Fitch anywhere, and do not say Fulton copied or stole anything. The script says neither on purpose.
- Visuals: nothing sensitive. Period prints, maps of the Delaware and the Hudson, and text cards are fine.

## 01 Cold Open

Ask almost anybody who invented the steamboat and you'll hear Robert Fulton, but seventeen years before Fulton's Clermont ran on the Hudson, a man named John Fitch was running a scheduled steamboat passenger service on the Delaware River out of Philadelphia, and Fitch had died in 1798, before Fulton launched a steamboat at all. So Fulton wasn't first with a steamboat service on a timetable, and I want to be careful about how we say that, because Fulton has a real claim, the fair way to put it is that his was the first commercially successful steamboat, which is a big deal and we'll give him full credit for it.

## 02 The Famous Version

The famous version is simple, in August 1807 Fulton's Clermont ran on the Hudson River, and the popular belief is that this is where Robert Fulton invented the steamboat. Now here's the part that matters, the real claim for the Clermont is about money, and Britannica puts it this way, it calls the Clermont "the first profitable venture in steam navigation." That's a fair claim, and it's a different thing from getting a steamboat to run and carry passengers, which is what Fitch had already done.

## 03 John Fitch

John Fitch lived from 1743 to 1798, and here's what he did. In March 1787 the state of New York gave him the exclusive right to run steamboats on its waters for fourteen years, and Britannica says he got exclusive rights from New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware as well, and then on August 22nd, 1787, a 45-foot steamboat of his had a successful trial on the Delaware River at Philadelphia, in front of delegates to the Constitutional Convention.

Then in 1790 a larger, paddle-driven boat of his ran advertised, regular passenger service between Philadelphia and Burlington, Bristol, and Trenton, so that's a steamboat on a schedule, carrying passengers, seventeen years before the Clermont. And there's more, on August 26th, 1791, Fitch got a US steamboat patent, and he got a French one the same year.

Now, to be fair, Fitch wasn't the only person working on this, James Rumsey got his own federal patent in the same period, and the two of them were rivals, so we're not saying Fitch built the first steamboat anywhere. The claim is narrower than that, it's a scheduled passenger service on the Delaware in 1790, and that's the one we can back up.

## 04 What Fulton Did

So the reason Fulton gets the credit is that Britannica says Fitch "was unable to make it a commercial success." Fitch's service was real, but it was short-lived and it was unprofitable, and Fulton's Clermont in 1807 is the one Britannica calls the first profitable venture in steam navigation. That's what Fulton did, he made a steamboat pay, and a boat that runs and a boat that pays for itself are two different problems, right? Fitch got a boat running and Fulton is the one who made one pay.

And Fulton didn't do it alone. He was born in Pennsylvania in 1765, and in Paris in 1801 he met Robert Livingston, the American minister, the man who held a twenty-year steamboat monopoly in New York. Fulton came back to New York in December 1806 to supervise a boat the two of them had planned in Paris, and the Clermont was built in New York City with Livingston's financial backing. It went from New York City to Albany, about 150 miles, in 32 hours, and it went on to carry paying passengers.

And I'm not saying Fulton copied Fitch, because nothing we found says that, and we don't know whether Fulton ever knew about Fitch's work.

## 05 New York

There's one more piece of this, and it's a legal one. In Gibbons against Ogden, the Supreme Court's record says that in March 1798 New York passed an act on the suggestion that Fitch was dead or had left the state without having made any attempt to use his privilege, and that was a few months before Fitch actually died. The exclusive right in New York later went to Livingston and Fulton, and I'm not putting a motive on any of that, it's just what the record says.

## 06 The Plain Version

Fitch died on July 2nd, 1798 in Bardstown, Kentucky, before Fulton launched a steamboat at all, so he never saw the Clermont run. So here's the plain version, John Fitch was running advertised, regular passenger service by steamboat on the Delaware in 1790, and Robert Fulton's Clermont in 1807 is what Britannica calls the first profitable venture in steam navigation. Somebody did it first, and Fulton is the one who made it pay.
