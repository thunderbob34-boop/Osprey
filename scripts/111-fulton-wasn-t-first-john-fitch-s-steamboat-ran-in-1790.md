# Fulton Wasn't First: John Fitch's Steamboat Ran in 1790

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 5 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 813 spoken words.
- Tone is calm and matter-of-fact, and fair to Fulton throughout. This is not a story about a thief, so keep the voice level.
- Say Clermont as KLAIR-mont, Rumsey as RUM-zee, Bardstown as BARDZ-town, Livingston as LIV-ing-stun.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "first commercially successful", "advertised, regular passenger service", "the first profitable venture in steam navigation", "was unable to make it a commercial success", "short-lived", "we don't know" and "on the suggestion that" exactly as written, because they are what keeps the channel credible.

## 01 Cold Open

Ask almost anybody who invented the steamboat and you'll hear Robert Fulton, but seventeen years before Fulton's Clermont ran on the Hudson, a man named John Fitch was running a scheduled steamboat passenger service on the Delaware River out of Philadelphia, and Fitch had died in 1798, before Fulton launched a steamboat at all. So Fulton wasn't first, and I want to be careful about how we say that, because Fulton has a real claim, the fair way to put it is that his was the first commercially successful steamboat, which is a big deal, and we'll give him full credit for it. But the steamboat running on a timetable was Fitch's, and Fitch is a name most people have never heard.

## 02 The Famous Version

The famous version is simple, in August 1807 Fulton's Clermont ran on the Hudson River, and the popular belief is that this is where Robert Fulton invented the steamboat. Now here's the part that matters, the real claim for the Clermont is about money, and Britannica puts it this way, it calls the Clermont "the first profitable venture in steam navigation." That's the claim, it's about being profitable, and it's a fair one, and it's a different thing from being the first person to put a steam engine in a boat and make it carry people.

## 03 John Fitch

John Fitch lived from 1743 to 1798, and here's what he did. In March 1787 the state of New York gave him an exclusive right to run steamboats on its waters for fourteen years, and Britannica says he got exclusive rights from New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware as well. Then on August 22nd, 1787, a 45-foot steamboat of his had a successful trial on the Delaware River at Philadelphia, in front of delegates to the Constitutional Convention.

Then in 1790 a larger, paddle-driven boat of his ran advertised, regular passenger service between Philadelphia and Burlington, Bristol, and Trenton, so that's a steamboat on a schedule, carrying passengers, in 1790, and that's seventeen years before the Clermont. And there's more, on August 26th, 1791, Fitch got a US steamboat patent, and he got a French one the same year.

Now, to be fair, Fitch wasn't the only person working on this, James Rumsey got his own federal patent in the same period, and the two of them were rivals. So we're not saying Fitch built the first steamboat anywhere, the claim is narrower than that, and it's the one we can back up, a scheduled passenger service on the Delaware in 1790.

## 04 What Fulton Did

So why does Fulton get the credit? Britannica says Fitch "was unable to make it a commercial success," and that's the answer, Fitch's service was real, but it was short-lived and it was unprofitable. Fulton's Clermont in 1807 is the one Britannica calls the first profitable venture in steam navigation, and that's what Fulton did, he made a steamboat pay, which is a different thing from making one run, right? A boat that runs and a boat that pays for itself are two different problems, and Fitch got the first one working and Fulton is the one who got the second.

And Fulton didn't do it alone. Fulton was born in Pennsylvania in 1765, and in Paris in 1801 he met Robert Livingston, the American minister, who had a twenty-year monopoly on steamboat navigation in New York. Fulton came back to New York in December 1806 to supervise the building of a steamboat that the two of them had planned in Paris, and the Clermont was built in New York City with Livingston's financial backing. In August 1807 it went from New York City to Albany, about 150 miles up the Hudson, in 32 hours, and it carried paying passengers on that route.

And I'm not saying Fulton copied Fitch, because nothing in what we found says that. We don't know whether Fulton ever knew about Fitch's work.

## 05 New York

There's one more piece of this, and it's a legal one. The Supreme Court's own record, years later, describes how in March 1798 New York passed an act on the suggestion that Fitch was dead or had left the state without making the necessary improvements, and that was a few months before Fitch actually died. The exclusive right in New York later went to Livingston and Fulton. I'm not putting a motive on any of that, it's just what the record says about the right Fitch was given in 1787.

## 06 The Plain Version

Fitch died on July 2nd, 1798 in Bardstown, Kentucky, before Fulton launched a steamboat at all, so he never saw the Clermont run. Here's the plain version, John Fitch was running advertised, regular passenger service by steamboat on the Delaware in 1790, Robert Fulton's Clermont in 1807 is what Britannica calls the first profitable venture in steam navigation, and Fitch was already gone by then. Somebody did it first, and that's what this channel is about.
