## 01 Cold Open

Three years before Britain built its first tank, an Australian engineer sent the War Office a design for one, and they shelved it. Britain gets the credit for the tank, and the first tank prototype ever finished was British, a machine called Little Willie, built in 1915. But in 1912, a South Australian engineer named Lancelot de Mole had already sent the British War Office plans for a tracked, armored vehicle that could cross trenches, and they put it on the shelf. And then there's a twist at the end of this one that cuts the other way, so stick around for it.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here's the version most people know. The tank is a British invention, from the First World War, and the names that go with it are William Tritton and Walter Wilson, who built Little Willie, along with Swinton, and Churchill's Landships Committee.

And Little Willie really does matter, it was built in 1915, it was the first tank prototype ever completed, it never saw combat, and it's the oldest surviving tank. You can still go and see it today at the Tank Museum in Bovington, in England. So when people say Britain built the first tank, they're pointing at something real.

## 03 Lancelot de Mole

But a few years before Little Willie, there was a man in Australia working on the same problem. His name was Lancelot de Mole, an engineer from South Australia, and he came up with his idea in 1911. And in 1912 he sent his design to the British War Office, plans for a tracked, armored vehicle that could cross trenches.

Tracked meaning it ran on tracks, the long belts that a tank rolls along on, instead of wheels, and that's the whole trick of a tank, right? Tracks spread the weight out, so the machine can get over rough ground that would stop a wheel, and de Mole's plans were built around crossing trenches. And the War Office rejected it, they shelved it, and this is 1912, three years before Little Willie.

## 04 He Tried Again

Now here's the part that matters. De Mole didn't give up after the first no, and in 1915 he sent his design in again. And then in 1917, a model of his machine was built in Melbourne, and that model was sent to England, where it was misplaced for about six weeks. So even the model got lost for a while, but it survived, and it's now at the Australian War Memorial, so you can go and see his version too, just on the other side of the world from Little Willie.

## 05 The Royal Commission

After the war, Britain set up a Royal Commission on Awards to Inventors, which was a panel that decided awards for inventors, and that was in 1919. Oh, and you might see 1929 for this in one place online, and that's just an error, it was 1919.

The Commission looked at de Mole's claim, and there's a line of praise from its findings that's widely quoted, so I'll give you the gist rather than the exact words, which is that back in 1912 he'd come up with a brilliant invention that got there ahead of the tank Britain actually used in 1916, and in some ways did it better. That's a big thing for an official commission to say about a man the War Office had turned down, right? And there's a paper trail for his claim, the State Library of South Australia holds a letter about it in its collection.

And here's the twist that cuts the other way. The same Commission found no evidence that his work had any influence on the tanks Britain actually built. So as far as the Commission could find, de Mole wasn't robbed of his idea, there's no evidence the British tank was copied from him, and Tritton and Wilson and the Landships Committee got there without him. De Mole was just there first, on paper, and nobody used it.

## 06 What He Got

Now, as for what de Mole got for all this, the Commission gave him about nine hundred and sixty-five pounds, and that was for his expenses. And in 1920 he was made a CBE, which is a British honour, Commander of the Order of the British Empire. So he got money for his expenses and an honour, and his name in the record, and that's about it.

Now, to be fair to Britain, the people who built Little Willie did build the first tank prototype ever completed, and the Commission found no evidence they took anything from de Mole, so that's their achievement and it stands. But a few years before them, a man in Australia had the idea, drew it up and sent it in, and was told no.

## 07 Who Was First

So the plain version is this. Lancelot de Mole, a South Australian engineer, came up with his tank in 1911 and sent the plans to the British War Office in 1912, a tracked, armored vehicle that could cross trenches, and they shelved it. Little Willie came in 1915, the first tank prototype ever completed, and it never saw combat and it still survives at Bovington. The Commission in 1919, in that widely quoted line, said his 1912 design got there ahead of the tank Britain used in 1916, and in some ways did it better, and it also found no evidence that his design shaped the tanks they built. Somebody did it before Britain did, and that somebody was Lancelot de Mole.
