## 01 Cold Open

The plastic age didn't start in a New York lab in 1907, it started at a London exhibition hall in 1862. Bakelite usually gets called the first synthetic plastic, and in a very real sense it was, but forty-five years before Bakelite, visitors to London's 1862 International Exhibition were already looking at a moldable, man-made plastic called Parkesine, named after the man who made it, Alexander Parkes.

## 02 The Famous Version

So the version you'll usually hear goes like this. In 1907, in Yonkers, New York, Leo Baekeland came up with Bakelite, and it's widely called the first synthetic plastic. And that's not some random internet claim, either, because in 1993 the American Chemical Society made Bakelite its very first National Historic Chemical Landmark.

So that's a big name with a big honor behind it, right? And nothing in this video is going to take that honor away, because when you get into the chemistry, Bakelite really does keep a title. But it's not the title most people think it is.

## 03 Parkes and Parkesine

Here's who came before. A British inventor named Alexander Parkes made a material he called Parkesine, and according to Britannica he patented it in 1861, though other sources give different dates. And in 1862 he showed it at the International Exhibition in London, in South Kensington. And the Science Museum in London has a bronze medal from that 1862 Exhibition in its collection, and by the museum's own record it was awarded to Parkes, so somebody at that exhibition noticed what he'd brought.

Parkesine was made from nitrocellulose, which is cellulose, the stuff plants are built from, treated with acid, and the thing that made it a plastic is that you could heat it, mold it into a shape, and it would hold that shape. So that's a moldable, man-made material, on show in London, in 1862, forty-five years before Bakelite.

And it didn't stop with Parkes. John Wesley Hyatt came along with celluloid, which was patented in 1870, so there were at least two of these early plastics around well before 1907. Put the dates in a row and it goes Parkesine on show in 1862, celluloid patented in 1870, and Bakelite in 1907, so by the time Bakelite showed up, man-made plastics had already been around for more than forty years.

## 04 The Catch

Now here's the part that matters, and it's why Bakelite still gets to keep a title. Parkesine and celluloid were what's called semi-synthetic. They started from cellulose out of plants, and then got treated and turned into something new, so they're man-made, but they're built on a natural starting material. Bakelite didn't start from a plant material like that, so it's the first fully synthetic plastic, and that title stands.

So the fair way to put it is this. Parkesine was the first man-made plastic, and Bakelite was the first fully synthetic plastic. Both of those are true at the same time, right? And to be fair, even one museum page gets this tangled up and calls Parkesine fully synthetic, and that's not right on the chemistry, so we're not going to say it.

## 05 Why You've Never Heard of It

And there's a reason Parkesine isn't a household name, and it's not a mystery, it was a commercial failure. It was expensive, it cracked, and it was flammable, and a material that costs a lot and cracks and catches fire is not a material that's going to take over the world.

So Parkes was first with a man-made plastic, and he got it in front of the public in 1862, but he didn't make it a success. And that's a pattern you see over and over on this channel, the person who did it first isn't always the person who made it work, and the person who made it work usually ends up with the name.

## 06 Who Did It First

So the plain version goes like this. Bakelite, 1907, Yonkers, New York, is the first fully synthetic plastic, and the American Chemical Society honored it in 1993, and that's earned. But Alexander Parkes patented Parkesine in 1861, by Britannica's date, and showed it at London's International Exhibition in 1862, a man-made plastic you could heat and mold and that held its shape, forty-five years before Bakelite, with Hyatt's celluloid patented in 1870 in between.

Somebody did it first, and in this case it was Alexander Parkes, in London, in 1862.
