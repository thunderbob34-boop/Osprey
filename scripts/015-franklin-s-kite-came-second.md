# Franklin's Kite Wasn't First

## 01 Cold Open

The first man to draw sparks out of a thunderstorm wasn't Ben Franklin, it was a retired French soldier following Franklin's own instructions. And that's not a story about somebody stealing Franklin's idea, the French said straight out that it was Franklin's idea, they just did it first. So the famous kite was real, as far as we can tell, but it wasn't first, it came about a month after a man near Paris most people have never heard of.

## 02 The Famous Version

Here's the version everybody knows. In June 1752, Benjamin Franklin went out in a thunderstorm with a kite, and he proved that lightning is electricity. It's one of the most famous science stories there is, and you can see why, it's a famous name, a storm, a kite, and a big idea proven right.

But the thing people skip is that Franklin had already written up the idea and published it, a proposal for an experiment to test whether lightning was electrical. And once that proposal was out in the world, anybody who read it could try it, right? And somebody did.

## 03 Marly-la-Ville

On May 10th, 1752, at Marly-la-Ville near Paris, a team working for a man named Thomas-François Dalibard followed Franklin's published proposal. They set up a tall iron rod, and they insulated it with wine bottles and silk, so the electricity in the rod couldn't just run off into the ground. And then they waited for a storm.

And when a storm passed over, Dalibard wasn't the one standing at the rod. The man there was a retired dragoon named Coiffier, and a dragoon is a soldier who rode to battle on horseback. Coiffier was standing in for Dalibard, and following Dalibard's instructions, he drew sparks from that iron rod. That's the first time anybody drew electricity out of a thunderstorm, and it was a retired soldier who did it.

Three days later, Dalibard reported it to the Académie des Sciences, and here's the part that matters, he credited Franklin's proposal. He didn't say it was his idea, he said they'd done what Franklin suggested. And eight days after Marly, on May 18th, a man named Delor repeated the experiment in Paris. So by the middle of May 1752, it had been done twice in France.

## 04 The Kite

Now the kite. According to Priestley's account, Franklin flew his kite in June 1752, and nobody knows the exact day. That's about a month after Marly, and according to Franklin, he flew it before the news from France had reached him. So if you take Franklin at his word, he wasn't copying anybody, he was running his own test, he just wasn't first.

But it's worth knowing that this rests on Franklin's own account. The news of the French success was printed in the Pennsylvania Gazette on August 27th, 1752, and Franklin's own account of the kite is dated October 19th, 1752. So the French result was in print before Franklin's kite story was, and his own account of the kite didn't come until October.

And there's more, a few skeptics even doubt the kite flight happened as told. Now, to be fair, that's a minority view, and Franklin scholars like I. Bernard Cohen don't share it. So I'm not saying the kite didn't happen, I'm saying the kite rests on Franklin's own account and Priestley's, and Marly rests on a report to the Académie three days after it happened.

## 05 Fair Credit to Franklin

Now, to be fair to Franklin, this was his idea. The test at Marly was his proposal, the French followed it, and Dalibard said so in his own report. That's a big deal, and it's the reason his name is on this story at all. Franklin worked out that lightning might be electrical, and he worked out how to test it, and he put it in print where other people could use it.

So the fair way to say it is, Franklin had the idea and published the method, and a French team ran it first and proved him right, and the kite came about a month later. It's a better story than most people know, right? Because Franklin comes out of it looking good, he just doesn't come out of it first.

## 06 Oh, One More Thing

Oh, one more thing, while we're on electricity, the word electric has its own credit problem. Ancient Greeks noticed that if you rubbed amber, it would pull in little bits of straw and feathers, and our word electric comes from elektron, which is Greek for amber. Then in 1600, an English physician named William Gilbert coined the Latin word electricus, in his book De Magnete.

You'll often hear that the Greek philosopher Thales discovered the amber trick, traditionally around 600 BC. Now, later writers credit Thales with that, but nothing Thales wrote survives, not a word. We only have it secondhand, through Aristotle and through a later writer named Diogenes Laertius, centuries after Thales. So the amber story is old and it's real, but putting Thales's name on it is tradition, because we don't have it from Thales himself.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's the order. Franklin published a proposal for testing whether lightning was electrical. On May 10th, 1752, at Marly-la-Ville, a retired dragoon named Coiffier, working for Dalibard and following Franklin's proposal, drew sparks from a thunderstorm. Delor did it again in Paris on May 18th. Franklin flew his kite in June, about a month later, before the news reached him, according to Franklin, and his account of it is dated October 19th. Franklin had the idea, and the French gave him the credit for it. But somebody did it first, and it was a retired soldier standing at an iron rod.
