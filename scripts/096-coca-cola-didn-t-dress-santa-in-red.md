# Coca-Cola Didn't Dress Santa in Red

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 5 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 727 spoken words. The research is short, so this one runs short rather than padded.
- Tone is warm and easy, it's a Christmas episode. Coca-Cola is not the bad guy here, the company corrects the myth itself, and it gets full credit for making Santa's look famous worldwide.
- Say Haddon Sundblom as HAD-un SUND-blum, and Thomas Nast as NAST, rhymes with "fast."
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "Nast created the look, and red versions were around long before Coke", "mostly black and white", "popularized", "standardized", "probably owes a lot", "probably why it stuck" and "nobody can point to the very first red Santa" exactly as written.
- Never say Nast's original 1860s drawings showed a red suit, and don't ad-lib other companies' Santa ads from before 1931. Neither was verified.
- Visuals: Nast's Harper's Weekly Santas are public domain. For Coca-Cola's ads and web pages, show the company's own history page as a screenshot with the headline, not their artwork full screen.
- Good on-screen text moments are the 1860s, 1881, 1823, 1931, and the line from Coca-Cola's own history that Santa wore red before Sundblom.

## 01 Cold Open

There's a Christmas story so widespread that the company it flatters has had to correct it on its own website. The story goes that Santa Claus wears red because Coca-Cola made him red, to match Coke's own red. But Coca-Cola's own official history says it plainly, Santa was wearing a red coat before their artist ever painted him in 1931. So the company that gets the credit is the one telling you it wasn't them.

## 02 The Coke Version

The famous version goes through an illustrator named Haddon Sundblom, who started painting Santa for Coca-Cola's ads in 1931, and Sundblom's Santa was inspired by a Christmas poem by Moore, from 1823. So you've got Santa in a red suit, in ads for a company whose color is red, and you can see how people put those two things together, right?

And that's the popular belief, that Coke made Santa red to match its brand. It's a tidy story, it sounds like the kind of thing a big company would do, and that's probably why it stuck. And the campaign itself is real history, the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History has a whole piece on Coke's Santa, called How Santa Brought Coca-Cola in from the Cold, so nobody's saying the ads weren't a big deal. The only question is the red.

## 03 Coca-Cola Says No

Now here's the part that matters. Coca-Cola's own company history page, the one about Haddon Sundblom and the Coca-Cola Santas, says Santa appeared in red before Sundblom ever painted him. And the company has a page on rumors and misinformation that addresses the myth too.

Think about that for a second. This is a story that makes Coca-Cola look good, it gives them credit for the way Santa looks, and they're the ones saying it isn't true. A company admitting something against its own interest is about as good as a source gets, so when Coke says it didn't make Santa red, I believe them.

## 04 Thomas Nast

As for the modern Santa, a lot of his look comes from a cartoonist named Thomas Nast, who drew Santa for Harper's Weekly from the 1860s through the 1880s, including a Santa from 1881. The Smithsonian calls Nast the Civil War cartoonist who created the modern image of Santa Claus, and Nast's drawings set the look we still think of, the plump Santa with the fur trim and the belt. And the Smithsonian has another piece, The Evolving Face of Santa, that walks through how his look changed, so the point is that Santa's look was built up over time, by more than one artist, long before 1931, right?

Now, I want to be careful here, because Harper's Weekly was mostly black and white, so I'm not going to tell you Nast's original drawings from the 1860s showed a red suit. What I can tell you is that Nast created the look, and red versions were around long before Coke. Red-suited Santas showed up in prints, on cards and in illustrations well before 1931, and that fits with what Coca-Cola's own history says.

## 05 Fair Credit to Coke

Now, to be fair to Coca-Cola, what they did is a really, really big deal, and every source agrees on it. Coca-Cola popularized Santa's look and standardized it, all over the world. Standardized just means they made one version of Santa the standard one, the one people everywhere picture, so when you think of Santa today, the one in your head probably owes a lot to Sundblom's paintings. Britannica's way of putting it is that Coke didn't invent the red Santa, but it did popularize him, so the credit Coke gets for making that Santa famous is earned, it's just that the red was there before Coke got to him.

## 06 Who Did It First

So here's the plain version. Thomas Nast's drawings for Harper's Weekly, from the 1860s into the 1880s, set the look of the modern Santa, the plump one with the fur trim and the belt, and Santa was showing up in a red suit in prints and cards and illustrations well before 1931. Then Haddon Sundblom started painting him for Coca-Cola in 1931, and Coke made that Santa famous all over the world, and Coca-Cola's own history says Santa wore red before they ever got to him. Somebody did it first, and nobody can point to the very first red Santa, but it came before Coca-Cola, and Coca-Cola is the one saying so.
