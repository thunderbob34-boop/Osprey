## 01 Cold Open

James Cook is called the man who discovered Australia, and you'll still hear it said that way, Captain Cook found Australia in 1770. But Cook was at least the third group to get there, and the first had been there for at least fifty thousand years. And even if you only count Europeans, a Dutch ship landed there in 1606, and ten years after that a Dutch captain nailed up a pewter dish on the west coast, and you can still go and see that dish today. So let's go through who actually got there first, and then what Cook actually did, because what he did was real, it just wasn't discovering Australia.

## 02 The Famous Version

The famous version goes like this. In 1770 Captain James Cook sailed the Endeavour along the coast of Australia, and that's the voyage most people have in their heads when they say he discovered it. Cook's real distinction, and it is a real one, is that he charted the east coast and claimed it for Britain, and because of that claim his voyage is the one people tend to remember.

Now, charting a whole coastline is a hard hard job, and claiming it for a crown changed everything that came after, so this is not a small footnote. But charting a place and claiming a place are different things from finding a place, right? And on the question of who found it, Cook was very, very late.

## 03 The First Australians

The first people to reach Australia were the ancestors of Aboriginal Australians, and they got there at least fifty thousand years ago. That's the safe number, at least fifty thousand years. There's a site in northern Australia called Madjedbebe, and in 2017 a team of researchers published a study in the journal Nature arguing that people were living there by about sixty-five thousand years ago. So it's possibly sixty-five thousand years, but that date is still debated, so the number to hold onto is at least fifty thousand.

Either way, by the time any European ship turned up, people had been living in Australia for tens of thousands of years. Cook arrived in 1770. You can see the size of the gap there, and that's why he's at least the third group to get there, and not even close to the first.

## 04 The Duyfken

Now here's the part most people never hear. If you only count Europeans, Cook still wasn't first. In early 1606 a Dutch ship called the Duyfken, with a captain named Willem Janszoon, landed at the Pennefather River, on the western side of Cape York, which is the big peninsula at the very top of Australia. That's the first recorded European landing in Australia, and Janszoon charted more than three hundred kilometers of that coast, and he did it a hundred and sixty-four years before Cook.

And I say first recorded on purpose. You'll hear theories about earlier visits, Portuguese sailors, or Makassan trepangers, people who came down to collect sea cucumbers, and none of those are proven. Maybe somebody got there before the Duyfken and maybe they didn't, we just don't know. But the Duyfken landing is on the record, and the record is what we go by on this channel.

## 05 Hartog's Dish

And then, ten years later, another Dutch captain shows up, and this is my favorite part of the whole story. On the 25th of October, 1616, Dirk Hartog landed on the west coast of Australia, and he left something behind. He took a pewter dish, a plate basically, flattened it out, had it inscribed, including the name of his ship, and nailed it up. That's 1616, a hundred and fifty-four years before Cook.

And then the dish just stayed there. Eighty-one years later, in 1697, another Dutch captain named de Vlamingh came along and found it, half-buried in sand. So it had been sitting there for eighty-one years, and it was still a hundred and fifty-four years older than Cook's voyage.

Today that dish is in the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam. It's the oldest European object ever left in Australia, the earliest one that survives, a flattened pewter plate with a Dutch ship's name on it.

## 06 Fair Credit to Cook

Now, to be fair to Cook, none of this makes his voyage small. Look at where everybody landed. Janszoon was on the western side of Cape York, up at the top. Hartog was on the west coast. And Cook charted the east coast, the other side of the continent, and then he claimed it for Britain.

So the honest way to put it is this. Cook didn't discover Australia, and he wasn't the first European to land there either. What he did was chart the east coast and claim it for Britain, and that claim is the thing that made his voyage matter so much. That's real, and it's a big deal, it's just a different thing from being first.

## 07 Who Was First

So who found Australia first? The ancestors of Aboriginal Australians, at least fifty thousand years ago, and possibly sixty-five thousand. The first recorded European landing was Willem Janszoon on the Duyfken, at the Pennefather River in 1606. Dirk Hartog came ten years later, in 1616, and left a pewter dish that's now in Amsterdam. And James Cook came in 1770, charted the east coast and claimed it for Britain. Somebody did it first, and in this case a lot of people did it first, and the very first of them got there tens of thousands of years before Cook was born.
