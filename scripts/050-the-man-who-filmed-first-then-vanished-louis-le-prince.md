## 01 Cold Open

In 1890, the man who made the world's oldest surviving film stepped onto a train, and nobody ever saw him again. His name was Louis Le Prince, and two years before that, in October 1888, he shot film in a back garden in Leeds, in England. That was seven years before the Lumière brothers' famous screening, and that film still exists, and nobody knows what happened to the man who shot it.

## 02 The Famous Version

If you ask who invented the movies, you'll usually get one of two answers. One is the Lumière brothers, who put on a public screening in 1895, and the other is Thomas Edison, with his Kinetograph camera and his Kinetoscope viewer. And both of those are real, the Lumières really did put on that public screening, and Edison's name really is on that camera and that viewer.

But neither of them shot the oldest film that survives, that was Le Prince, and most people have never heard of him. Britannica even has a whole article on who invented the first motion picture camera, which tells you the credit question isn't a simple one.

## 03 Roundhay Garden Scene

Louis Aimé Augustin Le Prince was born in 1842, and on the 14th of October, 1888, he filmed what's now called Roundhay Garden Scene. He shot it in the garden of his in-laws' home in Leeds, with a single-lens camera he built himself, and it's the oldest surviving film in the world.

The Science Museum Group in Britain holds two frames from it, with notes written on by his son, Adolphe. And Leeds, the city, still tells the story, its museums call it the oldest surviving film.

Now, I'm saying oldest surviving on purpose. There were earlier experiments with moving pictures, so this isn't the first time anybody ever tried it, right? And Le Prince never showed his films publicly, so the fair way to say it is that he made the oldest film we still have, and the Lumières put on the famous public screening.

## 04 The Train

And then here's the part that turned him into a mystery. On the 16th of September, 1890, Le Prince got on a train from Dijon to Paris, and he was never seen again, and nobody knows what happened to him. He was declared legally dead in 1897, seven years later, which is what happens when somebody's been missing long enough that the law treats them as dead.

By the museums' account, he had been planning a trip to the United States. So he'd built his own camera, he'd shot film that still survives today, he had plans to go to America, and then he got on a train in France and was gone.

## 05 The Theories

Now, whenever somebody vanishes like that, people come up with theories, and there are murder theories about Le Prince, and some of them even claim Edison was behind it, and the Le Prince family believed that. But there is no evidence for it, none, so it's speculation. And I'm not going to say Edison had anything to do with it, and I'm not going to say Edison took his camera either, because nobody has ever shown that.

Now, to be fair to Edison and the Lumières, they did things Le Prince never got to do. The Lumières put their films in front of the public in a screening, and Edison had his camera and his viewer, and those are real contributions, they're just not the same thing as shooting the oldest film that survives, right?

## 06 Who Was First

So who made the first film? The honest answer is that the oldest surviving film in the world is Roundhay Garden Scene, shot by Louis Le Prince on the 14th of October, 1888, in his in-laws' garden in Leeds, with a camera he built himself. That's seven years before the Lumière brothers' famous screening in 1895. On the 16th of September, 1890, he got on a train from Dijon to Paris and was never seen again, and he was declared dead in 1897, and we still don't know what happened to him. Somebody did it first, and then he disappeared.
