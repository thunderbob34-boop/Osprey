## 01 Cold Open

Polynesians and Native Americans met around the year 1200, almost three centuries before Columbus, and the evidence is in people's DNA. When most of us think about who the great ocean navigators were, we think of Columbus and the European explorers, but Polynesian navigators were crossing open ocean without a compass or a chart centuries before any of them. Now, I want to be straight with you up front, the meeting is real, but who sailed to whom is still an open question, and I'll show you exactly why.

## 02 The Credit Europe Gets

So here's the version most of us grew up with. The great age of ocean navigation belongs to the European explorers, the ships, the compasses, the charts, and Columbus is the name at the top of that list. When people talk about who first crossed big oceans on purpose and found their way, that's usually who they mean.

And it stuck because that's the history that got written down in the books most of us read, right? But there's a whole other ocean in this story, the Pacific, and the people who settled it were doing long-distance navigation a long time before any of those European ships showed up.

## 03 The Wayfinders

Polynesian navigators settled a huge triangle of ocean, from Hawai'i, to Aotearoa, which is New Zealand, to Rapa Nui, which is Easter Island, and they did it without compass or chart. They steered by the stars, by the swells, by the clouds and by the birds. So they read the ocean itself, and they read the sky.

And there's more, because out in the Marshall Islands, people built maps of the ocean swells out of sticks and shells. The curved sticks are the swells and the shells are the islands, and there were different kinds, too, so the mattang was a teaching chart, for learning how swells run into each other, the rebbelib covered a big area, both chains of islands, and the meddo covered one section of a chain. So these weren't maps of coastlines the way a European chart is, they were maps of how the water moves, and museums like the British Museum and the Met have real ones in their collections.

## 04 It Was Luck

Now here's the part that matters, some scholars in the West didn't believe the Polynesians had found their islands on purpose. In 1956 a historian named Andrew Sharp argued that they must have found them by accident, drifting, boats blown off course that happened to land somewhere.

And then in 1976 a voyaging canoe called Hōkūle'a sailed from Hawai'i to Tahiti. It left Hawai'i on May 1st, 1976, made landfall at Mataiva on June 1st, and reached Papeete, in Tahiti, on June 4th, and the navigator was a master navigator named Mau Piailug, who used no instruments at all. No compass, no chart, just the stars and the swells and everything else the old navigators used.

Now, to be fair, one voyage doesn't settle a whole argument by itself, so the careful way to say it is that Hōkūle'a challenged the drift theory and helped overturn it. But it showed that a traditional navigator, without instruments, could take a canoe from Hawai'i to Tahiti on purpose, and that's a long way to go by accident.

## 05 The DNA

So that's the navigation, and now for the part in the title. In 2020 a team of researchers, Ioannidis and colleagues, published a study in Nature, and what they found was Native American DNA in people from Eastern Polynesian islands. The Native American ancestry is closest to Indigenous groups from coastal Colombia and Ecuador.

And they could date when that mixing happened, roughly, island by island. In the South Marquesas they put it at about 1150, and on Rapa Nui at about 1380, and they put the contact itself at around 1200. So around the year 1200, Polynesians and Native Americans met and their families mixed, and that's centuries before Columbus, almost three centuries. And it's not just one study, either. In 2024 another paper in Nature looked at ancient genomes from the Rapanui people, the people of Rapa Nui, and it also found contact with the Americas before any Europeans arrived.

## 06 Who Sailed To Whom

But here's the thing the DNA can't tell us. It can't tell us who sailed to whom. The researchers themselves say they can't tell where the meeting happened. Maybe Polynesians sailed east and landed in South America and came back, or maybe people from South America ended up in Polynesia, and the researchers point out that Latin American fishermen have drifted to Polynesia before. So "Polynesians reached the Americas" is still unproven, and the honest version is that they met, and we don't know where.

Oh, one more thing, the sweet potato. Sweet potatoes come from the Americas, and they were in Polynesia before Columbus, there are burned sweet potato remains on Mangaia, in the Cook Islands, from about 1000 CE. That looks like a clue, right? But in 2018 a study in Current Biology argued that the sweet potato got to Polynesia on its own, by long-distance dispersal, long before people did, and that idea is contested. So the sweet potato is a real puzzle, and people still argue about how it got there.

And if you want a firm date for somebody reaching the Americas before Columbus, there is one, it just comes from the other side of the world. At L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland, Norse visitors were cutting trees in 1021, and we know the exact year because of a spike in the tree rings left by a burst of cosmic rays back in 993. Now, that's the year the trees were cut, not necessarily the year anybody first got there, and that's a whole other story. But it means Columbus wasn't the first European over there either, right?

## 07 The Plain Fact

So here's the plain fact, Polynesian navigators settled a huge triangle of the Pacific without compass or chart, steering by stars and swells and clouds and birds, and in 1976 Mau Piailug took Hōkūle'a from Hawai'i to Tahiti with no instruments at all. And around 1200, almost three centuries before Columbus, Polynesians and Native Americans met, and the DNA shows it, even though it can't tell us who made the trip. The European explorers get the credit for the great age of ocean navigation, and they earned their part of it. Somebody did it first, and it was the navigators of the Pacific, centuries earlier.
