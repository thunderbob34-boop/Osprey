# Koch Didn't Discover the Cholera Germ First

## 01 Cold Open

The same year John Snow traced cholera to a London water pump, an Italian scientist was already looking at the germ that causes it under his microscope, and almost nobody noticed for a hundred years. The name most people learn is Robert Koch, the scientist who's credited with discovering the cholera germ in the 1880s, and Koch did real, important work on it. But the first man to describe it was Filippo Pacini, in 1854, and today his name is written right into the germ's official name.

## 02 The Koch Version

So here's the version most people learn. Robert Koch went to Egypt and India in 1883 and 1884, he went after cholera, and he found the bacterium that causes it, and that's the discovery his name is attached to.

And it stuck because Koch didn't just see the thing, he nailed it down, right? Koch was the first to grow the organism in pure culture, which means growing that one germ on its own in the lab, with nothing else mixed in, so you can study it. And he made the case that convinced science that this germ was the cause of cholera. That's a really, really big deal, and it's why his name is on it in most people's heads.

## 03 Pacini, 1854

Now here's the part that matters. Thirty years before Koch, in 1854, an Italian anatomist named Filippo Pacini, working in Florence, looked at the intestines of cholera victims, and under the microscope he described tiny comma-shaped vibrios. And he didn't just say he'd seen something odd, he said the cause of cholera was a living organism, something that reproduced. And that comma shape stuck, an essay about cholera in a CDC journal is even titled Persistence of Memory and the Comma Bacillus.

So in 1854 Pacini had described the germ and said it was alive and that it was the cause, and that's the same year John Snow traced cholera in London to a water pump. So that's two people in two countries in the same year, both getting close to the truth about cholera from different directions.

## 04 Why Nobody Noticed

But Pacini published in an Italian journal that few people read, and that's the whole problem, his description was out there, it was in print, but it was in a place most of the scientists working on cholera just never saw.

And that's part of why Koch gets the credit, and it's why I'm not going to tell you Koch stole anything. The sources say Koch probably didn't know about Pacini's work, and they word it as an assumption, because nobody can say for sure. So as far as anybody can tell, Koch found the same germ thirty years later without knowing Pacini had already described it.

Now, to be fair to Koch, there's a real difference between what the two of them did. Pacini described and identified the germ, and Koch grew it in pure culture and made the case that convinced science. So Pacini was first to see it and say what it was, and Koch was the one who finished the job in a way nobody could ignore.

## 05 The Name On The Germ

And there's more, because Pacini's story has a better ending than you'd expect. In 1965, more than a century after Pacini's paper, the international committee that rules on the names of bacteria made a decision about the cholera germ, and they ruled that its official name is Vibrio cholerae Pacini 1854.

In biology, a formal name can carry the name of the person who described the thing and the year they did it, and that's what this is. So every time the germ's full formal name is written out, it says Pacini, and it says 1854, it doesn't say Koch and it doesn't say 1884. It took more than a hundred years, but the official record ended up putting the name of the man who described it first right on the germ, right?

And people are still writing about him. The epidemiology department at UCLA, on its pages about John Snow, has one titled First Discoverer of Vibrio Cholerae, and it's about Pacini. And a peer-reviewed microbiology journal ran a paper called The Enigma of Pacini's Vibrio Cholerae Discovery, so scientists are still writing about it.

## 06 The Plain Fact

So here's the plain fact, Robert Koch was the first to grow the cholera germ in pure culture, in 1884, and he made the case that convinced science, and he deserves the credit for that. But in 1854, the same year John Snow traced cholera to a London water pump, Filippo Pacini, an Italian anatomist in Florence, described comma-shaped vibrios in cholera victims and said the cause was a living organism. He published in a journal few people read, and almost nobody noticed. In 1965 the committee that names bacteria made it official, Vibrio cholerae Pacini 1854. Somebody did it first, and it was Filippo Pacini, in 1854.
