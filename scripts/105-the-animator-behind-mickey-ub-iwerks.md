# The Animator Behind Mickey: Ub Iwerks

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 5 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 666 spoken words.
- Tone is warm and fair to both men. This is shared credit, not a takedown, and both the Smithsonian and Britannica already name Iwerks.
- Say Iwerks as EYE-werks, and Ub as UB, rhyming with tub. Say Oswald as OZ-wuld and Mintz as MINTS.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "according to the Smithsonian", "probably", "co-created", "tasked with designing", "Britannica says", "the way the sources describe it" and "around 1928" exactly as written. Do not say Walt had nothing to do with it, and do not say Steamboat Willie was the first anything.
- Visuals: period stills of the two men, early cartoon title cards and text cards. Nothing sensitive here.
- Good on-screen text moments are May 15 1928, November 18 1928, 700 drawings a day, 1930, 1936, 1940 and 1947.

## 01 Cold Open

Walt Disney gave Mickey Mouse his voice, he voiced him until 1947, and a lot of people picture Walt as the man who created Mickey, but according to the Smithsonian, the man who was tasked with designing Mickey was Ub Iwerks, and Britannica says most of the early animation was probably Iwerks's work. Now, this isn't a case of somebody being erased, both of those sources name Iwerks, and Walt gets real credit too, but Iwerks is a name a lot of people haven't heard.

## 02 The Famous Version

The famous version is that Walt created Mickey, and there's real truth in that. Britannica says Mickey was co-created by Disney and Iwerks, so Walt is right there in the credit, and the way the sources describe it, he supplied the idea, the gags, the characterization and the voice. Mickey and Minnie first appeared in a cartoon called Plane Crazy, which Britannica dates to May 15th, 1928, and Steamboat Willie premiered on November 18th, 1928.

## 03 Two Friends From Kansas City

Before any of that, Walt and Ub were partners, and they'd been partners in Kansas City before either of them went to Hollywood. Britannica has them founding a studio there in 1922 called Laugh-O-gram Films, and the Walt Disney Family Museum describes an even earlier business the two of them tried, a short-lived commercial art company. So when Mickey comes along, this isn't a boss and a stranger he hired, it's two guys who'd already been in business together more than once, right?

## 04 Losing Oswald

Here's where the story turns. Walt had been making cartoons about a character called Oswald the Lucky Rabbit, and around 1928 the distributor of those cartoons, a man named Charles Mintz, took the character away. Both Britannica and the Walt Disney Family Museum say that, and the museum says a lot of Walt's animators went over to Mintz with him, and that Iwerks was one of the few who stayed behind. So Walt needed a new character, and Iwerks was one of the people still there.

## 05 What Iwerks Did

According to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, Ub Iwerks was tasked with designing the character, with input from Walt. Britannica says Iwerks drew Mickey in the early years, and that most of the early animation was probably his work, and I'm keeping that word probably, because probably is Britannica's word.

Now here's a number that tells you how much drawing that was. Britannica says that at his peak, Iwerks completed 700 drawings a day on Plane Crazy, and the Walt Disney Family Museum also gives 700 drawings a day for him in those early Mickey years. I can't tell you more than that about how it was split up, but 700 a day is a lot of pencil work for one man.

## 06 Fair Credit

Now, to be fair to Walt, Britannica also says Iwerks worked under Walt's close supervision, so I'm not going to say Walt had nothing to do with it, because he had a lot to do with it, right? Britannica's account of Steamboat Willie has the same split, with Iwerks drawing Mickey while Walt created the character and gave him his voice. Co-created is the right word, and it's the word Britannica uses.

## 07 What Happened to Iwerks

Oh, one more thing, because the story doesn't stop in 1928. In 1930 Iwerks left Disney to run his own animation studio, and both Britannica and the Walt Disney Family Museum say that studio closed in 1936. After that he directed cartoons for other producers, and in 1940 he came back to Disney, and Britannica says he stayed there until he died. So the man who drew Mickey left the company, ran his own studio for about six years, and then went back to Disney.

## 08 Close

So Walt supplied the idea, the gags, the characterization and the voice, and Iwerks, according to the Smithsonian, was the one tasked with designing Mickey, and Britannica says he probably did most of the early animation. Mickey was co-created by the two of them, and Iwerks is the half a lot of people haven't heard of.
