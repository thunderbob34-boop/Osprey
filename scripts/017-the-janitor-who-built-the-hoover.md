# The Janitor Who Built the Hoover

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 6 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 885 spoken words. The research is thin, so this one runs short rather than padded.
- Tone is warm and plain. Spangler is the underdog, Hoover is the businessman who backed him, and nobody in this story is a crook.
- Say Spangler as SPANG-ler.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "patented in 1908", "the portable upright electric vacuum", "not the vacuum cleaner itself" and "I couldn't pin down how much he earned" exactly as written.
- Don't give a patent number, sources disagree on it. Don't ad-lib Spangler's age or any family link to the Hoovers, neither one was pinned to a source.
- Good on-screen text moments are Aug 30 1901, "Puffing Billy", 1907, 1908, and Canton, Ohio.

## 01 Cold Open

The most famous vacuum name in the world belonged to a man who made horse harnesses, and the guy who actually built it was the janitor. His name was James Murray Spangler, he was a janitor in Canton, Ohio, and in 1907 he built a portable electric suction sweeper out of, among other things, the motor from a ceiling fan and a pillowcase. And then, because he couldn't afford to make them, the whole thing ended up with somebody else's name on it.

## 02 The Hoover Version

Hoover is one of those brand names that turned into a word. People say they're going to hoover the carpet, the same way people say they're going to google something, and the name and the thing are basically the same word. So it's natural to assume that the man named Hoover, William H. Hoover, invented the vacuum cleaner, right?

But William H. Hoover wasn't an inventor of vacuums. He was a leather goods maker, he made harnesses, the straps and gear you put on a horse, that's what his business was, and the machine that made his name famous was built by somebody else.

## 03 The Janitor

James Murray Spangler worked as a janitor in Canton, Ohio, and in 1907 he built a portable electric suction sweeper. And the way he built that first prototype is my favorite part of this whole story, because he took the motor from a ceiling fan, and for the bag that caught the dust, he used a pillowcase.

And if you think about how a vacuum works, that makes a lot of sense. A fan pulls air in, the air drags the dust along with it, and something with tiny holes in it lets the air through and keeps the dust. So a fan motor and a pillowcase is basically the whole idea, built out of whatever a janitor could get his hands on.

He filed for a patent, and it was patented in 1908. Now, the patent details vary a little depending on which source you read, so I'm going to keep it at that, patented in 1908. The point is that the patent was his, the janitor's.

## 04 The Harness Maker

Now here's the part that matters, Spangler had a working machine and a patent, but he couldn't fund production, he didn't have the money to make these things and sell them, and that's where William H. Hoover comes in. Hoover bought in, and Hoover's company, the Electric Suction Sweeper Company, took the patent, and they kept Spangler on.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History has a Hoover upright vacuum cleaner in its collection, and its record lays it out the same way, Spangler the janitor, 1907, Hoover the harness maker, and the patent assigned to Hoover's company. That's how a janitor's invention ended up with a harness maker's name on it.

And one thing I'd love to tell you and can't is how much Spangler got out of the deal, because I couldn't pin down how much he earned from it, so I'm not going to guess.

## 05 Spangler Wasn't First Either

And there's more, because Spangler didn't invent the vacuum cleaner either. What he invented was the portable upright electric vacuum, not the vacuum cleaner itself. Before him there were hand-powered and mechanical sweepers, and there was a man in Britain named Hubert Cecil Booth.

Booth patented a powered suction cleaner on August 30th, 1901, six years before Spangler built his. And Booth's machine was nothing like the one in your closet, it was huge, it was pulled by a horse, and people nicknamed it Puffing Billy. So nobody was pushing Booth's cleaner around a living room, it was a machine you needed a horse to move, right?

So if you want to be strict about it, the vacuum cleaner doesn't belong to Hoover, and it doesn't belong to Spangler either. What Spangler did was take the idea of a powered suction cleaner and make it something one person could push around a room, with a fan motor and a pillowcase. The Smithsonian's own magazine ran a piece on this with the headline The Vacuum Cleaner Was Harder to Invent Than You Might Think, and that's about right.

## 06 Fair Credit to Hoover

Now, to be fair to Hoover, he did something Spangler couldn't. Spangler had the machine and couldn't fund it, and Hoover had the money and put it behind the machine, and his company kept Spangler on. That's real, and that's how Spangler's machine got into production.

But putting money behind a machine and building the machine are two different things. Hoover made it a business, and the name on the business is the name that stuck, but the guy who built the thing was the janitor.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's the order. In Britain, Hubert Cecil Booth patented a powered suction cleaner on August 30th, 1901, a huge horse-drawn machine nicknamed Puffing Billy. In 1907, in Canton, Ohio, a janitor named James Murray Spangler built a portable electric suction sweeper with a ceiling fan motor and a pillowcase, and it was patented in 1908. He couldn't fund production, so William H. Hoover, a harness maker, bought in, his company took the patent and kept Spangler on, and the name on the vacuum became Hoover. Somebody did it first, and in this case it was a janitor, and before him, a man with a horse-drawn machine in Britain.
