# Atari Didn't Invent the Game Cartridge: Jerry Lawson's Channel F

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 5 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 677 spoken words.
- Tone is calm and matter-of-fact. Fair to Atari, and fair to Magnavox, because the exact wording of the claim is the whole point.
- Say Fairchild as FAIR-child, Alpex as AL-pecks, Magnavox as MAG-nuh-vox, Odyssey as AH-dih-see.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "widely remembered", "the first home console with a microprocessor and game cartridges holding the software on a ROM chip", "led the team", "is credited with", "took an Alpex prototype", "in much the same way as", "as the Smithsonian describes it" and "went on to eclipse" exactly as written, because they are what keeps the channel credible.
- Never say Atari copied Fairchild, and never call Lawson the only inventor. Say he led the team.
- Visuals: nothing sensitive. Period photos of the consoles and cartridges, and text cards, are fine.
- Good on-screen text moments are 1972, 1974, Nov 1976, 1977, 1980, and 1940-2011.

## 01 Cold Open

The Atari 2600 is widely remembered as the console that brought the plug-in game cartridge into living rooms, and it came out in 1977, but the Fairchild Channel F came out in November 1976, the year before, and it was the first home console with a microprocessor and game cartridges holding the software on a ROM chip. I want to be exact about that wording, because there's an older console people bring up, and we'll get to it, and the exact wording is the reason the claim holds up. So the Atari 2600 wasn't the first to do that, and we'll give it its due too.

## 02 The Famous Version

Now, to be fair to Atari, Smithsonian Magazine says the Atari 2600 went on to eclipse the Channel F, so it makes sense that the Atari is the one most people remember, right? It's the one that stuck, and the Channel F is a console you've probably never touched.

## 03 The Man Who Led It

The man behind the Channel F was Jerry Lawson, who lived from 1940 to 2011, and he worked at Fairchild. Here's how Smithsonian Magazine tells the way he ended up with the job, he had built his own coin-operated arcade game using a Fairchild microprocessor, and when his bosses found out, they tasked him with creating a gaming division inside the company.

## 04 The Alpex Prototype

Now here's the part that matters for how we word this. Smithsonian Magazine says that in 1974 an electronics company called Alpex developed a prototype that could run games off portable microprocessors encased in protective boxes, and Alpex didn't have the means to commercialize it, so it pitched the idea to Fairchild. The Strong National Museum of Play, which holds Lawson's papers, says Lawson's team took an Alpex prototype and developed it into the Channel F. So that's the record, Lawson led the team that turned an Alpex prototype into a product you could buy, and that's why I keep saying he led the team and not that he was the only inventor.

## 05 The Channel F

The Channel F was released by Fairchild in November 1976, and that's the first home console with a microprocessor and interchangeable cartridges holding the game software in a ROM chip. The Strong's archive holds the 1976 user's manual, which is titled Fairchild Video Entertainment System, and the Computer History Museum titles its page Channel F Video Entertainment System. Lawson is credited with developing the console, and again, what the record says is that he led the Fairchild engineering team.

And here's a detail I like, Fairchild's game cartridges were loaded in much the same way as an 8-track tape, and that comes from Smithsonian Magazine and from the archives at The Strong.

## 06 The Odyssey Objection

Now, there's an objection you've probably already thought of, the Magnavox Odyssey. The Odyssey came out in 1972, and yes, it had plug-in cards, which is why the claim has to be worded carefully. As the Smithsonian describes it, the Odyssey was built from discrete analog circuits with no microprocessor, and its cards configured the console's circuits rather than holding game code, so the Odyssey had plug-in cards, and the Channel F was the first home console with a microprocessor and cartridges holding the game software on a ROM chip, and both of those are true at the same time.

## 07 After the Channel F

And I'm not saying Atari copied Fairchild, because nothing in what we found says that, and we don't know whether Atari's designers drew on the Channel F at all. What we do know about Lawson is that in 1980 he founded a company called Video Soft, and it made game cartridges for the Atari 2600 and other systems. In 2011 the International Game Developers Association honored him, and he died that same year.

So here's the plain version, the Atari 2600 in 1977 is widely remembered as the console that brought the game cartridge home, and the year before, in November 1976, Fairchild shipped the Channel F, the first home console with a microprocessor and game cartridges holding the software on a ROM chip, with Jerry Lawson leading the engineering team. Somebody did it first.
