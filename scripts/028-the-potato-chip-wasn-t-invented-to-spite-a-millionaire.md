# The Potato Chip Wasn't Invented to Spite a Millionaire

## 01 Cold Open

The potato chip's origin story has a cranky millionaire, a spiteful chef and a famous comeback, and almost none of it holds up. You've probably heard it, a rich man sends his fried potatoes back to the kitchen, and the chef gets fed up and slices them paper-thin just to spite him. But there's a recipe for potatoes fried in slices in an English cookbook thirty-six years before that story is supposed to have happened, and the most famous name in the legend has a biographer who says his part of it never happened.

## 02 The Legend

Here's how the story usually goes, it's 1853, at a place called Moon's Lake House in Saratoga Springs, and the chef is a man named George Crum. A fussy diner sends back his fried potatoes because they're too thick, so Crum, out of spite, slices them paper-thin, and according to the legend, that's how the potato chip was born.

And the diner in the most famous version of that story is the railroad tycoon Cornelius Vanderbilt, he's often named as the guy who sent the potatoes back. And you can see why the story stuck, right? It's got a villain, it's got a comeback, and it's got a railroad tycoon getting put in his place by the cook.

## 03 What Vanderbilt's Biographer Found

But here's the part that matters, Vanderbilt has a biographer named T.J. Stiles, and Stiles is a Pulitzer Prize-winning biographer. So if anybody would know whether Vanderbilt sent back a plate of potatoes at Saratoga, it's the man who wrote his biography. And Stiles looked into it and concluded, "There is no truth to the tale."

Now, to be fair, that finding is about Vanderbilt specifically, Stiles is saying the Vanderbilt part didn't happen, so the most famous version of the potato chip story, the one with the millionaire in it, is the version that doesn't hold up.

## 04 The Cookbook

And the bigger problem for the legend is that the potato chip, or something a lot like it, was already in print decades before 1853. In 1817 an English doctor named William Kitchiner published a cookbook called The Cook's Oracle, and in it there's a recipe for potatoes fried in slices. In the first edition, that's the wording, potatoes fried in slices, and in later editions it became potatoes fried in slices or shavings.

That's thirty-six years before the Saratoga story, and we're going to be careful with this one, the Kitchiner recipe is the earliest known published recipe, it's not proof that Kitchiner invented the chip, and Smithsonian says that explicitly. What it does prove is that the idea of frying potatoes in slices was sitting in a cookbook in England in 1817, long before the Saratoga story is supposed to have happened. And the book is real, the Library of Congress and the University of Leeds both have early editions of it in their collections.

## 05 Fair Credit to George Crum

Now, as for George Crum, historians agree that Crum helped popularize what was called the Saratoga chip. So Crum is a real part of this story, he didn't need a millionaire and a spite story to matter, he was a chef who helped make the Saratoga chip popular, and that's a real thing to have done. And George Crum was a Black and Native American chef, and his real part in this deserves to be told straight, without the legend stacked on top of it.

And here's the honest part, nobody knows for sure who first fried Saratoga chips. There are leads people have followed, but nothing we could confirm well enough to put on air. So we're not going to replace one legend with another. What we can say is that Crum helped popularize the chip, and that Vanderbilt's own biographer says the Vanderbilt version didn't happen.

## 06 Who Did It First

So here's the plain version, in 1817, an English doctor named William Kitchiner published a recipe for potatoes fried in slices in The Cook's Oracle, and later editions called them potatoes fried in slices or shavings. That's the earliest known published recipe, thirty-six years before the story about George Crum at Moon's Lake House in 1853. Crum helped make the Saratoga chip popular, and Vanderbilt's Pulitzer Prize-winning biographer says there's no truth to the part with Vanderbilt in it. Somebody did it first, and the oldest recipe we know of was in an English cookbook in 1817.
