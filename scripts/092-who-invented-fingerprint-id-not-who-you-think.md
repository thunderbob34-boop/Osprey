## 01 Cold Open

Scotland Yard gets the credit for fingerprint ID, and the names that usually go with it are Francis Galton and Sir Edward Henry, but the first murder ever solved by fingerprint evidence wasn't solved in London, it was solved by police in Argentina. And before Argentina, there were two other men, one in India and one in Japan, who each have a claim to being first, and the two of them ended up in a bitter public fight over it. So let's take it in order, because this story has a lot of people in it and every one of them did something real.

## 02 The Famous Version

If you ask who gave us fingerprint identification and you get an answer at all, it's usually Scotland Yard, and the system behind it is called the Galton-Henry system, named for Francis Galton and Sir Edward Henry. That's the classification system Scotland Yard adopted, and the classification part is the piece people forget about, because a fingerprint on its own doesn't help you much if you can't find the matching card in a cabinet full of them, right? You need a way to sort prints so you can go straight to the right drawer.

And that's a big part of why the credit stuck to those two names, because theirs is the system that got adopted, and it got adopted by Scotland Yard. But using prints to tell people apart started somewhere else, and it started a good while earlier.

## 03 Herschel in India

The story starts in India, with a British administrator in Hooghly named William Herschel. In 1858 Herschel had a local contractor named Rajyadhar Konai put his handprint on a contract, so that's 1858, and it was a handprint, not a fingerprint yet. But Herschel kept at it, and later on he was using fingerprints as signatures, and he described what he'd been doing in a letter dated August 15th, 1877, which people now call the Hooghly Letter.

Now, to be fair, that isn't crime-solving, that's using a print the way you'd use a signature, to show that one particular person agreed to something. But using a print as a signature only makes sense if your print is yours and nobody else's, so the basic idea was already there, in India, in 1858.

## 04 Faulds in Japan

Then there's Henry Faulds, a missionary doctor working in Japan, and Faulds went a step further. On October 28th, 1880, the science journal Nature published a letter from Faulds proposing that fingerprints left at a crime scene could be used to identify criminals. That's the first published proposal to do that, and it's the version we'd recognize today, right? You find a print where something happened and you work out whose it is.

And here's where it gets messy, because Faulds and Herschel ended up in a bitter public dispute over which of them was first. Herschel had the earlier practice, Faulds had the earlier published proposal for catching criminals, and Nature itself keeps its own record of the question, under the title The Origin of Finger-Printing. So I'm not going to pick one of them as the one true first. They were first at different things, and that's the fair way to tell it.

## 05 What Galton and Henry Actually Did

Now, to be fair to Galton, he did real work here, important work. Galton did the key statistical work showing that fingerprints are unique and that they stay stable, and that matters, because if your prints could change, or if two people could share the same ones, none of the rest of this would hold up in front of anybody.

And the Galton-Henry classification system is what Scotland Yard adopted, so the credit those two names get for a system police could actually use isn't made up. It's just that they weren't the first to use prints to tell people apart, and the first murder solved with a fingerprint wasn't theirs either.

## 06 Vucetich and the Rojas Case

And that brings us to Argentina, and a man named Juan Vucetich. Vucetich built his own classification system, and the National Library of Medicine calls it the first workable one, so he wasn't just borrowing somebody else's filing method, he built a system that worked.

And in 1892 his methods solved a murder. I'll say this plainly. Police found a bloody thumbprint on a door, and the print matched the victims' own mother, a woman named Francisca Rojas, and she confessed. That's the case, and the sources call it the first homicide solved by fingerprint evidence, so that's exactly how I'll say it. It wasn't London, it was Argentina, in 1892.

Now here's the part that matters, because this is the exact thing Faulds had proposed in Nature twelve years earlier, a print left where a crime happened, matched to one person, and it's Herschel's idea underneath that too, that a print belongs to one person and nobody else, right? So the ideas came out of India and Japan, and the first time they solved a murder, it happened in Argentina.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's who did what. Herschel had a handprint put on a contract in India in 1858 and later used fingerprints as signatures, Faulds proposed catching criminals with crime-scene prints in Nature in 1880, Galton showed that prints are unique and stable, Galton and Henry's system is the one Scotland Yard adopted, and Vucetich's methods solved the first homicide solved by fingerprint evidence, in Argentina, in 1892.

That's a lot of names for one idea, and most of them aren't the names people think of. Somebody did it first, and in this one it's more than one somebody, and the one who solved the first murder with it was working in Argentina.
