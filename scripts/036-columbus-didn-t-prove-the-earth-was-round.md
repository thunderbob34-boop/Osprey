## 01 Cold Open

Columbus didn't prove the world was round. A Greek scholar named Eratosthenes had already measured how big around it was, using the angles of shadows, about seventeen centuries before Columbus set sail. And the people who doubted Columbus in 1492 didn't think the Earth was flat, they thought it was bigger than he did, and they were right. So the story most of us learned in school has the whole thing backwards, and the hard part was done a very long time before 1492.

## 02 The Famous Version

The story goes like this, in 1492 Christopher Columbus wants to sail west, and all the experts around him think the world is flat and he's going to sail right off the edge, and he goes anyway and proves them wrong, right? It's a great story, it's got a brave guy and a room full of people who are wrong, and that's exactly why it stuck.

Now here's the part that matters, a lot of that story took off with one book. In 1828 the writer Washington Irving published a biography of Columbus, and it was embellished, it dressed the story up, and the flat Earth version of Columbus really got going from there. So the myth isn't something people believed in 1492, it's something that got told about 1492, a few hundred years later.

## 03 The Greeks Already Knew

Because educated people knew the Earth was round long before Columbus. Educated Greeks knew it centuries before him, you can trace the idea back through Greek thinkers like Pythagoras, and Aristotle pointed to observations that supported it. The historian Jeffrey Burton Russell put it this way, no educated person believed in a flat Earth after the third century BC.

So by the time Columbus was planning his trip, a round Earth wasn't some bold new idea, it was the normal educated view, and it had been for well over a thousand years. And the real argument, which we'll get to, wasn't about the shape at all. It was about the size.

## 04 Eratosthenes Measures It

And the Greek who actually put a number on the size was Eratosthenes, from Cyrene. Around 240 BC, he measured the circumference of the Earth, the distance all the way around it, and NASA describes him as the first person to calculate it.

The way he did it is simpler than you'd think. He compared the angle of the sun's shadows in two cities, Syene and Alexandria, and the difference between them came out to 7.2 degrees. Now 7.2 degrees is one fiftieth of a full circle, so if the angle between the two cities is one fiftieth of the circle, then the distance between the two cities has to be one fiftieth of the way around the whole Earth. So you take the distance between the cities, multiply it by fifty, and you've got the size of the planet, right?

And his answer comes out at roughly forty thousand kilometers, which is about twenty-five thousand miles. Now, I want to be careful with that number, because Eratosthenes didn't work in kilometers, he worked in a unit called the stadion, and nobody knows exactly which stadion he used. The local lengths ran anywhere from about a hundred and fifty-four meters to about two hundred and fifteen meters. So how close he got depends on which stadion he meant, and the fair way to say it is that he was remarkably close, maybe within a few percent, not exactly right. But that's a man measuring the whole planet with shadows, around 240 BC.

## 05 The Doubters Were Right

And this is where the story really flips. The experts who doubted Columbus didn't think the world was flat. They thought it was bigger than he did. Columbus badly underestimated the size of the planet, and he underestimated how much ocean there was, and that's why he thought Japan was only a few thousand miles west of Europe.

So when the experts said the trip was too far, they were right about the distance, and Columbus was the one who had the planet's size wrong. The people the myth calls the flat Earth crowd were actually the ones with the better numbers, right?

## 06 Fair to Columbus

Now, to be fair to Columbus, he never set out to prove the Earth was round. That was never the point of the trip. The round Earth part of his story was pinned on him much later, mostly thanks to that 1828 biography, so this particular myth isn't really his fault.

And whatever else you think about him, he did make the voyage in 1492, and that's a real thing that happened, nobody's taking the voyage away from him. What he didn't do is prove the shape of the world, because that had been settled since the Greeks, and he didn't measure it either, because Eratosthenes had done that about seventeen centuries earlier.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's how it actually lines up. Educated Greeks knew the Earth was round centuries before Columbus. Eratosthenes measured how big around it was, around 240 BC, and got remarkably close. The experts who doubted Columbus in 1492 thought the world was bigger than he did, and they were right. And the flat Earth story got attached to him in 1828, by a writer dressing up his biography. Somebody did it first, and that's what this channel is about.
