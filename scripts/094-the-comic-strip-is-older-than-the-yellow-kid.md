## 01 Cold Open

Ask who started the comic strip in America and the usual answer is the Yellow Kid, Richard Outcault's character from the 1890s, and Outcault gets called the father of the newspaper comic strip. But the Yellow Kid wasn't the first comic Americans ever read, because fifty-three years before the Yellow Kid's newspaper run, Americans were already reading a comic, and it was a pirated story from a Swiss schoolteacher, so that's the one we're talking about today.

## 02 The Yellow Kid

The famous version goes like this. Richard F. Outcault's the Yellow Kid ran in Hogan's Alley in the New York World in 1895, and that's the one that gets called the first comic strip, and it's why Outcault gets called the father of the newspaper comic strip. And there's a small wrinkle even in the famous version, because the Yellow Kid character actually first showed up in 1894, in Truth magazine, before the 1895 run in the World.

And I think the reason the credit stuck is pretty easy to see, right? It ran in a big New York newspaper, it was a success, and newspaper comic strips are the thing most of us grew up reading. So when people went looking for the first one, the Yellow Kid was the obvious place to stop, and you can see it in how it gets written up. Ohio State University's news office ran a piece with the headline History of Comics Begins with Yellow Kid, and Britannica's entry on the Yellow Kid carries the popular credit too, so this isn't some fringe idea, it's the standard version.

## 03 A Schoolmaster in Geneva

But the comic strip form is older than that, and our story starts in Switzerland, with a schoolmaster in Geneva named Rodolphe Töpffer. Töpffer drew picture stories, which is a story told in a sequence of drawings, one after another, and in 1827 he drew one called Histoire de Mr. Vieux Bois, the story of Mr. Vieux Bois.

Then from 1833 he started publishing these picture stories as albums, starting with Mr. Jabot, and in 1837 Les Amours de Mr. Vieux Bois came out, the loves of Mr. Vieux Bois. So by 1837 this Geneva schoolmaster had published picture stories, more than one of them, and that was nearly sixty years before the Yellow Kid ran in the World.

## 04 The Bootleg

Now here's the part that matters for America. In 1841 an unauthorized English version of one of Töpffer's stories came out, with a new name, The Adventures of Mr. Obadiah Oldbuck. Unauthorized means nobody asked Töpffer, so it was a pirated copy.

And then in 1842 it was reprinted in the United States, as a supplement to a magazine called Brother Jonathan, and a supplement is just an extra piece that came along with the magazine, so people picked up their copy of Brother Jonathan and there was a comic with it. That American edition was unauthorized too, it was a reprint of that English version, and the English version had itself been copied from a pirated French edition, so this story reached American readers as a bootleg. The Library of Congress has the American edition in its catalog, and Princeton University's Graphic Arts collection calls it the first comic strip published in America. And to be fair, that's their headline, their claim, and I haven't seen it checked against every other American publication from the 1840s, so I'll leave it as Princeton's claim. But either way, Americans were reading Mr. Obadiah Oldbuck in 1842, and the Yellow Kid's run in the World was 1895, and that's fifty-three years.

## 05 Fair Credit to Outcault

Now, to be fair to Outcault, his credit is real, it's just narrower than people make it. He gets credit for the first successful newspaper comic strip, and that's a real first. Töpffer's stories came out as albums, books, not as strips in a newspaper, so Töpffer didn't invent the newspaper comic strip, and I'm not saying he did.

What Töpffer did was the comic strip form, telling a story in a sequence of drawings, and Britannica's history of the comic strip gives him his place in that history. So Outcault took the form into the newspaper and made it work there, and Töpffer had done the form itself decades earlier, in Geneva, and you can give both of them their due without any trouble, right? One did the form, and the other one did the form in a newspaper.

## 06 Who Did It First

So here's the plain version. Richard Outcault's the Yellow Kid, first in Truth magazine in 1894 and then in the New York World in 1895, was the first successful newspaper comic strip. But a Geneva schoolmaster named Rodolphe Töpffer was drawing picture stories in 1827 and publishing them from 1833, and a pirated English version of one of them was sold in the United States in 1842 as The Adventures of Mr. Obadiah Oldbuck. Somebody did it first, and for the comic strip form, as opposed to the newspaper strip, that was a schoolteacher in Geneva, and Americans were reading a pirated copy of his work in 1842.
