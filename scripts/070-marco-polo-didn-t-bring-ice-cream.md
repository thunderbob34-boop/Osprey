## 01 Cold Open

Marco Polo didn't bring ice cream back from China, and food historians can't find any evidence that he did. And Catherine de' Medici didn't carry it to France either, that's the other story you'll hear, and there's no evidence for that one. The real story of how people learned to freeze a dessert doesn't start with either of them, it starts with a trick, salt and ice, and the first people to write that trick down were doctors and scholars most of us have never heard of.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here are the two stories most people have heard. The first one is that Marco Polo, the famous traveler, went to China and brought ice cream back with him. The second one is that Catherine de' Medici carried ice cream with her to France.

And food historians have looked hard at both of these. PBS calls both stories not likely true. History.com notes that European ices only show up in Italy in the early 1600s, which is about a century after Catherine. And the food historian Jeri Quinzio wrote a myth-busting history called Of Sugar and Snow that goes after stories like these. So the plain fact is that food historians find no evidence for either the Marco Polo story or the Catherine de' Medici story.

## 03 The Trick

So if not them, then where did it come from? Now here's the part that matters, because the real breakthrough wasn't a recipe, it was a trick. If you mix salt, or saltpetre, into ice or snow, it gets colder than ice on its own, cold enough to freeze things. And that's the same reason people throw salt and ice together in an old hand-crank ice cream maker, right?

Saltpetre, if you've never heard the word, is a salt-like mineral, and the history of this trick goes back a lot further than the 1600s.

## 04 The Arab Physicians

As the Stanford linguist Dan Jurafsky lays it out, in the 13th century an Arab physician named Ibn Abi Usaybi'a recorded that saltpetre chills water, and he credited an earlier physician, Ibn Bakhtawayh, from the year 1029.

Now, to be fair, and this is important, that's chilling water, not freezing food. Dissolving saltpetre in water on its own won't freeze a sorbet, the freezing method is the salt or saltpetre mixed with ice or snow. So what the Arab physicians recorded was the cooling power of saltpetre, and that's the first step, not the finished thing, right? And Jurafsky is one source on this part, a decent one, so take it as his account.

## 05 Italy Picks It Up

Then, in 1530, a professor at Padua named Zimara wrote the cooling trick up as a new discovery, a new discovery, about five hundred years after that physician in 1029.

By 1558, Giambattista della Porta was describing how to freeze wine to a slush in snow and saltpetre, in his book Magia Naturalis. And that's the actual freezing method, ice and saltpetre together, written down in Italy in the 1500s.

And then more than a century later, in the 1690s, Antonio Latini, who was steward to the Spanish viceroy in Naples, printed some of the first sorbetto recipes in a book called Lo scalco alla moderna, and that's some of the first, among the first published, not the first ever. And his recipes included chocolate, and even aubergine, which is eggplant.

Now, we don't know exactly how the idea got from the Arab physicians to the Italian professors. The chain makes sense, but parts of it are filled in by the historians rather than written down, so that link is a fair guess, not a fact.

## 06 Who Froze Cream First

Oh, one more thing. You'll sometimes hear that frozen milk dishes go back to China's Tang dynasty, and those claims are poorly sourced, so we're not going to tell you that. And the honest answer to who first froze a sweetened dairy mixture, actual ice cream in the way we'd think of it, is that nobody knows.

## 07 Salt and Ice

So the plain version is this. Marco Polo didn't bring ice cream back from China, Catherine de' Medici didn't carry it to France, and food historians find no evidence for either story. An Arab physician in the 13th century recorded that saltpetre chills water, crediting a physician from 1029, Zimara in Padua wrote it up in 1530, della Porta was freezing wine to slush in snow and saltpetre by 1558, and Latini printed some of the first sorbetto recipes in Naples in the 1690s. Somebody did it first, and it was a string of physicians and scholars, not Marco Polo.
