## 01 Cold Open

People have argued for decades over who invented television, John Logie Baird or Philo Farnsworth or Vladimir Zworykin, and it's been a long-running credit fight. But underneath all three of them there's a student in Berlin named Paul Nipkow, who patented the basic idea in 1884, more than forty years before anyone saw a TV picture. Now, to be fair, Nipkow patented an idea, he probably never built one, but the idea is the one that the first working television was built around.

## 02 The Credit Fight

So here's the famous version, and it's really three famous versions. John Logie Baird gets credit for the first working television pictures, in 1926, and Baird's 1926 demonstration is counted as the first working TV. Then Philo Farnsworth in 1927 and Vladimir Zworykin in 1928 get credit for electronic television, which is a different way of doing it, without the moving parts.

And that's the fight, right? Is the inventor the person who got the first working picture, or the people who got there electronically? And people have taken sides on that. But all three of those dates are in the 1920s, and the patent we're talking about is from 1884.

## 03 Christmas Eve 1883

Paul Nipkow was a student in Berlin, and according to the German Patent Office's own records, the idea came to him on Christmas Eve, 1883. The Early Television Museum gives the same date, so that's two sources on Christmas Eve.

And the idea was a spinning disk that broke a picture up into points. The basic idea is that you break a picture up into lots of little points, so you can send them one after another and build the picture back up at the other end, and Nipkow's way of doing the breaking up was a spinning scanning disk, and that's why it's called the Nipkow disk.

## 04 The Patent

He got German patent number 30105, for what he called an Elektrisches Teleskop, an electric telescope. It was effective from January 6th, 1884, and it was granted on January 15th, 1885, and it was the first patent for a TV-like system. And that's not only the German Patent Office talking about its own patent, Britannica puts Nipkow's patent in 1884 too, and the IEEE's engineering history site counts it as the first TV-like patent and lays it right next to Baird in 1926, Farnsworth in 1927 and Zworykin in 1928. So from 1884 to 1926, that's forty-two years between the patent and the first working television.

Oh, one more thing about that patent. According to the German Patent Office, it was Nipkow's girlfriend who paid the patent fee. That detail only comes from the Patent Office's page, so I'm giving it to you as theirs, but it's their own record of their own patent, and it's a nice thing to know about the first television patent.

## 05 Did He Ever Build It

Now here's the part that matters, and it's the honest part. Nipkow patented a concept, and he probably never built a working disk himself. The Early Television Museum's wording is that "one may assume" he did not, and nobody actually knows whether he ever built a prototype at all.

So I'm not going to tell you Nipkow had a working television in 1884, because there's no evidence for that. What he had was the patent, and the idea in that patent, and that turned out to be enough to put his name on the thing.

## 06 How Baird Used It

Because when John Logie Baird built his mechanical television, the machine IEEE Spectrum writes up as Baird's Televisor, he built it around Nipkow disks. So the first working TV, Baird's in 1926, ran on the same spinning-disk idea Nipkow had patented more than forty years earlier, right? Baird did the hard part of making it actually work, and he deserves that credit, but the disk at the heart of it already had Nipkow's name on it.

And according to the Early Television Museum, in 1935 there was a television station in Berlin, during the Nazi era, named Fernsehsender Paul Nipkow, the Paul Nipkow television station. That comes from one museum source, so take it as theirs, but if it's right, his name was still known in Berlin about fifty years after his patent was granted.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's the plain version. Baird gets the credit for the first working television, in 1926, and Farnsworth and Zworykin get credit for electronic television in 1927 and 1928, and all three of them earned it. But the first patent for a TV-like system went to a student in Berlin named Paul Nipkow, for a spinning disk that broke a picture into points, an idea that came to him on Christmas Eve, 1883. Somebody did it first, and in this case he did it on paper, more than forty years before anybody saw a picture.
