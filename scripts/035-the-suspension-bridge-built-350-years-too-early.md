## 01 Cold Open

A Tibetan builder was hanging iron-chain bridges across rivers in Tibet about three hundred and fifty years before the bridge that usually gets called the first modern chain suspension bridge went up in Pennsylvania. That Pennsylvania bridge was built by a man named James Finley in 1801, and he deserves real credit for it, and we'll give it to him. But he wasn't the first person to hang a bridge from iron chains, not by a long way.

## 02 The Famous Version

So first, what we're talking about, a suspension bridge is one where the part you walk or drive on hangs from chains or cables strung between supports, instead of sitting on top of arches or posts. And in the version you'll find in a lot of histories, the modern chain suspension bridge starts with James Finley, at Jacob's Creek in Pennsylvania, in 1801. He got a patent on it in 1808, and his bridge became a model for Telford's Menai bridge.

It's usually called the first modern suspension bridge, the Smithsonian's archives have a record of Finley's bridge at Jacob's Creek, and there's an old print in the Library of Congress with the title Chain Bridge invented by James Finley, invented, right there in the title. And that's how it stuck, there's a name, a place, a date and a patent, and that's usually what history remembers, right?

## 03 Thangtong Gyalpo

But in the 1400s, a man named Thangtong Gyalpo was building iron-chain suspension bridges in Tibet and in Bhutan, including bridges over the Tsangpo river. That's about three hundred and fifty years before Finley's bridge at Jacob's Creek. Britannica has an entry on him, and so does a biography collection called the Treasury of Lives, and both of them describe him as a fifteenth-century builder of iron-chain bridges.

Now, I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to overstate this. Thangtong's bridges were walkways held up by chains, so they're iron-chain suspension bridges, but they're not the same design as Finley's. So the fair way to say it is that the idea of hanging a crossing from iron chains was being used in Tibet about three and a half centuries before Pennsylvania, not that Finley copied it, and not that the two bridges were the same thing.

## 04 Fair Credit to Finley

And to be fair to Finley, the word modern is doing real work in his title. He built his bridge in 1801, he patented it in 1808, and his design became a model for Telford's Menai bridge. That's a real contribution, a working, patented design that a later bridge was modeled on.

So Finley gets credit for the modern version. What he doesn't get is credit for being the first person to hang a bridge from iron chains, because that was being done in Tibet in the 1400s, right?

## 05 The Bridges Older Than Both

And there's more, because bridges in general are a lot older than either of them. In Greece there's a bridge called the Arkadiko bridge, also known as the Kazarma bridge, and it dates to around 1300 BC, in the Mycenaean era. It's made of huge limestone blocks fitted together without any mortar or binding material holding them, and it sits on an old Mycenaean road that linked Mycenae, Tiryns and Epidaurus.

And Greece's culture ministry says the local people still use it, and you'll sometimes hear it called the oldest bridge in daily use, but that's too strong, oldest and daily haven't been proven, so the honest way to put it is that it's among the oldest bridges in the world still in use.

## 06 The Tacoma Narrows Myth

Oh, one more bridge myth while we're here, because this one is in a lot of physics classrooms. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington State opened on July 1st, 1940, and it collapsed on November 7th, 1940. And for generations, physics textbooks used it as the classic example of resonance, the same thing as a singer breaking a wine glass by hitting just the right note.

But that's not what happened. In 1991 two researchers, Billah and Scanlan, wrote in the American Journal of Physics that it was not a case of resonance, contrary to what the undergraduate textbooks said, and Washington State's own transportation department gives the main cause as something called torsional flutter, and torsional just means twisting. The wind and the twisting of the deck fed each other's motion, the twisting changed how the wind hit the deck, and the wind made the twisting worse, until the bridge tore itself apart. So for generations the textbook story put the wrong name on what brought that bridge down.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's how it lines up. James Finley built the bridge at Jacob's Creek in 1801, patented it in 1808, and gets fair credit for the modern chain suspension bridge. But Thangtong Gyalpo was building iron-chain suspension bridges in Tibet and Bhutan in the 1400s, about three hundred and fifty years earlier, and they weren't the same design, but they were bridges hanging from iron chains, so somebody did it first, a long time before Finley.
