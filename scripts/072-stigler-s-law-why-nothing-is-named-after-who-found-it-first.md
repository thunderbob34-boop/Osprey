## 01 Cold Open

There's a law that says nothing in science is named after the person who discovered it, and the law itself is named after the wrong guy. It's called Stigler's Law, after a statistician named Stephen Stigler, and the first person to say it wasn't really Stigler's idea was Stigler himself, he said the idea was really Robert Merton's. So a law that says discoveries are never named after their discoverer is named after the wrong person, and he did that on purpose. And since this whole channel is about the person who did it first and the person who ended up with the credit, this one is pretty much our mission statement.

## 02 The Law

So let's start with the law itself. Stephen Stigler is a statistician at the University of Chicago, and in 1980 he published a paper called Stigler's Law of Eponymy. Eponymy is just the word for naming something after a person, like the Pythagorean theorem or Halley's Comet, and the law says this, "no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer."

Now, to be fair, it's a joke law. It's a tongue-in-cheek line, it's not a statistical finding, and nobody went out and counted every discovery in history to prove it. But once you hear it you start seeing it everywhere, and that's pretty much what the rest of this video is.

And here's where it gets good. The paper came out in the Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences, and it was written for a festschrift, which is a book of papers that scholars put together to honor a colleague. And the colleague this one honored was the sociologist Robert K. Merton.

## 03 Merton Got There First

Robert Merton had already described this exact pattern. Back in 1957 he'd written about priority disputes, which is the formal name for scientists arguing over who found something first, so the idea that the credit doesn't always land on the person who got there first was already Merton's territory, years before anybody called it a law.

So when Stigler sat down to write his paper for a book honoring Merton, he openly credited Merton with the idea. He didn't hide it. He didn't hide it at all, he said it in the paper written for the man's own festschrift, and then the law went out into the world with Stigler's name on it anyway. And that's the joke, right? The law obeys itself. If it had been called Merton's Law it would've broken its own rule, so by putting his own name on it, Stigler made it come true the day it was published.

Now, to be fair to Stigler, he's the one who gave it the name and the one-line version everybody quotes, and he was straight with everybody about where it came from. Merton saw the pattern, Stigler named it, and the name went to Stigler, which is exactly what the law says is going to happen.

## 04 Hubble and Lemaître

Now let's try it on something big, the expanding universe. For a long time the idea that the universe is expanding went by the name Hubble's law, after the astronomer Hubble, but a Belgian priest named Georges Lemaître had published the expansion in 1927, two years before Hubble did.

And in 2018 the International Astronomical Union, which is the big international body for astronomers, put it to a vote, and 78 percent of 4,060 electronic votes came in favor of recommending a new name, the Hubble-Lemaître law. So Hubble keeps his name on it, and Lemaître finally gets his added.

Now, to be fair to Hubble, this is not a story where somebody stole something. The historian Helge Kragh called the background notes the astronomers put out with that vote "bad history," so the honest way to say it is the plain way, Lemaître published first, and that's all we're saying.

## 05 A Tablet Older Than Pythagoras

And there's more, and this one's older than any of them. You probably remember the Pythagorean theorem from school, right? For a right triangle, a squared plus b squared equals c squared, and a Pythagorean triple is just three whole numbers that make that work, like 3, 4 and 5, because 9 plus 16 is 25.

Well, there's a Babylonian clay tablet called Plimpton 322, from a place called Larsa, and it's dated to roughly 1822 to 1762 BCE, and it's at Columbia University now. And what it has on it is a table of Pythagorean triples, more than a thousand years before Pythagoras. Now, some researchers read it as early trigonometry, but that reading is debated, so the safe way to put it is the plain way, it's a table of Pythagorean triples, and it's more than a thousand years older than the man the theorem is named after.

And it keeps going. The Gaussian curve, the bell curve, traces back to de Moivre, and Halley's Comet turns up in Chinese records, so both of the examples I used at the start of this video, the Pythagorean theorem and Halley's Comet, are on the list too.

So the law is a joke, and it's named after the wrong person, and the man it's named after is the one who told us that. Stigler named it, Merton described it first, Lemaître published first on the expanding universe, and a clay tablet from Larsa listed Pythagorean triples more than a thousand years before Pythagoras.

And none of this is about tearing the famous names down, it's about the people whose names didn't stick, and the fact that even the law about it ended up with the wrong name on it. Somebody did it first, and that's what this channel is about.
