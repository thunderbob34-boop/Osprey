# The First Alcoholic Drink Was Chinese

- Runtime is about 5 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 824 spoken words. The research is thin and there's no single inventor here, so this one runs short on purpose.
- Tone is calm and curious. No jokes about drinking, just the dates and the chemistry.

## 01 Cold Open

The oldest alcoholic drink scientists have chemically confirmed isn't Egyptian beer or French wine, it's a brew from China that's about 9,000 years old. We hand beer to the Egyptians and the Sumerians, and we hand wine to France or Greece or Rome, but the oldest drink with chemical traces of alcohol that anyone has pinned down came out of a site in China, and it was made from rice, honey and fruit.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here's the version most people carry around. Beer is ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, the Egyptians and the Sumerians, and wine is the Mediterranean, France and Greece and Rome. And those cultures really are the ones we credit, that's the story most of us picked up somewhere along the way.

Now, to be fair, nobody's saying the Egyptians didn't brew beer or the French didn't make wine. The point is just that they weren't first, right? And there's no single inventor in this story, no one guy with a patent. It's places and dates, and the dates go back a lot further than most people think.

## 03 Jiahu, China

The place is called Jiahu, in China, and the date is around 7000 BC, roughly 7000 to 6600 BC, so about 9,000 years ago. A team of scientists including one named McGovern analyzed what was found there, and they published it in 2004 in PNAS, the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. What they found were chemical traces of a fermented drink made from rice, honey, and hawthorn fruit or grape. And fermenting, if you've never thought about it, is what happens when yeast gets at sugar and turns it into alcohol, and honey and fruit are full of sugar, so it's not a strange mix at all.

Now here's the part that matters. Nobody found a bottle of 9,000-year-old drink sitting there, what they found was residue, the chemistry left behind, and from that chemistry they can tell what went into it and that it was fermented. That's the standard way this kind of thing is done, so when you hear "chemically confirmed," that's what it means, chemical traces, right? It's chemistry, it's not a recipe card.

And the Penn Museum has a page on this research, and the title of it is The Earliest Alcoholic Beverage in the World. And on that research, China was making a fermented drink more than five hundred years before the oldest confirmed ones from the Near East. So that's the oldest chemically confirmed alcoholic drink we've got, and it's not beer and it's not wine, it's a Chinese brew of rice, honey and fruit.

## 04 The Oldest Wine Is From Georgia

And there's more, because wine has its own surprise. The oldest grape wine anyone has found comes from Georgia, the country, not the state, from two villages south of Tbilisi, and it dates to about 6000 to 5800 BC. That was published in 2017, again in PNAS, again with McGovern on the team.

The way they knew it was grape wine was tartaric acid, which is a chemical that points to grapes. And here's the thing, before Georgia, the record for the oldest grape wine was in Iran, at a site called Hajji Firuz, from about 5400 to 5000 BC. So the Georgian wine pushed the record back by something like 600 to 1,000 years. That's the oldest grape wine, and it's not French and it's not Greek and it's not Roman, it's from the South Caucasus.

## 05 The Beer Claim Nobody Agrees On

Oh, one more thing, and this one comes with a big warning label. One team argues that people called the Natufians, in Raqefet Cave in what's now Israel, were brewing a beer-like gruel about 13,000 years ago, which would be before farming. That team, with a researcher named Liu as the lead author, published it in 2018, and Stanford put out the news release on it. Their evidence is damage on starch grains, tiny bits of plant starch, that matches the damage you get when you malt grain in experiments, and malting is letting grain start to sprout and then drying it, which is one of the first steps in making beer.

But other archaeologists aren't convinced. A researcher named Eitam published a critique, and the team published a reply in 2019, so this is still being argued. So the honest way to say it is that the Natufians were possibly brewing something like beer 13,000 years ago, possibly, and if that holds up, they'd beat everyone in this video, but it hasn't settled, so it's not the record.

## 06 Who Was First

So the plain version is this. The oldest alcoholic drink with chemical traces anyone has confirmed comes from Jiahu in China, around 7000 BC, a brew of rice, honey, and hawthorn fruit or grape, and the oldest grape wine comes from Georgia, about 6000 to 5800 BC. Egypt gets the beer and France gets the wine, but somebody did it first, and on the evidence we've got, that somebody was in China about 9,000 years ago.
