# The Ice Cream Cone Wasn't Born at the 1904 World's Fair

## 01 Cold Open

The ice cream cone's origin story is a lucky moment at the 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis, but the recipe was in a London cookbook in 1888. Sixteen years before the fair, a British cookery star named Agnes Marshall, who got called the Queen of Ices, published a recipe for edible cornets that she said could be filled with ice cream. So the cone was not born at the fair, and the woman with the earliest claim to it is a name most people have never heard.

## 02 The Lucky Moment

The famous version is a good story, and it goes like this. At the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, a man named Ernest Hamwi was selling a kind of waffle called zalabia, and he rolled one of his waffles into a cone for the ice cream stand next to him, and that was that. That's how the story is often told, anyway.

And you can see why it stuck, right? It's got a big famous fair, a lucky moment, and two vendors helping each other out. But here's the thing, Hamwi isn't the only one. Several people claimed to have come up with the cone at that fair, History.com's write-up of the fair's foods says so too, and that tells you something right there, because if several people are claiming the same first, they can't all be right.

## 03 The Queen of Ices

Now here's the part that matters. In 1888, in London, Agnes Marshall published Mrs. A. B. Marshall's Cookery Book, and in it there's a recipe called Cornets with Cream. Cornets are horn-shaped cookies, so think of a cone, and she wrote that they could also be filled with any cream or water ice. And English Heritage calls her the Queen of Ices, so she wasn't some random cook, she was known for ices.

So that's an edible cone, filled with an ice, in a published London cookbook in 1888. Now, to be fair, what the recipe proves is that she said you could fill them with ice cream, it doesn't prove how people actually ate them in 1888, so I'll stick to what she wrote.

And the Smithsonian calls Marshall the earliest claimant. That's careful wording, and it's on purpose, because earliest claimant means she's the earliest one anybody has found, not proof that nobody did it before her.

## 04 The Man With the Mold

And there's more, because before the fair there was a patent too. In New York, a man named Italo Marchiony filed for a patent on September 22nd, 1903, and he was granted US Patent 746,971 in December 1903, for a mold that made edible ice cream cups. And you can look this one up yourself, the patent is on Google Patents under the plain title Mold, with Marchiony's name on it, and the Library of Congress lists patent 746,971 in its research guide on the history of ice cream, so this part isn't a story somebody told later, it's a government document from 1903.

Now, I want to be careful with this one, because his patent gets brought into the cone story, and his patent drawing shows a molded cup, not a rolled waffle cone. So the fair way to say it is that he patented an edible ice cream cup. And Marchiony said he'd been selling them since 1896, but that's his own claim, and I haven't seen anything that backs it up, so I'll leave it as what he said. But either way, a man in New York had a patent on an edible ice cream cup the year before the fair, and a cookbook in London had edible cornets you could fill with ice sixteen years before it.

## 05 Fair Credit to the Fair

Now, to be fair to the 1904 World's Fair, something real did happen there. The ice cream cone got popularized there, and that's real, and it's the reason the fair is in this story at all, right? It's just that "popularized there" and "invented there" are two different things, and the usual story mixes them up.

## 06 Who Did It First

So here's the plain version. The ice cream cone was popularized at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, where several people claimed it, and a man in New York named Italo Marchiony patented a mold for edible ice cream cups in December 1903. And in 1888, in London, Agnes Marshall, the Queen of Ices, published a recipe for Cornets with Cream, horn-shaped cookies she said could be filled with any cream or water ice. Somebody did it first, and on the evidence we have, the earliest recipe anybody's found that says to fill an edible cone with ice is hers.
