# Galileo Didn't Invent the Telescope

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 6 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 855 spoken words. The research is thin, so this one runs short rather than padded.
- Tone is calm and admiring of Galileo. He gets full credit for turning the telescope on the sky. The point is only that he didn't invent it.
- Say Lipperhey as LIP-er-high, Middelburg as MID-ul-burg, Zeeland as ZAY-lahnt, States General as "states general", Metius as MAY-tee-us, and Sacharias Janssen as sah-KAR-ee-us YAHN-sen.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "the first person on record", "three to four times", "weeks later" and "who really built the first one is murky" exactly as written. Never say Lipperhey definitely built the first telescope ever.
- Good on-screen text moments are 25 Sept 1608, 2 Oct 1608, "3 to 4x", 1609, and 1610.

## 01 Cold Open

A year before Galileo turned a telescope to the sky, a Dutch spectacle maker was asking his government for a patent on it. His name was Hans Lipperhey, he made eyeglasses in a town called Middelburg, and the Dutch government's own records have him applying for a patent on a telescope in the fall of 1608, and Galileo built his in 1609. So Galileo did something amazing with the telescope, and we'll get to that, but he didn't invent it, and the paperwork says so.

## 02 The Galileo Version

Here's the version a lot of people grew up with. Galileo Galilei invented the telescope and pointed it at the sky, and you can see why that sticks, because the second part is true, Galileo built improved telescopes in 1609, and in 1610 he used one to find the moons of Jupiter. That's a huge moment, and his name is attached to the telescope because of what he saw through it.

But if you ask NASA, it's a plain answer, NASA has a page that asks the question straight out, did Galileo invent the telescope, and the answer is no, right? And Rice University's Galileo Project, the American Institute of Physics, and Britannica all agree on who came before him.

## 03 Hans Lipperhey

The first person on record is Hans Lipperhey, a spectacle maker, a man who made eyeglasses, in Middelburg, in the Netherlands. And the reason we can say on record is that there are dated government documents.

The province of Zeeland wrote a letter to the States General, which was the Dutch national government, and that letter is dated September 25th, 1608. And then on October 2nd, 1608, the States General discussed Lipperhey's application for a patent on his device. So that's the government of the Netherlands, in writing, with dates on it, talking about Lipperhey's telescope, about a year before Galileo built his.

And a patent, just so we're clear, is the government saying you're the one who gets to make and sell this thing, and nobody else can, for a set number of years. That's what Lipperhey was asking for.

## 04 The Device and the Refused Patent

Lipperhey's device used two lenses, a convex one, which bulges outward, and a concave one, which curves inward. And it magnified things about three to four times. So it's not a powerful telescope by later standards, but it's a telescope, two lenses that make far-off things look closer.

Now here's the part that matters, the States General refused his patent, and the reason they gave was that the device couldn't be kept secret. But they didn't just send him away, they paid him to build binocular versions, which means versions for both eyes. So the Dutch government turned down the patent and then hired him to make more of them, right?

## 05 Who Really Built the First One

Now, to be fair, I want to be careful here. Lipperhey is the first person on record, but that's not the same as saying he definitely built the first telescope ever. A few weeks after Lipperhey, a man named Jacob Metius applied for a patent too. And later on, the family of a man named Sacharias Janssen claimed that he had made it first.

So who really built the first one is murky, and the records don't settle it. What the records can show is that Lipperhey's application was in front of the Dutch government on October 2nd, 1608, with the Zeeland letter a week before that, and those dated records are what put him first on record. That's why the fair way to say it is that Lipperhey is the first person on record, not the first person ever.

But either way you slice it, Lipperhey and Metius were both asking for patents in 1608, and Galileo built his in 1609. So whoever built the very first one, it wasn't Galileo.

## 06 Fair Credit to Galileo

Now, to be fair to Galileo, what he did with it really matters. He didn't invent the telescope, but in 1609 he built improved instruments, and then he did the thing that made the telescope famous, he turned it on the sky. In 1610 he found the moons of Jupiter, and that's real, and it's huge, and he deserves full credit for it.

So the fair way to say it is, Galileo didn't invent the telescope, he improved it, and he pointed it at the sky and found the moons of Jupiter. Lipperhey is the first on record, and Galileo is the one who turned it on the sky.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's the order. On September 25th, 1608, Zeeland wrote to the Dutch States General. On October 2nd, 1608, the States General discussed Hans Lipperhey's patent application for a device with a convex lens and a concave lens that magnified three to four times. A few weeks later, Jacob Metius applied too, and later Sacharias Janssen's family claimed it. The patent was refused because the device couldn't be kept secret, and Lipperhey was paid to build binocular versions. In 1609, Galileo built his improved telescopes, and in 1610 he found the moons of Jupiter. Galileo gets the credit for the sky. Somebody did it first, and the first one on record was a spectacle maker in Middelburg.
