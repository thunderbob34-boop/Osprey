# Lewis and Clark Were Twelve Years Late

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 4 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 606 spoken words.
- Tone is calm and respectful. Lewis and Clark did real work and the script says so, and the Indigenous peoples who knew the routes get named too.
- Say Mackenzie as muh-KEN-zee, Chipewyan as chip-uh-WY-un, Bella Coola as BELL-uh KOO-luh, Nuxalk as NOO-hawlk.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "first European", "north of Mexico", "about twelve years", "July 1793", "an inlet of the Pacific, not the open ocean" and "one of the things that shaped his thinking" exactly as written, because they are what keeps the channel credible.
- The twelve years is arithmetic, from Mackenzie reaching the coast in 1793 to Lewis and Clark reaching the Pacific in 1805. Counting from when Lewis and Clark set out in 1804 it is eleven, so don't say "twelve years before they left".
- Good on-screen text moments are 1792, July 1793, 1801, summer 1802, and November 1805.

## 01 Cold Open

Everybody learns that Lewis and Clark were the first to cross the continent to the Pacific, and they reached the mouth of the Columbia River in November 1805, but about twelve years before that, a Scottish fur trader named Alexander Mackenzie got to the Pacific coast and painted his name on a rock there. Now, we have to be exact about this, because what Mackenzie did was be the first European to cross North America north of Mexico, and that phrase matters, and we'll get to why. And there's more, because President Jefferson read Mackenzie's book in 1802, a few months before he asked Congress for money for the Lewis and Clark expedition.

## 02 The Famous Version

The famous version is Lewis and Clark, and it's a good story, a party reaching the Pacific at the mouth of the Columbia in November 1805. They were first for the United States, and they did major mapping and science along the way, and none of what I'm about to say takes that away. But the popular belief is that they were the first to cross the continent to the Pacific, and that needs some qualifiers.

## 03 Mackenzie

Alexander Mackenzie was a fur trader with the North West Company, and his party left Fort Chipewyan in 1792. In July 1793 he reached the Pacific coast at Dean Channel, near Bella Coola in British Columbia, and that made his party the first Europeans to cross North America north of Mexico. He painted his name on a rock there, and the inscription says, from Canada, by land.

Now, the exact day varies depending on the source, the rock itself says July 22nd and one extract gives July 20th, so we're just going to say July 1793. And one more careful thing, what he reached was an inlet of the Pacific, not the open ocean.

## 04 Who Else Was There

Here's where those qualifiers come in, and I want to be fair about them. Mackenzie didn't do it alone, he followed the Nuxalk-Carrier grease trail and he had Native guides, so Indigenous peoples knew the route long before he did. And the Spanish were already on the Pacific coast farther south, which is why the claim is first European, north of Mexico, and not first anybody.

Then in 1801 Mackenzie published his journals, in a book called Voyages from Montreal.

## 05 Jefferson Reads the Book

Now here's the part that matters. In the summer of 1802, President Jefferson read Mackenzie's book at Monticello, and Meriwether Lewis was working as his secretary at the time. A few months later Jefferson asked Congress for money for the expedition that became Lewis and Clark. Mackenzie's book was one of the things that shaped his thinking, it wasn't the only one, and I don't want to oversell it, but the Library of Congress and Monticello both cover Jefferson reading the book as part of how the expedition came about.

## 06 Fair Credit

So to give Lewis and Clark their due, they were first for the United States, they did major mapping and science, and nobody is saying they didn't earn their place in the story. It's just that the American story has a Scottish-Canadian prequel, and Jefferson had read it.

## 07 Who Did It First

So, the plain fact, in July 1793 Alexander Mackenzie reached the Pacific coast at Dean Channel and became the first European to cross North America north of Mexico, about twelve years before Lewis and Clark reached the mouth of the Columbia in November 1805. Mackenzie had Native guides and followed a route Indigenous peoples already knew, the Spanish were already farther south on the coast, and he reached an inlet, not the open ocean. Somebody did it first, and that's what this channel is about.
