# Lewis and Clark Were Twelve Years Late

## 01 Cold Open

Everybody learns that Lewis and Clark were the first to cross the continent to the Pacific, and they reached the mouth of the Columbia River in November 1805, but about twelve years before that, a Scottish fur trader named Alexander Mackenzie got to the Pacific coast and painted his name on a rock there. Now, we have to be exact about this, because what Mackenzie did was be the first European to cross North America north of Mexico, and that phrase matters, and we'll get to why. And there's more, because President Jefferson read Mackenzie's book in 1802, a few months before he asked Congress for money for the Lewis and Clark expedition.

## 02 The Famous Version

The famous version is Lewis and Clark, and it's a good story, a party reaching the Pacific at the mouth of the Columbia in November 1805. They were first for the United States, and they did major mapping and science along the way, and none of what I'm about to say takes that away. But the popular belief is that they were the first to cross the continent to the Pacific, and that needs some qualifiers.

## 03 The First Attempt

Alexander Mackenzie was a fur trader with the North West Company, and his first try didn't get him to the Pacific. In 1789 he made a trip that the Canadian Encyclopedia and Britannica both have ending at the Arctic Ocean, not the Pacific, and he was disappointed, so he tried again.

## 04 Mackenzie Reaches the Coast

His second attempt left Fort Chipewyan in 1792, and on May 9th, 1793, according to the Canadian Encyclopedia, he set out for the coast with a man named Alexander Mackay, six voyageurs and two Indigenous hunters. He went up the Peace River and then the Parsnip, and on the Fraser River local Carrier people advised him to finish the trip overland, and the Canadian Encyclopedia says he then borrowed canoes from the Nuxalk and followed the Bella Coola River to the sea.

In July 1793 he reached the Pacific coast at Dean Channel, near Bella Coola in British Columbia, and that made his party the first Europeans to cross North America north of Mexico. He painted his name on a rock there, and the words, as Parks Canada and the Canadian Encyclopedia give them, were, Alexander Mackenzie, from Canada by land, the twenty-second of July, one thousand seven hundred and ninety-three. The Canadian Encyclopedia says he wrote it in vermilion and grease, and that surveyors later inscribed the words.

Now, the rock itself says the twenty-second, but not every source agrees on the exact day, so we're just going to say July 1793. And one more careful thing, what he reached was an inlet of the Pacific, not the open ocean.

## 05 Who Else Was There

Here's where those qualifiers come in, and I want to be fair about them. Mackenzie didn't do it alone, he followed the Nuxalk-Carrier grease trail and he had Native guides, so Indigenous peoples knew the route long before he did. And the Spanish were already on the Pacific coast farther south, which is part of why the claim is first European, north of Mexico, and not first anybody.

Then in 1801 Mackenzie published his journals, in a book called Voyages from Montreal, and in 1802 he was knighted.

## 06 Jefferson Reads the Book

Now here's the part that matters. In the summer of 1802, President Jefferson read Mackenzie's book at Monticello, and Meriwether Lewis was working as his secretary at the time. A few months later, in a confidential message to Congress on January 18th, 1803, Jefferson asked for $2,500 to explore the West, and the archives and Monticello both have that message. In it he said an officer with ten or twelve chosen men might explore the whole line, even to the Western Ocean, and the National Archives and the Library of Congress both quote him on that.

Mackenzie's book was one of the things that shaped Jefferson's thinking, it wasn't the only one, and I don't want to oversell it, but the Library of Congress and Monticello both cover Jefferson reading the book as part of how the expedition came about. And Lewis, who was Jefferson's secretary, knew about that confidential message.

## 07 Fair Credit

So to give Lewis and Clark their due, they were first for the United States, they did major mapping and science, and nobody is saying they didn't earn their place in the story. It's just that Mackenzie got to the Pacific coast first, and Jefferson read his book before the expedition was commissioned.

## 08 Who Did It First

So, the plain fact, in July 1793 Alexander Mackenzie reached the Pacific coast at Dean Channel and became the first European to cross North America north of Mexico, about twelve years before Lewis and Clark reached the mouth of the Columbia in November 1805. Mackenzie had Native guides and followed a route Indigenous peoples already knew, the Spanish were already farther south on the coast, and he reached an inlet, not the open ocean. Somebody did it first, and that's what this channel is about.
