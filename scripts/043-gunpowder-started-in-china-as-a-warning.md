## 01 Cold Open

Gunpowder was probably stumbled on by Chinese alchemists who were experimenting with elixir ingredients, and a Daoist text from the middle of the 800s warns that mixing some of those ingredients had burned people's faces and burned houses down. So one of the earliest things we have written down about this mix isn't a recipe, it's a warning. And the oldest written recipe for gunpowder that we know of, anywhere, is Chinese too.

## 02 The Picture in Our Heads

When most of us picture gunpowder, we picture cannons and muskets, smoke over a battlefield, a lot of very European-looking scenes, because that's the part of the story most of us know. And that picture isn't wrong about what gunpowder ended up doing, it's just missing the beginning.

So let's start with what gunpowder actually is. It's a mix of three things, saltpeter, sulfur and charcoal, and the classic mix ended up at roughly seventy-five parts saltpeter, fifteen charcoal and ten sulfur. The saltpeter is the important one, because it feeds the fire by itself, so the whole thing burns really, really fast instead of just smoldering. And the question for this video is, who put those together first?

## 03 Chinese Alchemists

Gunpowder was almost certainly discovered in China, by alchemists, probably in the 9th century, under the late Tang dynasty, and History.com puts it at around the year 850. Now, the reason an alchemist would have saltpeter and sulfur sitting around in the first place is that those two were staples in Chinese elixir labs, so alchemists were mixing all kinds of substances trying to make elixirs, and saltpeter and sulfur were right there on the shelf, right?

And here's the part that matters, there's a Daoist text from the middle of the 800s that warns people about this exact thing, and it says mixing saltpeter and sulfur has burned people's faces and burned down houses. So this text isn't a discovery story, nobody in it is shouting eureka, it's a warning, don't do this, it's dangerous. But you only write that warning if people have already been doing it and getting hurt, right?

## 04 The First Recipe

And then there's the first actual recipe. In 1044, under the Song dynasty, a military manual called the Wujing Zongyao wrote down a formula for gunpowder, and that's the earliest known written formula for gunpowder anywhere in the world. So by 1044 this wasn't just a warning in a Daoist text anymore, it was in a military manual, written down so people could make it on purpose.

But the early uses weren't all about weapons, because Britannica notes that gunpowder's early uses included fireworks and signals, so early on, gunpowder was also something you set off to make noise and light and send a signal, not only something you pointed at people.

## 05 The Immortality Story

Now, there's one more myth in here, and it's the one you'll hear the most. The usual story says Chinese alchemists discovered gunpowder while searching for an elixir of immortality, a potion to let you live forever, and History.com tells it that way, alchemists seeking an elixir of life.

Now, to be fair, that story is plausible, and it's the general consensus about the setting. These were alchemists, they were making elixirs, and saltpeter and sulfur were sitting right there in those labs. But no text actually records anybody looking for immortality and finding gunpowder instead, there's no moment like that on paper, and what we have is the warning from the 800s and then the recipe in 1044. So the honest way to say it is that they found it while they were experimenting with elixir ingredients, and we don't know the moment, and we don't know who.

## 06 Who Was First

So here's where that leaves us. Gunpowder was almost certainly discovered by Chinese alchemists, probably in the 9th century under the late Tang, while they were experimenting with elixir ingredients like saltpeter and sulfur. A Daoist text from the middle of the 800s was already warning that the mix burned faces and houses, and the earliest known written recipe for gunpowder comes from a Song military manual, the Wujing Zongyao, in 1044. Somebody did it first, and we don't know their names, but we do know they were working in China.
