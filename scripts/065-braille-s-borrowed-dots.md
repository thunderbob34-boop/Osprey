# Braille's Borrowed Dots

## 01 Cold Open

Braille wasn't the first raised-dot alphabet, a teenager took someone else's dot code and cut it in half, and that teenager was Louis Braille, and he was only fifteen. Now, that's not a knock on Braille, what he did was brilliant, but the dots weren't his idea first, they came from a man named Charles Barbier. And here's the strange part, the famous story about Barbier, the one you'll find in a lot of places, may not be true either.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here's the version most people know. Louis Braille created the six-dot code that blind people read with their fingertips, he worked it out in 1824 when he was fifteen, and he published it in 1829, and all of that is true, his name is on the system, and it's on it for a good reason.

But Braille didn't start from nothing, because before his six dots, there was a system with twelve.

## 03 Barbier's Twelve Dots

Charles Barbier had a raised-dot system called night writing, and it also went by the name sonography. It was a code made of raised dots that you read by touch, and each character was built on a twelve-dot cell. A cell, if you've never seen braille up close, is just the little block of positions where the dots for one character can go.

Barbier's system reached Braille's school, the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris, around 1821. That's the usual date, and nobody has pinned it down more exactly than that. So a raised-dot code that blind students could read with their fingers was already at that school, years before Braille's own system.

## 04 Cutting It in Half

Now here's the part that matters. Braille took Barbier's twelve-dot cell and cut it in half, down to six dots, and the reason that matters is your fingertip. With six dots, one fingertip could read a whole cell at once. So instead of feeling your way around a big block of dots for every single character, you touch it and you've got it, right?

And he did that at fifteen. The American Foundation for the Blind puts his work on the code between about thirteen and sixteen years old, and the usual date for the six-dot system is 1824, when he was fifteen. So it's a teenager at a school for the blind, looking at a code somebody else made, and seeing how to make it actually work under one finger. That's the real achievement, and nobody should take that away from him.

And this part of the story isn't controversial at all. History.com, the American Foundation for the Blind, Perkins School for the Blind and the Encyclopaedia Britannica all line up on it, Barbier's twelve-dot system came first, and Braille, at fifteen, brought it down to six, every one of them agrees on that. Where they don't agree is on the story of how Barbier's dots got there, and that's where it gets interesting.

## 05 The Myth About the Myth

And there's more, because the story people usually tell about Barbier has its own problem. You'll read in a lot of places that Barbier invented night writing for soldiers, so they could read messages in the dark, and that he came to the school and showed it to young Louis Braille himself.

But archivists now say that's probably not how it happened. Perkins School for the Blind, citing primary-source research by a researcher named Campsie, says Barbier did not design his system for soldiers to read in the dark, and the military night-writing story isn't backed by primary sources. And the famous meeting, Barbier showing his code to the young Braille in the 1820s, may also be a myth, because according to Perkins, in 1833 Barbier wrote that he didn't know who Braille was.

Now, to be fair, this is mostly coming from Perkins and Campsie, and other big sources, like History.com and the American Foundation for the Blind, still tell the soldier version. So the honest way to put it is that the soldier story is widely told, and the archivists who went back to the primary sources say it doesn't hold up. And it's a funny thing, right? A story about who did it first turns out to have a shaky story attached to the guy who did it first.

## 06 Twelve Dots, Then Six

So the plain version is this. Charles Barbier had a raised-dot code built on a twelve-dot cell, read by touch, and it reached the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris around 1821. Louis Braille, at fifteen, cut that cell in half to six dots so one fingertip could read a whole cell, and that's the system we still call braille. Somebody did it first, and Braille made it work.
