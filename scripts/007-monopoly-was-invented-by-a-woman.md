# Monopoly Was Invented by a Woman

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 6 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 931 spoken words.
- Tone is calm and matter-of-fact. Let the irony sit on its own, no sarcasm about Darrow or Parker Brothers.
- Say Magie as MAG-ee (check before recording if you can find a reliable recording of the name), Darrow as DARE-oh.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "sold it as his own", "a folk version", "stole is NPR's word" and "about thirty years" exactly as written. Never say Darrow copied her board or stole it in our own voice, the game changed hands for decades before he got it. Leave out how much Parker Brothers paid Magie, that number wasn't checked.
- Good on-screen text moments are 1904, US patent 748,626, The Landlord's Game, 1924, 1935, and "Go to Jail".

## 01 Cold Open

Monopoly, the game about getting rich off property, started as a woman's warning about getting rich off property. For a long time the official story was that a man named Charles Darrow invented it and sold it to Parker Brothers in 1935, but about thirty years before that, a woman named Lizzie Magie patented a game with a square track and a Go to Jail corner, and she designed it to show people what's wrong with land monopoly. Now, to be fair, the path from her game to Darrow's is longer and messier than "he stole it," and we'll walk through it honestly, but she was first, and the game she made was meant to say the opposite of what Monopoly says.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here's the version that was told for a long time. Charles Darrow sold Monopoly to Parker Brothers in 1935, and he was promoted as its inventor, and not just its inventor, its sole inventor, the one guy who came up with the whole thing. And that's a great story for a game company, right? One man with one idea, and it's the most famous board game in the world.

And I'll say this now, the 1935 sale is real. Darrow did sell the game to Parker Brothers in 1935. The question is whether he invented it, and he didn't.

## 03 Lizzie Magie, 1904

In 1904, a woman named Lizzie Magie, Elizabeth Magie, patented a game called The Landlord's Game. It's US patent number 748,626, and a patent is primary evidence, it's a government record with her name and a date on it, so this part isn't a legend, it's on file.

And if you saw her board you'd recognise it, it had a square track running around the edge, and it had a Go to Jail corner. And then in 1924 she revised the patent, so she was still working on this game twenty years after she first patented it.

## 04 What It Was For

Now here's the part that matters, and it's the part that turns this from a trivia fact into a really strange story. Lizzie Magie didn't design this game to celebrate getting rich off property. She designed it to teach what were called Georgist ideas, and in plain terms that means ideas about the harm of land monopoly.

And land monopoly is pretty much what it sounds like, it's when a small number of people own the land, and everybody else has to pay them to live on it or use it. So her game was built to show people how that works and why it's a problem. One Smithsonian headline put it this way, Monopoly was designed to teach the 99 percent about income inequality.

So think about that for a second, the world's most famous game about buying up property and bankrupting everybody else started life as a game about why that's bad, right?

## 05 How It Got to Darrow

Now, to be fair, this is where the story gets messier than a lot of people tell it. It's not as simple as Darrow sitting down with Lizzie Magie's patent and copying her board. Her game changed over about thirty years of homemade versions, people making their own boards and playing their own rules, including Quakers in Atlantic City, and others.

And that's how it reached Darrow. Darrow got the game through a Quaker friend's folk version, and a folk version just means a homemade one, passed around from person to person, not something you'd buy in a store. And then he sold it as his own.

So the honest way to say it is that Darrow sold a folk descendant of her game as his own. You'll see headlines that say he stole it from a woman, and one NPR headline put it exactly that way, but stole is NPR's word, and the fair version is that he sold something as his own invention that came from her game, by way of a lot of other people's homemade boards.

## 06 Fair Credit

Now, to be fair to Charles Darrow, the version he sold is the version that went to Parker Brothers in 1935, and that's the version the world knows. He didn't invent it, but he's the one who sold it, and that's real.

And the people in between deserve a mention too, the Quakers in Atlantic City and everybody else who made their own boards and changed the game over those thirty years. Lizzie Magie made the first one, and then a lot of people nobody remembers kept it alive and kept changing it, and by the time it got to Darrow it had been through a lot of hands.

So nobody's saying Monopoly as you know it came out of Lizzie Magie's head exactly as it is in the box today. What the evidence says is that she patented the game it came from, in 1904, and she was first.

## 07 Who Was First

So here's where it lands. Charles Darrow sold Monopoly to Parker Brothers in 1935 and was long promoted as its sole inventor. But in 1904, Lizzie Magie patented The Landlord's Game, US patent 748,626, with a square track and a Go to Jail corner, and she revised it in 1924. She designed it to teach the harm of land monopoly, and after about thirty years of homemade versions, Darrow got a folk version of it through a Quaker friend and sold it as his own. Somebody did it first, and it was Lizzie Magie, with a game that was meant to warn you about the very thing Monopoly lets you do.
