## 01 Cold Open

Thirteen years before the iPhone, you could buy a touchscreen phone with apps on it, and it wasn't made by Apple, it was made by IBM. It was called the Simon, it had a touchscreen and email and a calendar and an address book, and it's widely considered the first smartphone. The iPhone is the one everybody remembers as the first smartphone, and it deserves to be remembered, but there was a phone with a touchscreen and apps on sale first, and it's sitting in three museums right now.

## 02 The Famous Version

So the version most people carry around in their heads goes like this. In 2007 Apple put out the iPhone, and it's popularly called the first smartphone, the first phone that was really a little computer you could carry in your pocket and touch with your finger.

And it makes sense that people think that, right? For a lot of us the iPhone was the first phone like that we ever saw, and it's the one that caught on. But the Simon was on sale more than a decade earlier, and it did a surprising amount of what we'd now call smartphone stuff.

## 03 The IBM Simon

The full name was the Simon Personal Communicator, and it came from IBM working with BellSouth. They first showed it in late 1992 at COMDEX, which was a big computer trade show, and it went on sale on August 16th, 1994, so the idea was out in public for well over a year before anybody could actually buy one.

And here's what it could do. It was a cellphone, and it had a touchscreen, and it had built-in apps, an address book, a calendar, mail, fax, a notepad, and a sketch pad. So you could make a call, check your calendar, send a message, and send a fax, all from the same thing in your hand, in 1994. That's the part that surprises people, right? The basic idea of a phone that's also a set of apps on a touchscreen was already for sale thirteen years before the iPhone.

And it's not a story that lives on some forum somewhere. The Simon is in the collection at the Computer History Museum, it's in the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, and it's in the Science Museum Group in Britain. Three museums have one, and that's a good sign the people who study this stuff take it seriously.

## 04 Why You Never Had One

Now here's why you've probably never heard of it. According to TIME's look back at the Simon, it cost about $900 with a two-year contract and $1,100 without one, the battery lasted about an hour, and it was off the market within about six months, after roughly 50,000 sales.

An hour of battery. An hour of battery on a phone that was also your calendar and your address book and your fax machine. And at about $900 on a contract, in 1994, that's a lot of money for something that needed charging that often, and whatever the reasons were, it didn't last on the shelves. Now, to be fair, those numbers come from TIME's retrospective, and nobody knows the exact sales, so roughly 50,000 is the honest way to say it.

## 05 What "First" Means Here

Now, there's a catch, and it matters. Nobody called the Simon a smartphone at the time, that word wasn't being used for it, and there were earlier handheld organizers, the kind people called PDAs, that already had touchscreens. So whether the Simon counts as the first smartphone depends on how you define a smartphone, and that's why the careful way to say it is widely considered the first smartphone, not the first, full stop. And that's actually the interesting part, because the pieces were already out there, cellphones on one side and touchscreen organizers on the other, and the Simon put them together in one device you could buy, and that's what it gets the credit for.

But if your definition is a phone with a touchscreen and built-in apps that a regular person could buy, the Simon was there in 1994. And to be fair to Apple, the iPhone is the one that caught on. The Simon was early, and it didn't sell, and the iPhone is the one people remember.

## 06 Thirteen Years Early

So the plain version goes like this. The iPhone came out in 2007 and gets called the first smartphone, and it's the one that caught on. But on August 16th, 1994, you could buy an IBM Simon, a cellphone with a touchscreen, an address book, a calendar, mail, fax, a notepad and a sketch pad, for about $900 on a contract, with a battery that lasted about an hour, and within about six months it was gone.

Somebody did it first, and in this case it was IBM, thirteen years before the iPhone.
