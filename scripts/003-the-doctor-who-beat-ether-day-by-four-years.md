## 01 Cold Open

Every year America marks National Doctors' Day on March 30th, and most people have no idea why it's that date, but the reason you'll usually see given is a country doctor in Georgia named Crawford Long, who on March 30th, 1842, put a man under ether and took a tumor off his neck. And that matters for this channel, because the famous moment in the history of anesthesia, the one in the textbooks, is a public demonstration in Boston in 1846, and Crawford Long beat it by four years. Now, to be fair, there's a real reason the Boston demonstration is the one people remember, and we'll give it its due, but Crawford Long got there first, and even he wasn't the first one.

## 02 Before Anesthesia

First you have to understand what surgery was like before any of this, and I don't mean the gory part, I mean what doctors actually believed. Before anesthesia, many doctors didn't think pain was purely a bad thing, some saw it as a sign the body was still fighting, and some opposed anesthesia because they thought taking away the pain could be dangerous.

And that wasn't just a few cranks, the great French physiologist François Magendie argued that pain, in his words, "has always its usefulness." A peer-reviewed medical review calls it an "erroneous yet pervasive Western view," the idea that the patient's anguish was part of what kept them alive. So when somebody came along and said, I can make this not hurt, that wasn't automatically welcome news, right? There were serious people who thought the pain was doing something.

## 03 Ether Day

So here's the famous version, on October 16th, 1846, in Boston, a man named William Morton gave a public demonstration of ether, and that's the moment everybody remembers. It's remembered as Ether Day, and the place everybody points to is the Ether Dome, and if you've ever read a short history of anesthesia, that's usually where the story starts.

And to be fair to Morton, a public demonstration is a really big deal. It means people saw it with their own eyes, in a room, on a date, and that's the kind of thing that gets written down and talked about and copied. So Morton gets the credit, and the big reason, the way I see it, is that he did it in front of people.

## 04 Crawford Long, 1842

But four years earlier, in Georgia, a country doctor named Crawford Long had already done it. On March 30th, 1842, Long removed a tumor from the neck of a man named James Venable, and Venable was under ether while he did it. So that's surgery under ether, on a named patient, on a known date, in 1842, and Long documented it, this isn't a story somebody made up about him later.

Now here's the part that matters. Long didn't publish it until 1849. He did it in 1842, and he didn't publish until 1849, which is three years after Morton's demonstration in Boston. And that's the whole problem, right? If you don't publish, nobody else can build on what you did, and nobody else knows it happened, so it's no surprise the name people remember is Morton's.

So the way I'd put it is this, Morton made it public, and Long did it first. Both of those are true, and they're not the same thing, and this whole channel is about the gap between those two things.

## 05 Why It's March 30th

And that brings us back to Doctors' Day. Today in the United States, National Doctors' Day falls on March 30th, and the reason usually given is Long's operation. So if that's right, Crawford Long did get something back, because the day America thanks its doctors lines up with the date of his operation, and not with October 16th, the day of the famous demonstration in Boston. Either way, it's the same date, and most people who see a Doctors' Day post every March have never heard of Crawford Long.

## 06 Hanaoka, 1804

And there's more, because Crawford Long wasn't the first either. Thirty-eight years before Long, and forty-two years before Morton, there was a Japanese surgeon named Hanaoka Seishu. On October 13th, 1804, Hanaoka removed a breast tumor from a patient named Kan Aiya, and the patient was under full general anesthesia while he did it.

And full general anesthesia means exactly what it sounds like, the patient isn't just numbed in one spot, the patient is completely out for the operation. And he didn't use ether, he used an herbal mix, a preparation called mafutsusan, which you'll also see called tsusensan, so this is a completely different road to the same place, and it's full general anesthesia, on a named patient, on a known date, in 1804, in Japan, decades before anybody in Boston or Georgia.

So now you've got three dates, right? 1804 in Japan, 1842 in Georgia, and 1846 in Boston, and the one everybody knows is the last one.

## 07 Who Was First

So here's where it lands. William Morton's public demonstration on October 16th, 1846 is the one in the textbooks, and it earned that spot by being public. But on March 30th, 1842, Crawford Long had already removed a tumor from James Venable's neck under ether, and he just didn't publish until 1849. And on October 13th, 1804, Hanaoka Seishu had already operated on Kan Aiya under full general anesthesia with an herbal mix, forty-two years before Boston. Somebody did it first, and in this story it was a surgeon in Japan, and then a country doctor in Georgia, and the next time you see Doctors' Day on March 30th, that's the same date as Crawford Long's operation.
