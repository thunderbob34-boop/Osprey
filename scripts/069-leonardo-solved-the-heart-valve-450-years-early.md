# Leonardo Solved the Heart Valve 450 Years Early

## 01 Cold Open

Around 1513, Leonardo da Vinci worked out a glass model of the aorta, with grass seeds in the water, to show how a heart valve closes. Heart researchers are usually credited with working that out in the late 1960s, and they did, with modern science, but Leonardo had already watched it happen about 450 years earlier, with glass and water and grass seeds.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here's the version you'd find in the medical history. The way the aortic valve closes, the mechanism behind it, is usually credited to two researchers, both named Bellhouse, who published it in the science journal Nature in 1968, and to later work that used imaging to watch the flow. And that's real science, and it's fair that they get credit for it.

But Leonardo da Vinci saw the same thing about 450 years before them, and he did it with a model he built himself, so this one is a lot older than the textbook date.

## 03 What the Valve Does

If you've never thought about it, here's the plain version of what we're talking about. The aorta is the big artery that carries blood out of your heart, and the aortic valve sits right at the start of it. The heart pushes, the valve opens and the blood goes out, and then the valve has to snap shut so the blood doesn't come back in, right? And it does that every single heartbeat.

The question is how it closes so well, and part of the answer is swirls in the blood, little eddies that form near the valve and help push it shut, and that's what Leonardo saw.

## 04 The Glass Model

Now here's the part that matters. Leonardo injected wax into an ox heart, and he built a glass model of the root of the aorta, the part right where the valve sits. Then he pumped water through it with grass seeds in the water, so he could see how the water moved. And what he saw were swirling eddies, little whirlpools, that help the aortic valve snap shut.

His drawing of it is still around, it's in the Royal Collection in Britain, catalogued as RCIN 919082, and it's dated to about 1512 to 1513. So this isn't a legend, you can look at the page.

And the grass seeds are the clever part. Water on its own is clear, right, you can't see where it's going, but if you put something small floating in it, you can watch the seeds and see the flow, the swirls. That's the same basic idea people use today when they want to see how a fluid moves, and Leonardo was doing it with grass seeds.

## 05 450 Years Later

Then, in 1968 and 1969, Bellhouse confirmed the mechanism, and that's about 450 years after Leonardo's model. The American Heart Association's research journal, Circulation Research, has a review of Leonardo's heart work that makes that same point, and so does a paper in the medical literature on Leonardo and the sinuses of Valsalva, which are the little pockets at the base of the aorta right behind the valve.

And there's more. In 2002, a team led by a professor named Gharib at Caltech rebuilt Leonardo's model and published it in a journal called Experiments in Fluids. So modern engineers took his idea seriously enough to build it again, nearly five hundred years later.

And his aortic work has fed into the design of prosthetic heart valves and surgery on the root of the aorta, where the role of those sinuses of Valsalva is recognized. So this isn't just a nice old drawing, it's work that still matters to heart surgery.

## 06 Not the Plumbing

Now, to be fair, you might run into a bigger version of this story online, that Leonardo's valve work gave us the modern check valve, the one-way valve you find in plumbing, and the research says that's wrong. His aortic work has influenced heart valve design, and it has nothing to do with plumbing valves. So keep it about the heart, because the heart part is true, and it's impressive enough on its own.

## 07 The Seeds in the Glass

So the plain version is this. Around 1513, Leonardo da Vinci built a glass model of the aorta, pumped water and grass seeds through it, and watched the swirling eddies that help the aortic valve snap shut. Heart researchers published the same finding in 1968, about 450 years later, and they deserve the credit for proving it with modern science. Somebody did it first, and it was Leonardo, with a glass model and grass seeds.
