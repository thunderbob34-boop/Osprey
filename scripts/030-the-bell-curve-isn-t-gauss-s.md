## 01 Cold Open

The bell curve is on every report card and every IQ chart, and it's named after the wrong man. Statisticians call it the Gaussian distribution, after Carl Friedrich Gauss, and Gauss did something important with it, and we'll give him his credit. But the first bell curve showed up in a seven-page pamphlet that a mathematician named Abraham de Moivre handed around to friends in 1733, and that's seventy-six years before Gauss wrote about it.

## 02 The Famous Version

Here's the version most people pick up somewhere along the way. The bell curve, the normal distribution, the Gaussian distribution, those are all the same thing, and it's named after Gauss, from his work in 1809.

And there's a real reason his name stuck. Gauss linked the curve to measurement error. If you measure the same thing over and over, you don't get exactly the same number every time, you get a spread, and Gauss tied that spread to the bell curve, and to a method called least squares, which is a way of getting the best answer out of a pile of measurements that don't quite agree. That's useful in a very, very practical way, so it makes sense that the curve ended up with his name on it.

But being the one who made it useful isn't the same as being the one who found it first, right? And the curve itself had been around for decades before 1809.

## 03 De Moivre's Pamphlet

His name was Abraham de Moivre, and he was a French-born mathematician working in London. And on November 12th, 1733, he privately circulated a pamphlet, written in Latin, seven pages long, and in it he worked out the normal curve.

And here's how he got there, it came out of coin flips. If you flip a coin a lot of times and count the heads, most of the time you land somewhere near half, and the further you get from half, the less likely it is, and if you draw out all those chances, you get a bump in the middle that falls off on both sides. De Moivre worked out the bell curve as an approximation to those coin-flip odds. And then a few years later he expanded on it in the second edition of his book The Doctrine of Chances, in 1738.

So that's 1733, and Gauss is 1809. Seventy-six years. Seventy-six years before Gauss, the bell curve was already sitting in a little Latin pamphlet in London.

## 04 The Fine Print

Now, to be fair, there are a couple of things de Moivre didn't do. He treated the curve as an approximation, a shortcut for working out odds, not as a law of errors the way Gauss later did. That's the big difference, and it's the reason Gauss gets talked about the way he does.

And there's more, de Moivre's version was missing a piece. The formula for the bell curve has a constant in it, the square root of two pi, and de Moivre didn't have it. That came from another mathematician, James Stirling.

And Gauss wasn't even the next one to pick it up. The mathematician Laplace developed the curve further, starting in 1774, and again in 1812. So the line goes de Moivre in 1733, Laplace from 1774, and Gauss in 1809, and it's the last of those three names that ended up on it.

## 05 The Coffee House

Oh, one more thing about de Moivre, and this one comes with a warning label. The story goes that he was the resident mathematician at Slaughter's Coffee House. That comes from one source, the MacTutor history of mathematics site at the University of St Andrews, so treat it as the story goes, not as a fact we'd swear to.

What isn't a story is how long people have known about the de Moivre part. Back in 1933, the journal Nature ran a piece on de Moivre and the origin of the normal curve, pointing out that de Moivre came before both Laplace and Gauss. So this isn't some new discovery, it's been on the record for almost a hundred years, and the name still hasn't changed.

## 06 Who Did It First

Now, Gauss still matters here. He's the one who tied the curve to measurement error and least squares, and that's why his name stuck. But one statistics reference, on ScienceDirect, puts it about as plainly as you can, it says the credit to Gauss came about through a historical error.

So here's the plain version. On November 12th, 1733, Abraham de Moivre privately circulated a seven-page Latin pamphlet that worked out the bell curve as an approximation to coin-flip odds, and he expanded it in The Doctrine of Chances in 1738. James Stirling supplied the square root of two pi. Laplace developed it from 1774, and Gauss linked it to measurement error in 1809. Somebody did it first, and his name was de Moivre.
