# There's No Evidence for Kaldi's Goats

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 7 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 1,037 spoken words. The research is solid but narrow, so this one runs short rather than padded.
- Tone is calm and a little amused. Nobody's a villain here, the legend just got retold until the details changed.
- Say Kaldi as KAL-dee, Antoine Faustus Nairon as an-TWAHN FAW-stus nay-RON, Maronite as MARE-oh-nite, Arabia Felix as uh-RAY-bee-uh FEE-licks, Ayaman as AY-uh-man, Sufi as SOO-fee, Ukers as YOO-kers, Fredholm as FRED-holm.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "no evidence", "the earliest known", "roughly 800 years", "appears to occur first", "as far as that history can tell", "seems to have been made popular", "nobody has shown that" and "as far as the evidence goes" exactly as written.
- Don't ad-lib that Ukers invented the name Kaldi. Nobody has shown who first attached it. And leave out any named Sufi figures or the 1500s Arabic account of coffee's beginnings, those weren't checked.
- Visuals: the 1671 title page (a scan is on Wikimedia Commons) is the hero image. Period engravings of coffee and old maps labelled Arabia Felix work well.
- Good on-screen text moments are 1671, the Latin title, "the most wholesome drink", "or, as others say, of goats", "Ayaman" and "Arabia Felix", 1922, 2010, and the 1400s.

## 01 Cold Open

Walk into almost any coffee shop and sooner or later somebody will tell you about Kaldi, the goatherd in Ethiopia whose goats got the jitters from eating coffee cherries, and that's supposedly how the world found coffee. It's a good story, and there's no evidence for it. The earliest known version of it was written down in Rome, roughly 800 years after it was supposed to have happened, and in that version the herder doesn't have a name, he doesn't live in Ethiopia, and he might not have been herding goats at all, he might have been herding camels.

## 02 The Story Everyone Tells

So here's the version most people know. There's a goatherd named Kaldi out in Ethiopia, his goats eat coffee cherries and get the jitters, and that's the beginning of coffee. You'll hear it in coffee shops, you'll hear it from people who really, really love coffee, and it gets told like that's just how it happened.

And it's easy to see why it stuck, right? It's got animals, it's got a lucky accident, and it's got one guy who gets the credit, which is exactly the kind of story people like passing along. Now, to be fair to the story, one piece of it isn't wrong, because the coffee plant really is native to Ethiopia. But the plant and the drink are two different things, and the drink is the thing the story is supposed to explain, and that's where it starts to come apart.

## 03 A Book From 1671

Now here's the part that matters. If you go looking for where the Kaldi story comes from, the trail runs back to one book, and it's not from Ethiopia, and it's not from anywhere near the time it's supposed to describe. It's a Latin treatise from 1671, written in Rome by a Maronite scholar named Antoine Faustus Nairon, and the title calls coffee "the most wholesome drink." So the oldest known telling of the coffee legend comes from a book written to praise coffee, by a man in Rome, roughly 800 years after the goats were supposed to have found it.

And that book is real, it's not one of those sources that only exists because somebody else says it does. It's in library catalogues, including the University of Bologna's, and there's a scan of it you can look at online. A peer-reviewed history of caffeine from 2010, by Bertil Fredholm, says the story of Kaldi "appears to occur first" in Nairon's book, and it says "local sources for the legend are lacking." So as far as that history can tell, nobody has turned up the story in older sources from the places it's supposed to come from.

## 04 Camels, Or Goats

And there's more, because the story in Nairon's book isn't quite the story you've heard. In 1922 a writer named William Ukers put out a big book called All About Coffee, and he quoted Nairon's version, and in that version the man is a keeper of camels, "or, as others say, of goats." So the oldest version we have isn't even sure which animals he was looking after.

He's a herder who brings his complaint to the monks at a monastery, in a place Nairon calls Ayaman, and he says that's Arabia Felix, and Arabia Felix is an old name for Yemen. And he doesn't have a name, he's just the herder. So think about what that does to the story. The two things the modern version is surest about are the name Kaldi and the country Ethiopia, and neither one is in the oldest version anybody's found. The herder has no name, he lives in Yemen, and he might have been watching camels.

## 05 Where The Name Came From

So where does Kaldi come from, and the honest answer is later retellings, because the name seems to have been made popular by European retellings that came long after Nairon, and Ukers' 1922 book is one of the big ones.

Now, I want to be careful here, because you'll sometimes hear that Ukers made the name up, and nobody has shown that. Maybe he did, maybe he picked it up from somebody before him, we don't know. What we know is that Nairon's herder didn't have a name, and that later retellings gave him one. And that's how a lot of these stories go, right? Somebody writes a version down, somebody else retells it and fills in a name and a place, and after enough retellings the filled-in version is the only one anybody remembers.

## 06 Who Actually Drank It First

So if it wasn't Kaldi, then who was drinking it first, and the answer is that the earliest solid evidence of people actually drinking coffee comes from the 1400s, in Sufi monasteries in Yemen. Sufis are Muslim mystics, and the coffee kept the worshippers awake through their night-time devotions. That's where Britannica puts the earliest substantiated evidence, and the National Coffee Association's own history has the drink spreading out from Yemen and Mocha.

One history from JSTOR Daily describes how coffee "went from a mystical sacrament to an everyday drink," and that's a good way to put it, because before it was something you drank to get through a morning, it was something people drank to stay up and pray. So the plant comes from Ethiopia, and the drink, as far as the evidence goes, starts with those Sufis in Yemen.

Oh, one more thing. That leaves the legend in an odd spot, because the oldest version of the goat story and the real history of the drink both point at Yemen. Somewhere between Nairon and today, the legend moved to Ethiopia and picked up a name.

## 07 The Plain Version

So here's the plain version, the coffee plant is native to Ethiopia, and nobody's arguing with that, but the drink is first documented in the 1400s, in Sufi monasteries in Yemen, where it kept people awake through the night. And the goat story first shows up in 1671, in a Latin book written in Rome by Antoine Faustus Nairon, about a herder with no name, in Yemen, who kept camels, or as others said, goats. The name Kaldi came later. Somebody did it first, and as far as the evidence goes, it was those Sufis in Yemen, a long time before anybody wrote down a story about a goatherd.
