# Pythagoras' Theorem Was in Use 1,000 Years Before Him

## 01 Cold Open

There's a clay tablet from somewhere around 3,700 to 3,800 years ago that shows Pythagoras' theorem in use, and that's more than a thousand years before Pythagoras was born. Everybody learns it in school with his name on it, and he's the name we all remember, but Babylonian scribes were using the same relationship about a thousand years before him.

## 02 The School Version

So here's the version we all got in school. Take a right triangle, the kind with one square corner, square the two short sides and add them up, and you get the square of the long side, a squared plus b squared equals c squared, and that's Pythagoras' theorem. The easy classroom example is a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5, because 3 squared is 9, 4 squared is 16, and 9 plus 16 is 25, which is 5 squared. A set of three whole numbers that fits like that is called a Pythagorean triple.

And the name stuck because it's been taught that way for a very long time, right? Pythagoras lived roughly 570 to 495 BCE, he's a famous name, and the theorem is one of the first real pieces of math most of us ever learn by name. But here's the odd part, Pythagoras didn't leave any writings, and the idea of crediting him with the theorem came centuries after he died.

## 03 Plimpton 322

Now here's the part that matters. There's a clay tablet called Plimpton 322, and it's from around 1800 to 1700 BCE, from Old Babylonian times, and it belongs to Columbia University. And on it there are 15 rows of numbers, and those rows are Pythagorean triples, sets of numbers that fit a squared plus b squared equals c squared, the same kind of thing as 3, 4, 5.

So more than a thousand years before Pythagoras, a Babylonian scribe was writing out rows of numbers that fit his theorem. The standard reading of the tablet goes back to 1945, to two scholars named Neugebauer and Sachs, and Britannica puts it at about a thousand years before Pythagoras.

## 04 YBC 7289

And there's more, because there's a second tablet, it's called YBC 7289, it's in the Yale Babylonian Collection, and it's from around 1800 to 1600 BCE. It shows a square with its diagonal drawn across it, and the diagonal is labelled with the square root of 2, accurate to about six decimal places.

And here's why that matters, if you take a square with sides of 1, the diagonal cuts it into two right triangles, and by the theorem the diagonal squared is 1 plus 1, which is 2, so the diagonal is the square root of 2. So that tablet is the theorem being applied to a right triangle with two equal sides, and the number on it is really, really accurate. There's even a peer-reviewed paper in a history of math journal that's just about how that estimate of the square root of 2 may have been calculated.

## 05 Used, Not Proved

Now, to be fair to Pythagoras, I want to be careful about what these tablets show. They show the Babylonians used the relationship, but they don't show that the Babylonians proved it, or wrote it down as a general rule the way we teach it, and whether they had a general proof or statement of it is still argued about. So the fair way to say it is that they used it, about a thousand years earlier, not that they proved it first.

Oh, one more thing, you might have seen headlines back in 2017 saying Plimpton 322 was the world's first trigonometry. Scientific American called that hype, and I'm not going to repeat it as fact. The tablet doesn't need it, right? Rows of Pythagorean triples from around 1800 BCE is plenty.

## 06 The Plain Fact

So here's the plain fact, Pythagoras lived around 570 to 495 BCE, he didn't leave any writings, and his name got attached to the theorem centuries after he died. More than a thousand years before him, Babylonian scribes were writing Pythagorean triples on Plimpton 322, and writing down the square root of 2 to about six decimal places on YBC 7289. They used it, we can't say they proved it. Somebody did it first, and it was the Babylonian scribes.
