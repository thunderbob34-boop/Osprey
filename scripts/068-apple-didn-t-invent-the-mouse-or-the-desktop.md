# Apple Didn't Invent the Mouse (or the Desktop)

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 5 to 6 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 855 spoken words. The research is narrow, so this one runs under the usual length on purpose rather than padding.
- Tone is calm and fair. Apple is not the villain here. Don't say Apple stole anything, the script doesn't, and the on-screen text shouldn't either.
- Say Engelbart as ENG-el-bart, PARC as park, Alto as AL-toh.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "mid-1960s", "according to Smithsonian magazine", "reinforced work Apple already had under way" and "licensed" exactly as written. Don't put a dollar figure on the SRI license anywhere, on screen or in the description.
- Visuals: archive footage of the 1968 demo, the Computer History Museum's photo of the wooden prototype, the patent drawing for US 3,541,541, photos of the Xerox Alto, and text cards.

## 01 Cold Open

More than fifteen years before the Macintosh, a man in San Francisco showed the world a mouse, clickable links and windows on a screen, all in one afternoon. A lot of people think Apple invented the mouse and the desktop, the pointer and the icons and the folders, because the Lisa and the Macintosh are where a lot of people picture it starting. But the mouse was built in the mid-1960s, and the man who showed it off was Douglas Engelbart, and his patent doesn't even call it a mouse.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here's the version most people carry around. Apple's Lisa came out in 1983 and the Macintosh came out in 1984, and those are the computers a lot of people credit with the mouse and the graphical desktop, the screen with little pictures you point at and click instead of typing commands. And it's easy to see why the credit lands there, those were the machines that put the Apple name on all of it, but by 1984, the mouse was already about twenty years old.

## 03 The Wooden Box

In the mid-1960s, Douglas Engelbart and Bill English built a prototype mouse at a research lab called SRI. And the dates on this differ a little depending on where you look, some say 1964 and some say 1965, so mid-1960s is the honest way to put it.

The first computer mouse was a wooden box with two metal wheels, and the Computer History Museum has a write-up on it. And when Engelbart patented it, the patent never called it a mouse. The patent is US 3,541,541, it was filed in 1967 and issued on November 17th, 1970, and its title is "X-Y position indicator for a display system," and X and Y just means across and up and down, the position on the screen. So it's a box that tells the computer where you're pointing, which is exactly what a mouse is, right? It just didn't have the name on the paperwork.

## 04 The Mother of All Demos

Now here's the part that matters. On December 9th, 1968, Engelbart got up in front of an audience and showed what his team had built, and it's gone down in history as the Mother of All Demos. In about ninety minutes, he showed the mouse, he showed hypertext, which is the clickable links you use on every web page, he showed windows on the screen, and he showed real-time collaborative editing, more than one person working on the same thing at the same time.

That's a mouse, links, windows and shared editing, all in one demonstration, in 1968. The IEEE, the big professional organization for electrical engineers, made it an official IEEE Milestone, under the name Public Demonstration of Online Systems and Personal Computing, 1968, and the Smithsonian has a whole write-up on what was in it. And there's footage of it, so if you've never seen it, it's worth looking up, because it's a man in 1968 doing things that look like a normal workday now.

## 05 Xerox and the Alto

And there's more, because the desktop didn't start at Apple either. Over at Xerox's research lab, PARC, they built a computer called the Alto in 1973, and the Alto had a mouse and a desktop of icons, folders and documents. So the screen with little pictures of folders that you click on, that was running at Xerox about ten years before the Lisa. IEEE Spectrum, the engineering magazine, ran a piece on the Alto with the title Fifty Years Later, We're Still Living in the Xerox Alto's World, and you can see why. And in December 1979, Steve Jobs and some Apple engineers visited PARC.

## 06 Fair Credit to Apple

Now, to be fair, you'll hear people say Apple stole all of this from Xerox, and that's not what the record says. Stanford University's library has a page on that PARC visit, and it says the visit reinforced work Apple already had under way. So Apple wasn't starting from nothing when they walked in there. And on the mouse itself, Apple licensed the mouse patent from SRI, which means they paid for the right to use it, so that's not stealing, right?

And the Lisa and the Macintosh are the computers that made a lot of people connect the mouse and the desktop with Apple, and that's why their name is the one people remember, and that counts for something.

Oh, one more thing. According to Smithsonian magazine, Engelbart never received royalties for the mouse, he got a one-time lump sum and that was it. That comes from one source, so take it as theirs.

## 07 The Box With Two Wheels

So the plain version is this. Douglas Engelbart and Bill English built the first mouse in the mid-1960s, a wooden box with two metal wheels, Engelbart's patent called it an X-Y position indicator for a display system, filed in 1967 and issued in 1970, and on December 9th, 1968, he showed the mouse, links, windows and shared editing in about ninety minutes. Xerox's Alto had a mouse and a desktop in 1973, Apple visited in 1979, and the Lisa and the Macintosh came in 1983 and 1984. Somebody did it first, and for the mouse, that was Engelbart and English.
