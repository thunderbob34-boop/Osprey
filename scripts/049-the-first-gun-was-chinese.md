# The First Gun Was Chinese

## 01 Cold Open

The oldest picture we have of a gunpowder weapon shows a demon pointing a fire lance at the Buddha. It's on a Chinese silk banner from Dunhuang, from around the year 950, and it's the first gunpowder weapon we can actually see. Now, whether that fire lance counts as the first gun is a real argument, and we'll get into it, but here's the part that isn't an argument, the oldest surviving metal guns in the world are Chinese too.

## 02 The Picture in Our Heads

When most people picture early guns, they picture hand cannons and long clunky guns from the 1300s and 1400s, a very European picture. And that's not crazy, that's the part of the story most of us know. But it skips the beginning, and the beginning is in China.

## 03 The Banner

So let's start with the banner. It's a Chinese silk banner from Dunhuang, around 950 CE, and it shows a demon holding a pole with a flaming tube on the end, and pointing it at the Buddha. That's a fire lance. And a fire lance is basically a gunpowder flamethrower on a spear, a tube packed with gunpowder on the end of a pole, and when you light it, fire shoots out the front.

And that banner is the earliest picture of a gunpowder weapon that we have. After that, the next time fire lances show up is in 1132, at the siege of a place called De'an. And Archaeology Magazine describes the fire lance as a proto-gun by about 1150.

## 04 What Counts as a Gun

Now here's the part that matters. Is a fire lance a gun? It depends on what you mean by gun, and the historians I'm going by say no, not quite. The historian Tonio Andrade, in his book The Gunpowder Age, from Princeton in 2016, defines a true gun as one that fires a projectile that fits the barrel, a bullet that fills the tube. A fire lance doesn't do that, it shoots fire, so it's not a true gun.

So historians call the fire lance the ancestor of the gun, a proto-gun, and Britannica calls these early tube weapons a proto-gun too, the idea being that you're channeling gunpowder through a cylinder. And Britannica describes bamboo tubes in the 13th century that were firing projectiles. So you can watch it turn into a gun over a few hundred years, right? First fire out of a tube on a spear, then tubes firing things out of them, and then metal.

## 05 The Oldest Metal Guns

And the oldest surviving metal guns are Chinese. There's the Heilongjiang hand cannon, which dates to no later than 1288. Now, that date comes from where it was found, the archaeological context, because it doesn't have an inscription, so I'm saying no later than 1288, going by where it was found.

Then there's the Xanadu gun, and that one does have an inscription, and the inscription gives the year 1298. That makes it the oldest gun with a dated inscription.

And to be fair to the other side of the argument, there's even a debate in here about how early Chinese guns go. There's a bronze cannon from Wuwei that's put around 1214 to 1227, but that date comes from context too, and it's debated, and one historian, Chen Bingying, argues there were no guns before 1259. So the dates at the very start are fuzzy, but the Xanadu gun has its year written right on it.

## 06 Who Was First

So was the first gun Chinese? If you go by the strict definition, a true gun that fires a bullet that fits the barrel, the fire lance doesn't count, but the oldest surviving metal guns we have are still Chinese, the Heilongjiang hand cannon from no later than 1288 and the Xanadu gun inscribed with 1298. And if you go back further, to the ancestor of the gun, the fire lance, the first one we can see is on a Chinese silk banner from around 950, a demon pointing it at the Buddha. Either way you count it, it starts in China. Somebody did it first, and somebody put it on a banner.
