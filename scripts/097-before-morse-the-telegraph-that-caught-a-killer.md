## 01 Cold Open

The first criminal ever caught by telecommunications was outrun by a wire, and it wasn't Morse's wire. In January 1845 a murderer got on a train at Slough thinking he'd gotten away, but police sent his description ahead by telegraph, and officers were waiting for him when he got off at Paddington. And the telegraph that did it wasn't Samuel Morse's, it was a British one, built by two men named William Fothergill Cooke and Charles Wheatstone.

## 02 The Morse Version

Samuel Morse is the name people know, the Library of Congress keeps his papers, and he's popularly credited with inventing the telegraph, and you can see why, because Morse code is the thing everybody's heard of. Morse developed his system independently, and his code became the world standard, and his simpler design, with a key on one end and a sounder on the other, is the one that won out in the long run.

So Morse earned a lot of credit, and he's going to keep it here. But the first English patent and the first permanent commercial telegraph, running along a railway, those weren't his.

## 03 Cooke and Wheatstone

On June 12th, 1837, William Fothergill Cooke and Charles Wheatstone got the first English patent for an electric telegraph, and a patent is just the government's official record that says this invention is yours, with your name and your date on it, so June 12th, 1837 is on paper. And there's a nice detail on the timing, because Alfred Vail, who joined Morse as a partner, only joined him after seeing a demonstration in September 1837, so that's three months after Cooke and Wheatstone already had their patent.

Then in 1839 their system went into the first permanent commercial telegraph service, along the Great Western Railway, from Paddington to West Drayton, about thirteen miles. And if you want to see what one of these looked like, the Science Museum Group has a Cooke and Wheatstone four-needle telegraph from 1838 in its collection.

Now, to be fair, I want to be careful here. Cooke and Wheatstone were the first commercial telegraph, not the first electric telegraph experiment of any kind. There were earlier experimental systems, and I didn't look into those for this video, so I'm not going to tell you Cooke and Wheatstone invented the whole idea. What they did was get the first English patent and put the thing into real, everyday service.

## 04 The Train From Slough

And that brings us to January 1845. The UK National Archives has the case on record as the murder of Sarah Hart, who also went by Ann Lawrence, by a man named John Tawell.

I'll keep this plain. Tawell got on a train at Slough, thinking he'd escaped. But the police sent his description ahead by telegraph, and the message got to Paddington before he did, so when he got off at Paddington, officers were waiting for him. King's College London calls it the first capture of a criminal by telecommunications, and the Science Museum's blog covers the same case. So the train was fast, and the wire was faster, right?

And as King's College London tells it, people started calling the telegraph wires "the cords that hanged John Tawell."

## 05 What About Morse Code

Oh, one more thing, because it's the other half of the credit question. Even the name Morse code has a credit fight in it. Alfred Vail, Morse's partner, is the one some historians point to, and how much of the code was really Vail's is contested.

And it matters, because if the code is the part of the telegraph everybody remembers, then who actually worked out the code is a big part of who gets remembered. So I'll put it to you as a question, not a claim. How much of Morse code was Morse, and how much was Vail? Historians don't agree, and I'm not going to pretend they do.

## 06 Who Did It First

So here's the plain version. Samuel Morse developed his telegraph independently, and his system and his code won out and became the world standard, and that credit is earned. But William Fothergill Cooke and Charles Wheatstone got the first English patent for an electric telegraph on June 12th, 1837, and their system was the first permanent commercial telegraph, running along the Great Western Railway from Paddington in 1839. And in January 1845, it was a Cooke and Wheatstone telegraph, not a Morse one, that got a killer's description to Paddington ahead of him. Somebody did it first, and it was two men most people have never heard of.
