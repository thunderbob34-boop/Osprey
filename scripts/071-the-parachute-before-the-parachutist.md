# The Parachute Before the Parachutist

## 01 Cold Open

About five hundred years after Leonardo da Vinci drew a parachute, a man finally jumped with it, and it worked. The man who gets the credit for the parachute is André-Jacques Garnerin, who made the first recorded parachute jump in 1797, and that credit is fair, but the parachute was on paper for about three hundred years before he jumped, first in a sketch by Leonardo and then in a book by a Croatian-Venetian bishop.

## 02 The Famous Version

So here's the version most people know, if they know one. On October 22nd, 1797, André-Jacques Garnerin went up over Parc Monceau in Paris in a hydrogen balloon, and at about 3,200 feet he cut himself loose from the balloon and came down under a parachute. That's the first recorded parachute descent, and Britannica and the Smithsonian both back that up.

And that's a real first, nobody before him has a documented jump, so when people say Garnerin made the first parachute jump, that's right. But the parachute itself, the idea and the drawing of it, is a lot older than 1797.

## 03 Leonardo's Pyramid

Around 1485, Leonardo da Vinci sketched a parachute shaped like a pyramid. Now, to be clear, this was a drawing, and as far as the record goes, nobody jumped with Leonardo's design until the year 2000, which we'll get to. So we're not saying Leonardo's design came first in use, Garnerin has that. What Leonardo had was the idea, drawn out, more than three hundred years before Garnerin went up over Paris.

## 04 The Flying Man

And there's more, because Leonardo isn't the only one who drew a parachute before Garnerin. There's a Croatian-Venetian bishop named Faust Vrančić, who's also known by the Italian version of his name, Fausto Veranzio, and he lived from 1551 to 1617. He published a book called Machinae Novae, which means new machines, and in it there's an engraving called Homo Volans, the flying man, of a man hanging under a square parachute with a frame.

Now, the dates on that book are a bit messy. The first edition is often given as 1595, and the Library of Congress dates the Homo Volans print to 1615, so the honest way to say it is the early 1600s. And you can see the print for yourself, the Library of Congress has it, and so does the Smithsonian's air and space archive. So that's two people with a parachute on paper long before anybody actually jumped, Leonardo in the 1480s and Vrančić in the early 1600s.

## 05 The Jump in 2000

Now here's the part that matters. A parachute works by catching air, it's a big surface that the air pushes against as you fall, so you come down slower, and the question with Leonardo's pyramid was whether that shape would actually do it, right? For about five hundred years, as far as anyone knows, nobody had tried it to find out. And then on June 26th, 2000, a British skydiver named Adrian Nicholas built Leonardo's parachute out of wood and canvas, and he jumped with it from 10,000 feet.

And it worked, it flew gently, but at about 2,000 feet he cut away from it, because the whole rig weighed 185 pounds, and it might have crushed him when he landed. So he rode Leonardo's design most of the way down, and then he let it go for the last part.

PBS has a Secrets of the Dead video on it, called Did Leonardo Really Invent the Parachute?, and Britannica has the numbers, the date, the 10,000 feet, the cutaway at about 2,000, and the 185 pounds.

So the fair way to say it is that Leonardo's parachute was tested, and it flew, but Nicholas only partly came down under it, and that test came about five hundred years after the drawing.

## 06 Who Drew It First

So the plain version is this. Leonardo da Vinci sketched a pyramid-shaped parachute around 1485, Faust Vrančić published his Homo Volans engraving of a framed square parachute in the early 1600s, and André-Jacques Garnerin made the first recorded parachute jump over Paris on October 22nd, 1797. And in 2000, Adrian Nicholas built Leonardo's version, rode it down from 10,000 feet to about 2,000, and it flew. Garnerin was the first to jump, but somebody did it first on paper, and that was Leonardo.
