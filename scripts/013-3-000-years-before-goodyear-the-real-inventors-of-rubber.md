# 3,000 Years Before Goodyear: The Real Inventors of Rubber

READING NOTES
- Runtime is about 6 minutes at a relaxed pace, roughly 898 spoken words.
- Tone is calm and impressed. The ancient rubber makers are the heroes, and Goodyear is the man who found the industrial version, not a villain.
- Say Castilla elastica as kas-TEE-yah eh-LAS-tih-kah, Ipomoea alba as ip-oh-MEE-ah AL-bah, Mesoamerica as MEZ-oh-uh-MAIR-ih-kuh, Hosler as HOZE-ler, and Tarkanian as tar-KAY-nee-un.
- Every hedge in here is on purpose. Read "by about 1600 BC", "the researchers' estimate", "more than three thousand years", "according to Britain's Science Museum Group", and "that's not vulcanization" exactly as written. Never call the ancient method vulcanization, on screen or in an ad-lib.
- Visuals: use rubber trees, morning glory vines, museum rubber balls and sandals, and maps. No ballgame sacrifice or beheading imagery, even though some coverage of this topic leans on it.
- Good on-screen text moments are about 1600 BC, 1839, 1844, 21 Nov 1843, and June 18, 1999.

## 01 Cold Open

Charles Goodyear got the name on the tire, but someone was making bouncing rubber balls three thousand years before he was born. And these weren't lucky accidents either, the people of ancient Mexico and Central America had a recipe for rubber, and they changed the recipe depending on what they were making, a bouncier rubber for balls and a tougher rubber for the soles of sandals. So this is a story about a famous inventor who really did figure something out, and the people who figured out their own version of it more than three thousand years before him.

## 02 The Goodyear Version

Here's the story most people know. In 1839 Charles Goodyear discovered that if you treat rubber with sulfur and heat, you get a material you can actually build things out of, and that process is called vulcanization, and he got his US patent for it in 1844. And the popular way that story gets told is that Goodyear invented rubber, like before him there wasn't any rubber worth using, and then he came along and made it.

And you can see why it stuck, his name is on the tire, so every time people think about rubber, his is the name sitting right there. But Goodyear didn't invent rubber, and he wasn't the first person to take the raw stuff from a tree and turn it into something useful, not by a long way.

## 03 Ancient Mexico and Central America

The people who were first lived in what historians call Mesoamerica, which is ancient Mexico and Central America. There's a tree there called Castilla elastica, and it gives you latex, which is a milky sap, and there's a morning glory vine there called Ipomoea alba, and what they figured out is that if you take the latex from that tree and process it with juice from that vine, you get real rubber.

And they were doing this by about 1600 BC. That date is the researchers' estimate, and it comes from a team at MIT, Hosler, Burkett and Tarkanian, who published the work in the journal Science on June 18th, 1999. So by their estimate, the rubber makers of ancient Mesoamerica were at it more than three thousand years before Goodyear's discovery, right?

## 04 They Had Recipes

Now here's the part that matters, it wasn't one product, they didn't just make rubber, they tuned it. Later research written up by MIT, and covered separately by National Geographic, found they varied the recipe depending on what the rubber was for. So for the balls they played their ballgame with, they made it bouncier, and for sandal soles, they made it tougher, so it would hold up, and they also made rubber for adhesives, for sticking things together.

That's the part I keep coming back to. They didn't stumble onto a sticky material and stop there, they had different mixes for different jobs, which is what a material scientist does today when they adjust a formula. They had bouncy balls, they had tough soles, and they had glue, all from the same tree and the same vine, mixed different ways. MIT's own news office ran that story under the headline A good many years before Goodyear, and that's about the plainest way anybody could put it.

## 05 Fair Credit to Goodyear

Now, to be fair to Goodyear, what he did was real, and it's not the same thing. The Science paper credits organic compounds in the vine juice for what happens to the latex, not sulfur, and not sulfur and heat, so that's not vulcanization. It's a different chemistry that gets you to a similar result, which is rubber you can actually use.

So you shouldn't say the ancient Mesoamericans invented vulcanization, because they didn't, they invented their own way of making rubber. And Goodyear's 1839 discovery, the sulfur and heat method, still stands as the industrial process. That's a big deal, and he deserves full credit for it, he just didn't invent rubber, and he wasn't the first person to turn tree sap into a material people could rely on.

## 06 The Race in England

And there's one more twist, and this one we have to say softly, because it comes from one strong source. According to Britain's Science Museum Group, a man in England named Thomas Hancock filed a provisional patent on vulcanization on November 21st, 1843, and that was after he'd examined samples of Goodyear's rubber. A provisional patent is basically an early filing that stakes your claim while the full paperwork catches up. That filing date is before Goodyear's US patent was granted in 1844, so even the process that made Goodyear famous had a rival filing on the other side of the Atlantic, right?

Now, I'd keep that one in perspective. Goodyear's discovery was 1839, and Hancock was looking at Goodyear's samples, so nobody's saying Hancock had the idea first, it's just that the paperwork race was closer than most people know.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's how it actually goes. By about 1600 BC, people in ancient Mexico and Central America were processing latex from the Castilla elastica tree with morning glory juice to make real rubber, and they made it bouncier for balls, tougher for sandal soles, and sticky for adhesives. In 1839, Charles Goodyear discovered vulcanization, the sulfur and heat process, and he got his US patent in 1844. They're different methods, and both are real achievements, but one of them came more than three thousand years before the other. Somebody did it first.
