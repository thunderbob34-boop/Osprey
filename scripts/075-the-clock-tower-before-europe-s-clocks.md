## 01 Cold Open

By 1088, a clock tower in the Chinese city of Kaifeng was keeping time with water and an early kind of escapement, which is the part of a clock that makes it tick along in steady steps. When most of us think about where the clock came from, we think about Europe, about pendulums and clockmakers, and Europe really did do a lot with it. But a water-driven clock tower in China had an escapement-type mechanism by 1088, and there was an earlier one in China before that.

## 02 The Famous Version

So the version most of us picked up goes like this. The mechanical clock is a European thing, and the big names in the story are European, people like Huygens, who built the first working pendulum clock in 1656.

And the heart of any mechanical clock is the escapement. Here's the plain version of what it does. Something has to push a clock along, a weight or a spring or, in this case, water, and if you just let that push go, it all runs out at once. The escapement is the piece that holds it back and lets it go a little at a time, in small, even steps, and that's what turns a push into a steady beat you can keep time with, right? So when we ask who had the escapement first, we're really asking who had the guts of the clock first.

## 03 Su Song's Clock Tower

And that's where Su Song comes in. In 1088, in Kaifeng, Su Song's astronomical clock tower was running, and it was water-driven, and it had an escapement-type mechanism. Now, an astronomical clock is one that's built to follow the sky, not just the hour, and this one was a tower, and the thing doing the pushing was water, and the thing letting that push out in steady steps was an early kind of escapement.

And here's the part that matters, that's 1088, which is more than five hundred years before Huygens built his working pendulum clock in 1656. And Su Song wasn't even the first in China, because there was an earlier one, from Yi Xing, in 725, more than three hundred and fifty years before Su Song's tower.

So the honest way to say it is that China had escapement-type mechanisms in water-driven clocks centuries before Huygens and his pendulum. I'm saying escapement-type on purpose, because it's an early kind of escapement working with water, and that's the fair way to put it.

## 04 Fair Credit, and Galileo

Now, to be fair to Europe, Huygens did something real. He made the first working pendulum clock, in 1656, and that's his, nobody's taking that away.

But even that one has a somebody-got-there-first twist. Galileo designed a pendulum clock before he died in 1642, and his son Vincenzio tried to build it. So Galileo had the design first, right? His son tried to make it real, and Huygens is the one who made a pendulum clock that actually worked, in 1656, and that's why it's Huygens' name on it. Designed first, built first, those aren't the same thing, and both of them deserve to be said out loud.

## 05 Way Further Back

Oh, one more thing, and this goes way further back than any of them. In 2013 a team from the University of Basel was digging in the Valley of the Kings in Egypt, and they found a sundial painted in black on a flat piece of limestone, a half-circle split into twelve sections, with a hole in the middle for a peg to cast a shadow.

It dates to the 13th century BCE, and they found it among the huts of the workmen who cut the royal tombs, so it was probably used to time their work. Now, to be fair, the team's own press release called it one of the world's oldest sundials, not the oldest, because Egyptians were already using sundials and shadow clocks by around 1500 BCE. So it's one of the oldest. It's one of the oldest, and it's not even the oldest, and that tells you how long people have been working on this problem, right?

And there's one claim in this part of the story to be careful with. A popular timeline from NIST, the US standards agency, says Egyptian obelisks were being built as early as 3500 BCE, and you'll see that tied to the idea of obelisks as shadow clocks. But Britannica says obelisks are known from Egypt's 4th dynasty, about 2575 to 2465 BCE, none survive from that time, and the earliest one that does survive is Senusret I's, from 1918 to 1875 BCE. So that 3500 BCE date doesn't hold up, and we're leaving it out.

## 06 Who Had It First

So the plain version goes like this. Huygens built the first working pendulum clock in 1656, and Galileo had designed one before he died in 1642. But in 1088, in Kaifeng, Su Song's water-driven clock tower already had an escapement-type mechanism, and Yi Xing had an earlier one in 725, and Egyptians were telling time with shadow clocks by around 1500 BCE.

Somebody did it first, and on the escapement, the part that makes a clock tick along in steady steps, China had one working in a water-driven clock tower by 1088, and Yi Xing's came earlier still.
