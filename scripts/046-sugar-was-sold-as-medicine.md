## 01 Cold Open

When sugar first reached Europe, you didn't buy it at the grocer, you bought it at the apothecary, next to the spices and the medicines. It was a luxury, it sat on the shelf beside pepper and cinnamon, and it was prescribed for coughs and fevers. And by the time Europe got its hands on it, people on the other side of the world had been growing sugarcane for thousands of years, and people in India had worked out how to turn it into crystals.

## 02 Sugar at the Apothecary

So let's start with the apothecary, because it's the part that surprises people. An apothecary was basically a pharmacist, the place you went for medicine, and when sugar first showed up in medieval Europe, that's where it was sold. It was expensive, it was a luxury, and it went on the same shelf as pepper and cinnamon, and it got prescribed for coughs and fevers.

Sidney Mintz wrote a book about this in 1985 called Sweetness and Power, and he lists five ways sugar got used. It was a spice, it was a medicine, it was a decoration, it was a sweetener, and it was a preservative. So sweetener was just one job out of five, right? We think of sugar as the sweet stuff, and in medieval Europe it was also the cough medicine.

## 03 New Guinea

Now, the usual story of sugar in the West is a European story, Europe and the plantations in the Caribbean. But the sugar story doesn't start in Europe, and it doesn't start in the Caribbean either.

It starts with the plant, and sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea, thousands of years ago. You'll see specific dates for that floating around, but the ones I found only show up in secondary sources, so I'm going to leave it at thousands of years ago. Either way, the people of New Guinea were growing sugarcane long, long before sugar ever reached medieval Europe, right?

## 04 Crystals From India

And then there's the part that turned cane into the sugar we'd recognize. Somebody had to figure out how to boil cane juice down into solid crystals, and that was worked out in India. Better sources put crystallised sugar in India at least by the start of the common era, and possibly as early as around 500 BCE.

And here's a fun one, the word sugar itself goes back to India. It comes from the Sanskrit word śarkarā, which means grit, which is pretty much what a pile of sugar crystals looks like, so every time you say sugar, you're using a word that started out in India meaning grit.

And Greek and Roman writers already knew about Indian sugar by the 1st century AD, so the Mediterranean world had heard about it, and then Persians and Arabs spread the refining technique before Europe saw it. The evidence for that is solid in outline, even if a lot of the specific dates are fuzzy.

## 05 The Gupta Myth

Now, there's one more myth in here, and it's a small one, but you'll run into it. A lot of popular websites will tell you sugar crystals were invented in India under the Gupta dynasty, around the 5th century AD, like that's the date. But that claim comes mostly from popular sites, and the better sources put crystallised sugar in India much earlier, at least by the start of the common era. So if you hear Gupta, the fair way to say it is that India had crystal sugar by the Gupta period at the latest, not that it was invented then.

## 06 Who Was First

So who did sugar first? Farmers in New Guinea domesticated sugarcane, thousands of years ago. People in India worked out how to boil the juice into crystals, at least by the start of the common era and possibly around 500 BCE, and they gave us the word, from śarkarā, grit. Greek and Roman writers knew about it by the 1st century AD, and Persians and Arabs spread the refining before Europe saw it. And when it finally got to medieval Europe, it went on the apothecary's shelf, next to the pepper and cinnamon, as a medicine for coughs and fevers. Somebody did it first, and it was New Guinea and India, long before Europe.
