# Peanut Butter Before George Washington Carver

## 01 Cold Open

Ask anyone who invented peanut butter and they'll say George Washington Carver, and plenty of people really do believe that, but the patent office says a Canadian pharmacist had a patent on a peanut paste decades before Carver's peanut recipes ever came out. And I want to say this right up front, this isn't a story about Carver getting caught out, Carver was a real scientist who did real work. It's a story about a myth that got stuck to the wrong man, and the myth actually makes him smaller, not bigger.

## 02 The Famous Version

The way most people hear it, Carver is the peanut man, and somewhere along the way peanut butter got folded into that, so now plenty of people will tell you he invented it, full stop. And you can see why it stuck, because Carver really did make the peanut a big part of his work, and in 1916 he put out a bulletin with a hundred and five peanut recipes in it, and yes, peanut butter is one of them.

But a recipe in a bulletin isn't an invention, right? If you can put peanut butter in a recipe collection in 1916, that means somebody already had peanut butter. And when you go looking for who had it on paper first, the trail goes back more than thirty years before that bulletin, and it goes to Canada.

## 03 The Pharmacist From Montreal

His name was Marcellus Gilmore Edson, he was born in 1849 in Bedford, Quebec, and he worked as a pharmacist in Montreal. And in 1884 he got a United States patent, number 306,727, issued on October 21st, 1884, for roasted peanuts milled into a paste.

Now a patent, if you've never read one, is a public document where an inventor describes the thing in enough detail that the government will give them the rights to it for a while, and the useful part for us is that the words don't go anywhere, you can still pull up Edson's patent and read it today. And in his own words, the paste had "a consistency like that of butter, lard, or ointment."

And here's the part that matters, Edson didn't call it peanut butter, he called it peanut-candy, so he had the paste, and he had the word butter sitting right there in his own patent as a comparison, but the name he actually put on it was peanut-candy.

## 04 So Did Edson Invent It?

Now, to be fair, it would be really easy to swap one myth for another here and say Edson invented peanut butter, and we're not going to say that. The Canadian Encyclopedia, which has a whole entry on him, says he may not have invented peanut butter. What Edson has is a patent with a date on it, and that's the earliest piece of paper in this story, but it's not proof that nobody before him ever ground up a peanut.

And there's more, because Edson isn't the only name ahead of Carver. Dr. John Harvey Kellogg patented a process for making nut butter, a paste called nutmeal that could be made from peanuts or other nuts, and he applied for that patent in 1895. History.com says most food historians credit Kellogg's 1895 patent as the first patent on a process for making nut butter. So Edson's patent is for a peanut paste, and Kellogg's is for a way of making nut butter, and those are two different things, so both names end up in this story.

Then there's a man named George Bayle in St. Louis, who, according to History.com, may have been selling peanut butter by 1894, and that's a may have, and we're going to leave it as a may have.

## 05 Long Before Any Patent

Oh, one more thing, and this one goes back a lot further than any patent office. According to History.com, Andean peoples in South America, the Inca, were making pastes out of peanuts long before any of this.

So when somebody asks who invented peanut butter, the honest answer is that you can't hand it to one person, right? People were grinding peanuts into paste long before anyone wrote a patent for it. And once the patents start, the first one in this story belongs to Edson in 1884, then Kellogg comes along with his nut butter process in 1895, and Carver's recipe bulletin comes along in 1916, which puts Carver last of the three.

## 06 Fair Credit to Carver

Now, as for Carver, Smithsonian ran a piece called "In Search of George Washington Carver's True Legacy," and the point of it is that the myth ends up standing in front of the real man. Carver was a scientist, and the peanut butter story takes his actual work and swaps it out for something he didn't do.

And that's why it's worth getting this one right, because he didn't invent peanut butter, and he doesn't need to have invented peanut butter to be worth knowing about.

## 07 Who Had It First

So here's the plain version, people in the Andes were making peanut pastes long before any of it was written down in a patent, and then a pharmacist in Montreal named Marcellus Gilmore Edson got a US patent in 1884 for roasted peanuts made into a paste like butter, lard, or ointment, and he called it peanut-candy. Kellogg applied for his nut butter patent in 1895, and George Washington Carver's bulletin with a hundred and five peanut recipes came out in 1916. Somebody did it first, and on paper, the first patent in this story belongs to a pharmacist from Quebec.
