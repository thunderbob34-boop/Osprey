## 01 Cold Open

The Romans told a great story about how glass was discovered, a story about merchants, a beach and a cooking fire, and it goes back about two thousand years. But even the Corning Museum of Glass says nobody really knows how glass was first made. And what we do know is that people were making glass a very long time before the Romans ever wrote that story down.

## 02 Pliny's Story

The story comes from Pliny the Elder, a Roman writer, in the first century AD. In his Natural History, in Book 36, he says glass was discovered by accident. Merchants carrying a cargo of natron landed on the shore near the Belus river, in Phoenicia, which is in what's now Lebanon or Israel. Natron is a kind of natural soda. And they propped their cooking pots up on lumps of the natron, and the heat of the fire fused the natron with the sand on the beach, and out ran a stream of glass.

And you can see why it stuck, right? It's got an accident, a cooking fire, and glass turning up where nobody was trying to make it. One small thing, you'll often hear this told with sailors, but Pliny says merchants, so we'll say merchants.

## 03 What the Experts Say

Now here's the part that matters. The Corning Museum of Glass tells Pliny's story, and they tell it as a legend, and they say plainly that no one really knows how glass came to be made.

And I want to be fair to Pliny here too, because you'll sometimes hear people say his story is impossible, and we can't say that either. There's no strong evidence it's impossible, and at least one argument, not a peer-reviewed one, says the chemistry could work. So we can't call it impossible, and we can't confirm it either. It's a legend, and nobody can check it.

## 04 Who Was Making Glass First

But what we can check is how old glass is, and it's a lot older than Pliny. People in Mesopotamia were making glass beads by the third millennium BC, which is more than four thousand years ago. The Corning Museum puts the start of glassmaking at about four thousand years ago, and the Smithsonian puts the beads at somewhere between four and five thousand years ago, so the safe way to say it is more than four thousand years ago.

And there's more. By the fourteen hundreds BC, people in Mesopotamia and in Egypt were making glass vessels, actual containers. Some were what's called core-formed, where the glass is built up around a core that's taken out afterward, and some were cast, which means shaped in a mold. The Metropolitan Museum of Art dates those to the fourteen hundreds BC, and the Smithsonian describes glassmaking really taking off in what's called the Late Bronze Age, which is that same stretch of history.

So glass beads were being made more than two thousand years before Pliny wrote his story, and glass vessels were being made well over a thousand years before he wrote it, right?

## 05 Fair Credit to Pliny

Now, to be fair to Pliny, he wasn't claiming to have invented anything. He wrote down a story about how glass started, and he wrote it in the first century AD, a very long way from when glass actually started. And his is the version that got remembered, it's the one the Corning Museum still tells, even while they tell you it's a legend.

So Pliny gets credit for the story, and it's a good one. What the story doesn't get is the evidence, because nobody can check it, and the glass that people actually made comes from Mesopotamia and Egypt, long before his merchants ever landed on that beach.

## 06 Who Did It First

So here's how it lines up. Pliny the Elder, in the first century AD, said merchants discovered glass by accident on a beach near the Belus river, in Phoenicia. The Corning Museum of Glass says nobody really knows how glass was first made. And people in Mesopotamia were making glass beads more than four thousand years ago, and people in Mesopotamia and Egypt were making glass vessels by the fourteen hundreds BC.

We don't know their names, but they were making glass long before anybody told the story. Somebody did it first, and that's what this channel is about.
