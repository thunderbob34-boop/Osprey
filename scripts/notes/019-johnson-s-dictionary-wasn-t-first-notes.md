## Re-check A (2026-10-02)
- Searched: none. The session-wide web search budget was exhausted before this script was reached, so everything below was checked against the research entry and standard references only.
- Fixes: section 05 and section 07, Cockeram. "The first person to actually call his book a dictionary was Henry Cockeram, in 1623" was overstated: bilingual books were already called dictionaries, e.g. Sir Thomas Elyot's Latin-English "The Dictionary of Syr Thomas Eliot" (1538). Cockeram's The English Dictionarie (1623) is the first English to English one with the word in the title, which is what the LMH write-up means in context. Now "the first to call one of these English to English books a dictionary" (section 05, plus "the first English to English one to use the word") and "the first to call an English to English book a dictionary" (section 07). Word count 840 → 851; hedge list updated.
- Caveats noted, no change: (1) the only known copy of the 1604 first edition is generally reported to be at the Bodleian Library, Oxford; the Folger record is probably a later edition. "A surviving copy of it at the Folger" is still true of the book, but if a title page goes on screen, label its edition. (2) Cawdrey's Oakham schoolmastering is dated earlier than 1580 in some accounts (he was rector of South Luffenham from 1571); "around 1580" comes from the Britannica-based entry and couldn't be search-checked, so worth a look in the triple check.
- Not search-verified (checked against research/knowledge only): A Table Alphabeticall 1604, first monolingual English dictionary (BL); 151-year gap; about 3,000 hard words (range 2,500-3,000); "Ladies, Gentlewomen, or any other vnskilfull persons" title; Johnson 1755, over 40,000 words; Cawdrey drew on earlier school texts; bilingual lists before 1604.
