# Notes: Atari Didn't Invent the Game Cartridge: Jerry Lawson's Channel F
Research entry: 100-episode-lineup.md, section "## History Channel-inspired additions (101–114)", entry "### 112."

| Claim in script | Source(s) named in the research entry |
|---|---|
| Atari 2600 (1977) widely remembered as the console that brought the plug-in cartridge to living rooms | Entry "Credited" line |
| Fairchild Channel F released Nov 1976; first home console with a microprocessor and interchangeable cartridges holding game software in a ROM chip | Smithsonian Magazine; The Strong National Museum of Play; Computer History Museum (extract) |
| Jerry Lawson (1940-2011) led the Fairchild engineering team and is credited with developing it | Entry "Actually first" line; The Strong (Lawson blog and papers) |
| Cartridges loaded in much the same way as an 8-track tape | Smithsonian Magazine; The Strong archives (Videocart brochure) |
| The Strong holds Lawson's papers | The Strong archives, Lawson papers finding aid |
| Smithsonian Magazine says Atari 2600 eclipsed the Channel F | Smithsonian Magazine |

| Not saying Atari copied Fairchild; unknown whether Atari drew on Channel F | Entry hedges and "Unknown" line |
| Smithsonian Magazine: Lawson built his own coin-operated arcade game using a Fairchild microprocessor; when bosses found out they tasked him with creating a gaming division | Smithsonian Magazine (seen in search extract); The Strong (Lawson blog, extract says management asked him for a console prototype after discovering his coin-op game) |
| 1974: Alpex developed a prototype running games off portable microprocessors in protective boxes; could not commercialize it, pitched it to Fairchild | Smithsonian Magazine (extract) |
| In 1976 Lawson led a team of engineers who took an Alpex prototype and developed it into the Channel F | The Strong National Museum of Play (extract, Lawson/Video Soft blog results) |
| 1976 user's manual in The Strong archive calls it the Fairchild Video Entertainment System | The Strong archives item 11575 (title); CHM page titled "Channel F Video Entertainment System" |
| Lawson left Fairchild and in 1980 founded Video Soft, which made game cartridges for the Atari 2600 and other systems | The Strong (Video Soft blog extract); Smithsonian Magazine / search extract (games for Atari systems) |
| In 2011 the International Game Developers Association honored Lawson, and he died that year | Smithsonian Magazine extract (IGDA recognition 2011); The Strong (IGDA, died April 2011) |

Hedges kept: "took an Alpex prototype", "went on to eclipse" (Smithsonian Magazine wording), "tasked him with creating a gaming division" (attributed to Smithsonian Magazine). Earlier: "widely remembered", "the first home console with a microprocessor and game cartridges holding the software on a ROM chip", "led the team", "is credited with", "in much the same way as", "as the Smithsonian describes it", "went on to eclipse".
Left out on purpose (unverified / keep off air in research): the "yellow" cartridge colour (Smithsonian Magazine extract only, entry says omit if uncertain); the reason Atari eclipsed it (marked inferred); the spring-loaded door detail (source unclear); calling Lawson the sole inventor; any claim Atari copied Fairchild; "every later console followed this design" (not in the confirmed list).

## Deepening (2026-10-02)
- New facts added: (1) Lawson's own coin-op arcade game on a Fairchild microprocessor led his bosses to task him with a gaming division (Smithsonian Magazine; The Strong). (2) Alpex 1974 prototype pitched to Fairchild, and Lawson's team took an Alpex prototype and developed it into the Channel F (Smithsonian Magazine; The Strong). This resolves the entry's "Alpex origin not re-verified" note, and it is why the script says "led the team". (3) 1976 manual calls it the Fairchild Video Entertainment System (The Strong archives; CHM title). (4) Video Soft, 1980, cartridges for the Atari 2600 and other systems (The Strong; Smithsonian Magazine extract). (5) IGDA recognition in 2011 and death in 2011 (Smithsonian Magazine; The Strong).
- Looked for but not usable: "yellow" cartridge and spring-loaded door (Smithsonian Magazine extract only this pass; kept off air); Demolition Derby name, early 1975 date, F8 chip, pizza parlor test, "among the earliest microprocessor-driven games" (appeared in extracts without a clear page-level source; left out); Lawson's childhood in Queens, TV and radio repair, ham radio (page-level source not isolated; left out); Video Soft as "first Black-owned video game company" (Strong only; left out); Atari VCS renamed 2600 in 1982 (one Strong extract; left out); Stanford mentoring (one extract); school named for him in 2012 (one extract). Note that this pass saw only search extracts, not full pages.
