## 01 Cold Open

The fax machine was invented before the telephone. Not a little before, either, the first fax patent is from 1843, and by 1865 there was a commercial fax service running in France, and that's about a decade before anybody made a phone call. Most of us think of the fax as an office machine, a 20th-century thing, and it's a lot older than that.

## 02 The Office Machine

So here's the picture most people have. The fax is an office machine, the thing that prints out a page somebody sent you, and it feels like it belongs to the same era as the photocopier and the desk phone. If you had to guess, you'd probably guess it came after the telephone, because it seems to need a phone line to work, right?

And that's the reason the picture sticks. We think of the fax as a phone-line gadget, so we assume the phone had to come first, and then somebody figured out how to send a page down it. But the idea of sending a picture of a page down a wire is older than the phone, and the first man to patent it was a clockmaker.

## 03 Alexander Bain, 1843

Now here's the part that matters. Alexander Bain was a Scottish clockmaker from Caithness, and on May 27th, 1843, he got a British patent for a device that could reproduce images and writing over wires. So not just a signal, but the actual marks on a page, written or drawn, copied at the other end of a wire.

And he kept at it. Bain's invention later got a US patent too, number 5,957, on December 5th, 1848. Britannica counts him as a pioneer of the facsimile, and facsimile is the long word that fax is short for, an exact copy. So the man who patented the idea of sending a copy of a page down a wire was a clockmaker in the 1840s, and that's 33 years before the telephone patent.

## 04 How It Was Supposed To Work

And it makes sense that it was a clockmaker, because the hard part of sending a page down a wire is timing. The machine at the sending end goes over the page bit by bit, and the machine at the receiving end has to put each mark in the same spot at the same moment, or you just get a smear. So both ends have to stay in step, and Bain's machine used pendulums to keep the sender and the receiver in step with each other.

Now, to be fair, I want to be careful about how much credit to hand him. Bain patented the idea and built experimental apparatus, but his machine never reached the market, it was never sold. And we don't really know how well his prototype actually worked in practice. So he's the first to patent it, and his machine used pendulums to keep the two ends in step, but he's not the one who turned it into a service people paid for.

## 05 Caselli, 1865

That was Caselli. The first commercial fax service started in France in 1865, and it used a machine called Caselli's pantelegraph. So a little over twenty years after Bain's patent, somebody had taken the same basic idea and turned it into something people could actually use, sending pages over the wires for paying customers.

And there's more, because it wasn't a one-off. There's a whole IEEE history paper on the Caselli pantelegraph and its successors, covering 1859 to 1871, so the pantelegraph wasn't just one machine, it had successors. And if you want the full story of the fax, a historian named Jonathan Coopersmith wrote a book called Faxed, The Rise and Fall of the Fax Machine. Rise and fall, right? So the fax had a whole life, start to finish, and it started a lot earlier than people think.

## 06 Then The Telephone

So line it up, Bain's patent is 1843, his US patent is 1848, Caselli's commercial service in France is running by 1865, and Alexander Graham Bell's telephone patent is 1876. That's about a decade after the first fax service, and 33 years after the first fax patent.

So the fax didn't come after the telephone, it came first, and by a lot. The idea of sending a copy of a page down a wire was patented, and then sold as a service, years before Bell's telephone patent.

## 07 The Plain Fact

So here's the plain fact. Alexander Bain, a Scottish clockmaker from Caithness, patented a way to reproduce images and writing over wires on May 27th, 1843, using pendulums to keep both ends in step, and his machine never reached the market. Caselli's pantelegraph was running a commercial fax service in France by 1865, about a decade before anybody made a phone call. The office fax most of us remember came a long time after that. Somebody did it first, and it was a clockmaker, back in 1843.
