## 01 Cold Open

The trebuchet is about the most medieval-looking weapon there is, the giant wooden throwing machine outside a castle wall, and most people picture it as something medieval Europe came up with. But people in China were hauling trebuchets with teams on ropes more than a thousand years before that picture. And here's the twist, the big version everybody pictures, the one with the heavy weight, didn't come from western Europe or from China, and it ended up traveling back to China.

## 02 The Castle Picture

So here's the version most people have in their heads. A trebuchet is the icon of the medieval European siege, a big wooden frame with a long beam on a pivot, and it throws stones at castle walls. If you've seen it in a movie or a museum, it was probably outside a castle somewhere in Europe.

And the idea behind it is just a lever, right? You have a long beam on a pivot, and you pull down hard and fast on the short end, and the long end whips up and throws whatever's on it. The only question is what does the pulling, and that question is the whole story, because the first trebuchets and the famous ones don't get pulled down the same way.

## 03 China, With Ropes

Now here's the part that matters. The trebuchet started in China, somewhere between the 5th and 3rd centuries BCE, and it was what's called a traction machine. That means the pulling was done by people, a team of people hauling down on ropes all at once, and their pull is what threw the stone.

And from China the idea spread across Eurasia, and it reached the Mediterranean by the 6th century CE. So by the time trebuchets were in the Mediterranean world, they'd already been around in China for centuries. Think about that for a second, the machine we think of as a symbol of medieval Europe was already old by the time it showed up anywhere near there, and it got there because people carried the idea a very long way.

## 04 The Counterweight

But that's still not the trebuchet most people picture. The one you see in movies doesn't have a crowd on ropes, it has a huge weight on the short end of the beam, and when that weight drops, the long end flies up. That's the counterweight trebuchet, and it came about 1,500 years after the first trebuchets, in the 12th century.

And it didn't come from China, and it didn't come from western Europe either. It came from the Byzantine and Islamic world, around the eastern Mediterranean and the Middle East. The earliest written record of it comes from a Byzantine historian named Niketas Choniates. A study on where it came from, by Paul Chevedden in the Dumbarton Oaks Papers, is titled The Invention of the Counterweight Trebuchet, A Study in Cultural Diffusion, and cultural diffusion just means an idea moving from one people to another, which is what this whole story is. And historians have also written about in-between designs, what they call transitional trebuchets, so it wasn't one clean jump from the rope kind to the weight kind.

Now, to be fair to the medieval picture, the counterweight trebuchet really is a medieval invention, it's 12th century, and that's the machine people are thinking of when they say trebuchet. So the medieval part isn't wrong, it's the European part, and the idea that it all started there, that's the part that doesn't hold up.

## 05 Back To China

And there's more, because the trebuchet started in China, it spread west, and somewhere in the Byzantine and Islamic world it got its big upgrade, the counterweight. And then that upgrade went back east. China only got the counterweight trebuchet in the 1270s, when Muslim engineers built them for Kublai Khan's siege of Xiangyang.

So the idea left China as a machine pulled by people on ropes, and it came back, many centuries later, as a machine powered by a falling weight, built by Muslim engineers. That's a two-way story, right? China gets the credit for the trebuchet, and the Byzantine and Islamic world gets the credit for the counterweight, and each one handed something to the other.

## 06 The Plain Fact

So here's the plain fact, the trebuchet started in China between the 5th and 3rd centuries BCE, pulled by teams of people on ropes, and it reached the Mediterranean by the 6th century CE. The counterweight version came about 1,500 years after the first one, in the 12th century, from the Byzantine and Islamic world, and it got back to China in the 1270s for Kublai Khan's siege of Xiangyang. The castle picture is the famous one, and it's not wrong about the century. Somebody did it first, and for the trebuchet itself, that was China, a very long time before the castles.
