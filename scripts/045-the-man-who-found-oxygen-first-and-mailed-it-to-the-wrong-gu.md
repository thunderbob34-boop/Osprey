## 01 Cold Open

The man who discovered oxygen first lost the credit because his book was slow to come out, and because his most important letter went unanswered. His name was Carl Wilhelm Scheele, and he made oxygen two or three years before Priestley did, and on September 30th, 1774, he wrote to the chemist Antoine Lavoisier and explained how to make it. As far as anyone knows, he never got an answer.

## 02 The Famous Version

If you learned who discovered oxygen in school, you probably learned one of two names. The first is Joseph Priestley, who made the gas on August 1st, 1774, and called it dephlogisticated air. Back then chemists thought burning things gave off a substance they called phlogiston, so that name made sense to them at the time. And Priestley holds the official credit, the American Chemical Society named his discovery of oxygen a National Historic Chemical Landmark.

The second name is Lavoisier, who's the one who named it oxygen and explained what it actually was. So between the two of them, Priestley made it and published it, and Lavoisier named it and explained it, and that's the story that stuck. But neither of them was the first to make it.

## 03 Fire Air

Carl Wilhelm Scheele was a Swedish apothecary, basically a pharmacist, and he made oxygen around 1771 to 1772. Sources differ on the exact year, so I'm giving you the range, but either way that's two to three years before Priestley. Scheele called it fire air, which is a pretty good name for it, because it's the part of the air that keeps a fire going.

The reason he doesn't get the credit is that he didn't get it into print in time, because Scheele's account didn't come out until his book was published in 1777, and Priestley had his in print by 1775. So Priestley made it second and published it first, and in science, publishing first is usually how you get your name on something.

And think about the names for a second, right? Scheele called it fire air, Priestley called it dephlogisticated air, and Lavoisier called it oxygen. That's one gas with three names, and the name that stuck came from the one man of the three who wasn't first to make it and wasn't first to publish it.

## 04 The Letter

Now here's the part that matters. On September 30th, 1774, Scheele wrote a letter to Lavoisier, and that's about two months after Priestley made the gas and three years before Scheele's book came out. And in that letter Scheele explained how to make the new gas, using a method with silver carbonate, so he basically handed Lavoisier the recipe.

And as far as anyone knows, Lavoisier never answered, there's no record he replied, and there's no record he credited it. The letter itself was thought to be lost, until a French chemist found it in the archives of the French Academy of Sciences, in Paris, and published it in 1890, and a draft of it is kept at the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences.

Now, to be fair to Lavoisier, we don't know whether he read the letter, and we don't know whether he did anything with it. Nobody can show that he took the idea, and I'm not going to say he did. All we can say is that the letter was sent, and there's no record he replied or credited it, right? And that's the whole story of the letter, and it's still a pretty rough one for Scheele.

## 05 Fair Credit All Around

And to be fair to Priestley and Lavoisier both, what they did was real. Priestley made the gas and got it into print first, and Lavoisier gave it the name oxygen and explained what it was, and that's the name all of us use today. Those are real contributions, and they're not small.

These days the credit is usually shared three ways, Scheele, Priestley and Lavoisier, but on the question of who made it first, it's not just me saying Scheele. The Royal Society of Chemistry says he made it in 1771, Britannica treats him as a discoverer of oxygen, and there's peer-reviewed work backing Scheele as the one who discovered it first, right? So the honest way to put Scheele is, first to make it, last to get credit.

## 06 Who Was First

So who discovered oxygen? Carl Wilhelm Scheele, a Swedish apothecary, made it around 1771 to 1772 and called it fire air. Joseph Priestley made it on August 1st, 1774, called it dephlogisticated air, and published first. Scheele wrote to Lavoisier on September 30th, 1774, and explained how to make it, and as far as anyone knows that letter never got an answer. His book came out in 1777, and by then Priestley had already published. Somebody did it first, and it was Scheele.
