## 01 Cold Open

Elias Howe, the man famous for the sewing machine, had a patent for a zipper-like fastener more than forty years before the slide fastener the zipper story usually starts with, and more than seventy years before anybody called one a zipper. And he never marketed it, and we don't know why.

## 02 The Zipper Story

So here's the version people know, when they know one at all. The zipper usually gets credited to two men, and the first is Whitcomb Judson, who came up with what he called a clasp locker in 1893, a slide fastener for closing things up. And the second is Gideon Sundback, who made a design called the Hookless Number 2 in 1913 and 1914, and it went on sale in 1914 and got its patent three years later, in 1917. Sundback's design is the one that went on sale, and his patent model is at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History.

And those two really are the zipper story, right? Judson had his slide fastener and Sundback's design is the one people could buy. But there's a much more famous name that shows up decades before either of them.

## 03 Elias Howe, 1851

Now here's the part that matters. Elias Howe is the famous sewing-machine inventor, and in 1851 he got US Patent number 8,540. On the patent itself it's titled Improvement in Fastenings for Garments, but it's popularly called the Automatic Continuous Clothing Closure. So that's 1851, which is more than forty years before Judson's clasp locker, and more than sixty years before Sundback.

And he never marketed it, the patent was granted, and as far as the sources go, that's where it ended. Now, it's tempting to come up with a reason, but we don't know why he never pursued it, and I'm not going to guess. All we know is that it was never marketed. And this isn't some internet rumor, the patent is on record, and the Smithsonian Libraries tell the Howe part of the story in their own history of the zipper, which they titled The Up and Down History of the Zipper.

## 04 Not Quite A Zipper

Now, to be fair, I have to be careful here, because Howe's fastener was not a modern zipper. A zipper works with interlocking teeth, two rows that lock together when the slider runs up them. Howe's device worked with clasps on a cord, not interlocking teeth. So it's an early zipper-like closure, a continuous way of fastening a garment, but if you put it next to the zipper on your jacket you wouldn't call them the same thing.

And that's why Judson still matters. Judson's 1893 slide fastener is the closer ancestor of the zipper we actually use. So the fair way to say it is that Howe got a patent on the idea of a continuous clothing closure decades early, and then Judson and Sundback are the ones who built the thing that turned into the zipper.

## 05 Fair Credit

So, to be fair to Judson and Sundback, they earned the credit. Judson had his slide fastener in 1893, and Sundback's Hookless Number 2 is the design that went on sale in 1914, and his patent model is in the Smithsonian's collection.

And Howe, the guy with the famous name, got there first with the idea of a continuous fastener for clothes, and then walked away from it. That's what makes it a strange little story, right? The name everybody knows from sewing machines had something in this direction, more than forty years early, and never did anything with it.

## 06 The Name

And there's more, because even after Sundback, nobody called it a zipper. That name came in 1923, when B.F. Goodrich put the fastener on rubber overshoes, and a man named B.G. Work at Goodrich gave it the name zipper. The Smithsonian has one of the original B.F. Goodrich zipper boots. So the idea had been patented in one form or another since 1851, it had been on sale since 1914, and it didn't get the name everybody uses until a rubber boot in 1923.

## 07 The Plain Fact

So here's the plain fact, Gideon Sundback made the Hookless Number 2, and it went on sale in 1914, and Whitcomb Judson had a slide fastener before him, in 1893. But in 1851, Elias Howe, the famous sewing-machine inventor, got US Patent 8,540 for an early zipper-like closure that worked with clasps on a cord, and he never marketed it. Then in 1923, B.F. Goodrich put the fastener on overshoes and called it a zipper. Somebody did it first, and in this case it was Elias Howe, with a zipper-like closure, back in 1851.
