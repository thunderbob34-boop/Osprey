## 01 Cold Open

Ask a lot of people who was first across the Atlantic by air and they'll say Charles Lindbergh, in 1927, but about eight years before that, in June of 1919, two men named John Alcock and Arthur Whitten Brown flew across it nonstop, and each of them carried a toy cat mascot. Now, to be fair to Lindbergh, his record is a different one, and it's a real one, and we'll get to it. But the first nonstop crossing came in 1919, and so did the first crossing by air of any kind, with stops.

## 02 What Lindbergh Did

Lindbergh made his flight on May 20th and 21st, 1927, according to the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum, flying solo and nonstop from Long Island to Paris, about thirty-six hundred and ten miles, in about thirty-three and a half hours. That's his actual record, the first SOLO nonstop crossing of the Atlantic.

He was going after the Orteig Prize, twenty-five thousand dollars offered by a New York hotel owner named Raymond Orteig for a nonstop flight between New York and Paris, and the Smithsonian says that prize was offered back in 1919. Lindbergh ordered his airplane from Ryan Airlines in San Diego in February of 1927, and the work on it was finished late in April. He landed at Le Bourget to a crowd of about a hundred thousand people, according to the Smithsonian, and he won the prize.

## 03 Nungesser and Coli

Oh, and one thing from that same spring. On May 8th, 1927, two French aviators, Nungesser and Coli, took off from Le Bourget in a plane called The White Bird, flying from Paris to New York, and they disappeared, and they were last sighted over Ireland. That was about two weeks before Lindbergh landed in Paris.

## 04 The Navy's NC-4

Now back to 1919. The first aircraft to fly across the Atlantic at all, with stops, was the US Navy's NC-4 flying boat. Three Navy flying boats, NC-1, NC-3 and NC-4, set out from Rockaway, New York, on May 8th, 1919, and NC-4 went across by stages, by way of Newfoundland, the Azores, and Lisbon, and on to England. Its crew was six men, with Lieutenant Commander Albert Read as commanding officer and navigator and Lieutenant Elmer Stone of the Coast Guard as pilot.

The other two didn't make it. NC-1 and NC-3 both made forced landings in clouds and rain, NC-1's crew was rescued by a Greek freighter, and the crew of NC-3 sailed two hundred and five miles to the Azores. So only one of the Navy's NC flying boats finished the whole trip, and that was NC-4, which landed at Horta in the Azores on May 17th, reached Lisbon on May 27th, stopped at Ferrol in Spain, and arrived at Plymouth, England, on May 31st, 1919.

## 05 Alcock and Brown

Then on June 14th and 15th, 1919, John Alcock and Arthur Whitten Brown flew from St. John's, Newfoundland, to a bog near Clifden, Ireland, in a Vickers Vimy biplane, and that was the first nonstop crossing. Sources differ a little on the flight time, so I'll say about sixteen hours, right? Much of the flight was in fog, and they had bad weather and mechanical trouble along the way, and when they landed the ground was soft and it tipped the plane onto its nose.

Both of these men had been through the First World War, and both of them had been prisoners of war. Alcock was forced down into the sea near Suvla Bay in September of 1917, and he and his crew of two were captured by the Turks, and he stayed a prisoner until the war ended. Brown was an observer with Number 2 Squadron of the Royal Flying Corps, he was shot down twice, he was captured on November 10th, 1915, and he was sent home in 1917.

## 06 What Came Next

They won the Daily Mail's ten thousand pound prize, and King George V knighted both of them. But Britannica notes that Alcock died a few months later, on December 18th, 1919, in a flying accident at Rouen in France, while he was flying a Vickers Viking amphibian to the Paris Air Show, and Brown lived until 1948.

## 07 Two Toy Cats

Each of them carried a toy cat mascot on the flight, and I do mean toys, not live animals. Alcock's was named Lucky Jim, and the Science Museum Group has a Lucky Jim toy cat mascot in its collection, listed as Alcock's mascot on the 1919 flight. Brown's was named Twinkletoes, and the RAF Museum has Twinkletoes in its collection, so both cats survive in museums today.

## 08 Close

So Lindbergh's record is the first solo nonstop crossing, in May of 1927. The first aircraft across the Atlantic at all was the Navy's NC-4 in May of 1919, with stops along the way, and the first nonstop crossing was Alcock and Brown in June of 1919, so somebody did it first.
