## 01 Cold Open

The oldest recording of a human voice that you can actually make out was made in 1860, and nobody heard it until 2008. It was made seventeen years before Thomas Edison's phonograph, and then it just sat there for almost a hundred and fifty years, because the machine that made it was never built to play anything back. Ask most people who invented sound recording and they'll tell you Edison, 1877, and Edison really did do something nobody had done before, and we'll give him full credit for exactly what that was. But he wasn't the first person to record sound, and he wasn't even the first person to work out how to play a recording back.

## 02 The Famous Version

The version most of us got goes like this, in 1877 Thomas Edison builds the phonograph, a machine that records sound and plays it back, and that's the birth of recorded sound. And it stuck because it's a clean story, one famous inventor, one famous machine, one year. It also stuck because for most of us recording sound and playing it back are the same idea, you don't record something unless you want to hear it again, right? But the man who made the very first recordings didn't think that way at all, because he wasn't trying to hear anything.

## 03 Scott and the Phonautograph

His name was Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville, he was a typesetter in Paris, and in 1857 he patented a machine called the phonautograph. What it did was trace sound, sound went into the machine and it drew the sound waves as wavy lines in a layer of soot, on paper or on glass. So what you got out of it was a picture of a sound, a squiggle on a page, right?

Now here's the part that matters, Scott never meant for anybody to play those squiggles back, they were for looking at, and as far as he was concerned the job was done once the line was on the paper.

Oh, one small thing on that date, if you go looking you'll find two days for the patent application, the First Sounds team, who we'll get to in a minute, say it was deposited on March 24th, 1857, and other sources say the 25th, so we'll just say 1857.

And then on April 9th, 1860, one of those tracings caught a voice singing the French song Au Clair de la Lune. Nobody could hear it, nobody was meant to hear it, it was just one more squiggle in soot.

## 04 Played Back in 2008

For almost a hundred and fifty years, that's all it was. Then in March 2008, the First Sounds team, working with two scientists at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Carl Haber and Earl Cornell, took that 1860 tracing and turned it into sound. The way they did it is sometimes called a virtual stylus, a stylus being the needle on a record player. They scanned the tracing as an image, and instead of a real needle riding along the line, a computer followed the wavy line and turned it back into sound.

And out came a voice singing Au Clair de la Lune, recorded on April 9th, 1860. That's the oldest recognizable recording of a human voice, and it was made seventeen years before Edison's phonograph, and the patent for the machine that made it was twenty years before Edison.

## 05 It Was Playing Too Fast

And there's more, because the first time people heard it, they heard it wrong. When it was released in 2008 the voice sounded like a woman singing, but to play one of these tracings at the right speed, you need to know how fast it was moving when it was made, and the reference they'd used for that had been misread, and that misreading doubled the playback speed.

So in 2009 they corrected it, and slowed down to the right speed it sounds like a different recording, it's roughly twenty seconds of a man singing slowly, possibly Scott himself. And I want to keep that word, possibly, because nobody can say for sure whose voice it is, but the voice that people heard in 2008 as a woman turned out to be a man, singing at half the speed everybody first heard.

## 06 Cros and Edison

Now, to be fair to Edison, there's a real first in his story, and it's an important one. Scott's machine recorded sound, but it couldn't play it back. Edison's phonograph did both, it recorded sound and then it played it back, in 1877, and that's the thing he gets credit for and that's the thing he deserves credit for.

But even the playback idea has somebody ahead of Edison. On April 30th, 1877, a Frenchman named Charles Cros deposited a sealed packet at the French Academy of Sciences, the Académie des sciences. The French call that a pli cacheté, and it's basically a sealed, dated envelope, so if anybody argues later about who thought of something first, there's a record. And what was inside Cros's packet was a design for playing back sound from traced sound waves, so not just drawing the sound but getting it back out again, and later on it got called the paléophone.

The catch is Cros never built it, right? He didn't have the resources to make a working machine, so he had the idea on paper, and Edison's phonograph came along later that same year, and Edison had one that worked.

## 07 Who Did It First

So here's how it actually lines up. Scott patented a machine that recorded sound in 1857, and in 1860 it recorded a man, possibly Scott himself, singing Au Clair de la Lune. Cros wrote down a way to play recordings back in April 1877. And Edison built the first machine that did both, later in 1877.

None of that takes anything away from what Edison built, but somebody did it first, and the first recorded voice we can make out isn't his, and nobody heard it until 2008, a hundred and forty-eight years after it was made.
