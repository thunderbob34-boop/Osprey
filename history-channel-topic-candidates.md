# History Channel Topic Map: 24 New Candidates for "Somebody Did It First"

**Status 2026-10-02:** the top 15 (plus reserve Walter Reed/Carlos Finlay) were researched to the two-source standard. 14 passed and are now episodes 101–114 in `100-episode-lineup.md`, with full entries there. Fritos needs a second strong source. Steamboat Willie was cut, because whether it was first depends on how you define sound.

## What this is

The owner suggested using the History Channel "That Built" shows as a topic map. That is all they were used for here. I read episode titles and one-line synopses from TV listing pages to see which people, companies and products the shows cover. I took no facts, footage, quotes or framing from the shows. Every "who was first" claim below was checked (or marked as unchecked) against museums, journals, Britannica, Smithsonian, national libraries, patents and similar. Wikipedia, IMDb and TV listing sites were never used as a source for a fact.

Shows mapped: The Men Who Built America (and Frontiersmen), The Food That Built America, The Toys That Built America, The Machines That Built America, The Titans That Built America, The Engineering That Built the World, The Mega-Brands That Built America, How Disney Built America, The Icons That Built America, and Hazardous History with Henry Winkler.

Every candidate was checked against `100-episode-lineup.md` (main table and Reserve list) and `episode-research.md` (Job 4 and the cut list). None is in the lineup or reserves, and none is the same story under another name. Video games before Pong, the telephone, Tesla and Marconi, TV, Edison, the assembly line, and Coca-Cola are already covered, so they were skipped.

## How to read the ratings

- **Confidence** is how likely the "first" claim is to survive a pedantic historian. High = clean, dated, documented. Medium = true, but "first" depends on a definition or the credit is shared. Low = contested or thinly documented.
- **Appeal** is my 1 to 5 judgment of how well the story would hold a general YouTube audience. It is a judgment, not a measurement.
- **Score** = confidence (High 3, Medium 2, Low 1) times appeal. The table is ranked by score.
- **Search status** says how much checking was done. "Searched" means I found the named sources in search results. "Not searched" means the search budget ran out, so the claim and the expected sources come from background knowledge and must be confirmed before anyone scripts it. All of these still need the channel's normal two-strong-source check. The search tool returns extracts, not full pages.
- **Myth flag**: "Yes" means the "somebody did it first" angle has a popular-myth or over-correction risk that the script must avoid.

## Myth watch (read before picking)

We must not swap one myth for another. These need the most care:

1. **Eiffel (3).** "Eiffel stole it" is not what the record shows. His engineers worked for his firm, and the official tower site says the patent was registered in Eiffel's name in 1884. Say "the idea began with two of his engineers," not "he stole it."
2. **Oreo (4).** Hydrox being first is solid. "Oreo copied Hydrox" is an inference. Use "came four years later," not "ripped off."
3. **Fitch (6).** Fitch ran a steamboat service first. There is no documented proof that Fulton copied him. Fulton's Clermont was the commercial success. Don't say "Fulton stole Fitch's idea."
4. **Steamboat Willie (11).** "First sound cartoon" depends on what counts: sound-on-film, fully synchronized, or sound added later. Name the definition on air.
5. **Golden Gate (12).** Strauss was a real chief engineer and the project's driving force. Leon Moisseiff and architect Irving Morrow also mattered. The claim is "Ellis's design work was left out," not "Strauss did nothing."
6. **Mickey (13).** Walt supplied the idea and the voice, and Iwerks drew the character. Britannica says Mickey was created by both. Don't say "Walt had nothing to do with it."
7. **Boone (18).** "Boone discovered Cumberland Gap" is the popular myth, but Native Americans used the route for generations. Say "first documented European passage," not "first person."
8. **Finlay (19).** "Reed stole Finlay's credit" is a popular over-correction. Reed's commission did the experimental proof, and Finlay's 1881 contribution was proposing the mosquito vector.
9. **Cabbage Patch (23).** The Martha Nelson Thomas story is widely told and the sources I know of are mostly press. It may be a real priority fight or a one-sided retelling. Do not script it without strong sources.
10. **Disneyland (24).** "First theme park" is Disney's own marketing line and the real answer turns on the definition of "theme park."

## Candidate details

Candidates are in rank order.

### 1. Barbie Had an Older Sister: Bild Lilli
- **Inspired by:** The Toys That Built America, Season 1, Episode 2, "Clash of the Toy Titans" (Mattel, Barbie, G.I. Joe).
- **Credited:** Ruth Handler and Mattel, who launched Barbie in 1959.
- **Who was first:** The Bild Lilli doll. It came from a cartoon character in the West German newspaper Bild-Zeitung, was sold as a plastic doll in 1955, and was meant as a gag gift for adults before children took to it. Handler saw a Lilli on a 1956 trip to Europe. Mattel faced a copyright and patent challenge from Lilli's German makers, settled it in 1963, and bought the Lilli copyright and patents in 1964 (the 1963 and 1964 detail came from a search extract with no clear source, so confirm it).
- **Sources:** Found in search: Britannica, "Bild Lilli" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Bild-Lilli); Library of Congress, "Ruth Handler, Entrepreneur, Introduces the Barbie Doll" (https://guides.loc.gov/this-month-in-business-history/march/ruth-handler-introduces-barbie); The Strong National Museum of Play, "Barbie, You're Beautiful" (https://www.museumofplay.org/blog/barbie-youre-beautiful/); Smithsonian NMAH Barbie doll object (https://www.si.edu/object/nmah_1155897).
- **Confidence:** High. Four strong sources agree on the chain of events.
- **Why it's a good video:** The best-selling doll line in history began as a newspaper-cartoon joke gift for adults, and the lawsuit that followed ended with Mattel buying the original.
- **Watch for:** Barbie was still a new design with its own career-woman marketing. Credit Handler for that.

### 2. The Tailor Who Riveted the First Blue Jeans
- **Inspired by:** The Mega-Brands That Built America, "Pants on Fire" (Levi Strauss and a tailor partner). Job 4 in `episode-research.md` had listed "Jacob Davis vs. Levi Strauss on riveted jeans" as "needs checking." It now checks out.
- **Credited:** Levi Strauss, the name on the jeans.
- **Who was first:** Jacob Davis, a Latvian-born tailor in Reno, Nevada. In December 1870 he reinforced canvas work pants with copper rivets for a customer. He then partnered with Strauss, who supplied the cloth and the business backing, and they filed the riveted-trousers patent in May 1873.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Smithsonian Magazine, "After 150 Years, Levi's 501 Blue Jeans Are Still Kicking" (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/levis-jeans-150-years-180982241/); Smithsonian Magazine, "The Origin of Blue Jeans" (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/the-origin-of-blue-jeans-89612175/); Britannica, "Jeans" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/jeans); Smithsonian NMAH, Levi's brown duck trousers (https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_371609).
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Why it's a good video:** Everyone knows the brand and almost nobody knows the tailor whose idea it was. It also settles a very common "Levi invented jeans" belief.
- **Watch for:** Don't say Davis invented denim trousers. Say he invented the riveted version. Strauss was the business partner, not a thief.

### 3. The Eiffel Tower Started as Two Employees' Sketch
- **Inspired by:** The Engineering That Built the World, "Liberty Rising" (Eiffel built the Statue of Liberty's frame). This is an adjacent topic. I did not confirm that the show covers the tower itself.
- **Credited:** Gustave Eiffel.
- **Who was first:** Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier, chief engineers at Eiffel's firm. Koechlin's sketch dated June 6, 1884 showed a 300-metre iron pylon. They brought in architect Stephen Sauvestre to shape the look. A patent for the configuration was registered on September 18, 1884 under Eiffel's name.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Eiffel Tower official site, "How the Eiffel Tower was invented" (https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/130-years/how-was-eiffel-tower-invented), and its history page (https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/the-monument/history). Need a second independent source before scripting; expect Britannica (Eiffel Tower and Gustave Eiffel).
- **Confidence:** High on the engineers' origin of the idea. I did not confirm the common claim that Eiffel "bought" the engineers' patent rights.
- **Why it's a good video:** The most photographed monument in the world is named for the boss, not the people who sketched it.
- **Watch for:** See myth watch. Eiffel's firm took the financial and political risk and won the contest. No "stolen" language.

### 4. Oreo Was the Second Cookie: Hydrox Came First
- **Inspired by:** The Food That Built America, cookie episode (likely Season 3, "Cookie Fortunes"; not confirmed). Note that a search summary mislabeled "Baking Chocolate Chip History" as an Oreo episode. That title describes a teacher-turned-restaurateur, which points to the Toll House cookie, not Oreo.
- **Credited:** Nabisco's Oreo (1912), widely treated as the original chocolate cream sandwich cookie.
- **Who was first:** Hydrox, released in 1908, four years earlier. I did not confirm the maker in search. Background says Sunshine Biscuits (Loose-Wiles).
- **Sources:** Found in search: Britannica quiz page stating Hydrox was released in 1908, four years before Oreo (https://www.britannica.com/quiz/snack-time-quiz); Britannica, "Nabisco" (https://www.britannica.com/money/Nabisco) for the 1912 Oreo; Smithsonian Magazine, "The Factory That Oreos Built" (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/factory-oreos-built-180969121/); Smithsonian NMAH, Sunshine Biscuit Company archive (https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/archival-item/sova-nmah-ac-0059-ref10605). The quiz page is thin. A second strong source for the Hydrox date is needed.
- **Confidence:** High for "Hydrox came first." Say "first chocolate cream sandwich cookie in the US" and nothing broader.
- **Why it's a good video:** The cookie everyone calls the original was the follow-up.
- **Watch for:** "Knockoff" is not documented. Use dates and let viewers draw the conclusion.

### 5. Lewis and Clark Were Twelve Years Late
- **Inspired by:** The Men Who Built America: Frontiersmen, "Never Surrender" (Lewis and Clark's expedition).
- **Credited:** Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, whose party reached the Pacific in November 1805.
- **Who was first:** Alexander Mackenzie, a Northwest Company trader, reached the Pacific at Bella Coola on July 20, 1793. He was the first European to cross North America north of Mexico. He published his journals in 1801, and Jefferson read them in 1802 before commissioning Lewis and Clark.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Library of Congress Maps blog, "The Crossing of a Continent" (https://blogs.loc.gov/maps/2023/07/the-crossing-of-a-continent/); The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Sir Alexander Mackenzie" (https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/sir-alexander-mackenzie-explorer); National Park Service, Lewis and Clark Trail, "Little Known Fact" (https://www.nps.gov/lecl/learn/historyculture/little-known-fact-1.htm); Library of Congress, "Before Lewis & Clark" (https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/lewisandclark/lewis-before.html); Library and Archives Canada (https://www.bac-lac.gc.ca/eng/discover/exploration-settlement/pathfinders-passageways/Pages/mapping-northwest.aspx).
- **Confidence:** High, with the "European, north of Mexico" qualifier.
- **Why it's a good video:** The American national story has a Scottish-Canadian prequel that the US government's own park service mentions, and Jefferson used it as a guide.
- **Watch for:** The route Mackenzie used was already known to Indigenous peoples. Lewis and Clark still deserve credit for the first US crossing and for the science and mapping.

### 6. Fulton Wasn't First: John Fitch's Steamboat Ran in 1790
- **Inspired by:** The Men Who Built America (Vanderbilt's steamship business). Adjacent topic. I did not confirm the show covers Fulton or Fitch.
- **Credited:** Robert Fulton, whose Clermont ran in 1807.
- **Who was first:** John Fitch. A 45-foot steam craft had a trial on the Delaware River on August 22, 1787, in front of delegates to the Constitutional Convention. By 1790 a larger boat ran scheduled passenger service between Philadelphia, Bristol, Burlington and Trenton. Fitch received a US steamboat patent on August 26, 1791.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Britannica, "John Fitch" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Fitch); Library of Congress, Today in History, August 26 (https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/august-26/); National Park Service, "New Castle's Harbor" (https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/new-castle-s-harbor.htm); Smithsonian NMAH, "John Fitch's Model Steam Car" (https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1375391).
- **Confidence:** High for Fitch's dates. Careful on "first steamboat anywhere" (Jouffroy d'Abbans ran one in France in 1783, and James Rumsey had his own Potomac demonstration; both background, not searched).
- **Why it's a good video:** The man who ran the first steamboat line died poor and mostly forgotten, and the person who got the glory arrived 17 years later.
- **Watch for:** Fulton's real contribution was commercial success. No "Fulton stole it."

### 7. Lindbergh Wasn't First Across the Atlantic
- **Inspired by:** The Titans That Built America (Lindbergh is in the cast list). Adjacent topic.
- **Credited:** Charles Lindbergh, May 1927.
- **Who was first:** John Alcock and Arthur Whitten Brown flew nonstop from Newfoundland to Ireland on June 14 to 15, 1919. The US Navy's NC-4 flying boat crossed the Atlantic by air, with stops, in May 1919. Lindbergh was first to fly it solo and nonstop between New York and Paris. All dates are background knowledge.
- **Sources:** Not searched. Expect Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum (Spirit of St. Louis), the Science Museum Group (Alcock and Brown's Vickers Vimy), Britannica ("Alcock and Brown").
- **Confidence:** High. These facts are textbook, but they still need the two-source pass.
- **Why it's a good video:** A national hero with a precise "first" that isn't the one most people credit him with.
- **Watch for:** Never imply Lindbergh claimed more than he did. His real record is "first solo nonstop."

### 8. Hershey Didn't Invent Milk Chocolate
- **Inspired by:** The Food That Built America, Season 2, Episode 2, "The Chocolate Rush."
- **Credited:** Milton Hershey, who launched Hershey's Milk Chocolate in 1900.
- **Who was first:** Daniel Peter of Switzerland, who added dried milk to chocolate. Search sources gave 1876. Many accounts say 1875. Pick whichever Britannica states and check the other.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Britannica, "Daniel Peter" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Daniel-Peter); Hershey Community Archives, "Origins of Hershey's Milk Chocolate" (https://hersheyarchives.org/encyclopedia/origins-of-hersheys-milk-chocolate/), which itself says Hershey was not first; Britannica, "Sweet chocolate" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/sweet-chocolate). Expect a Nestlé history source too.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Why it's a good video:** Even Hershey's own archive says he wasn't first, and his real breakthrough was making it cheap enough for everyone.
- **Watch for:** Credit Hershey's real innovation: fresh milk and mass production.

### 9. Atari Didn't Invent the Game Cartridge: Jerry Lawson's Channel F
- **Inspired by:** The Toys That Built America, Season 2, "Plumber Bros vs. Hedgehog" (the console wars) and the "Video Game Visionaries" episode. Adjacent topic. Not a Pong story, so it does not overlap episode 99.
- **Credited:** The Atari 2600 (1977), usually remembered as the system that made game cartridges mainstream.
- **Who was first:** The Fairchild Channel F, released in November 1976, the first home video game system with separate programmable game cartridges. Jerry Lawson (1940 to 2011), one of the first African American video game designers, led the engineering team that turned an Alpex prototype into the Channel F and designed the cartridge mechanism.
- **Sources:** Found in search: The Strong National Museum of Play, on Jerry Lawson's career (https://www.museumofplay.org/blog/collection-documents-the-career-of-video-game-pioneer-jerry-lawson/); Smithsonian Magazine, on Lawson (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/unsung-black-developer-unlocked-code-turning-video-game-consolo-into-virtual-living-room-arcade-180988819/); The Strong's Lawson papers finding aid (https://archives.museumofplay.org/repositories/3/resources/32).
- **Confidence:** High, if worded as "first home console with ROM game cartridges."
- **Why it's a good video:** A forgotten console, a pioneering engineer, and a design choice every later console copied.
- **Watch for:** The 1972 Magnavox Odyssey used plug-in cards that changed its circuits and held no game code. Say that so a pedant can't sink the claim (Odyssey detail is background, not searched).

### 10. Kraft Didn't Invent Processed Cheese: Two Swiss Chemists
- **Inspired by:** The Food That Built America, Season 2, Episode 4, "American Cheese."
- **Credited:** James L. Kraft, whose US patent for sterilized cheese was granted in 1916.
- **Who was first:** Swiss food chemists Walter Gerber and Fritz Stettler, who used sodium citrate on Emmentaler to improve shelf life and found it melted better. One search extract dated their work to 1913. Background says 1911 to 1912. The date needs resolving.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Smithsonian Magazine, "How Boxed Mac and Cheese Became a Pantry Staple" (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/how-boxed-mac-and-cheese-became-pantry-stable-180975023/); Google Patents, US 1,186,524, Kraft's patent (https://patents.google.com/patent/US1186524A/en); Britannica, "Kraft Foods" (https://www.britannica.com/money/Kraft-Foods-Inc). Need a second source for Gerber and Stettler. Expect the Swiss patent record and a dairy-history source.
- **Confidence:** High that the Swiss work was earlier. Hedge the exact year.
- **Why it's a good video:** Two Swiss chemists solved a spoilage problem, and a Chicago salesman patented the US version.
- **Watch for:** Kraft's patent really was the first US patent for the process. Say so.

### 11. Steamboat Willie Wasn't the First Cartoon With Sound
- **Inspired by:** How Disney Built America, Season 1, Episode 1, "The Birth of Mickey." Adjacent topic. Does not overlap episode 25 (animated features).
- **Credited:** Walt Disney, with Steamboat Willie (November 1928).
- **Who was first:** Paul Terry's Dinner Time (1928) was the first sound-on-film cartoon produced in New York, and the Fleischer brothers did sound-on-film animation experiments in the mid-1920s (the Fleischer Song Car-Tunes of 1924 to 1926 were silent bouncing-ball shorts, so the sound claim depends on which Fleischer work is meant).
- **Sources:** Found in search: Library of Congress National Film Preservation Board essay on Steamboat Willie (https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-film-preservation-board/documents/steamboat_willie.pdf); Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences, "The History of Silent and Early Sound New York Animation" (https://www.oscars.org/events/history-silent-and-early-sound-new-york-animation); Britannica, "Fleischer brothers" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Fleischer-brothers).
- **Confidence:** Medium. "Fully synchronized" is the line Disney can defend.
- **Why it's a good video:** The most famous sound cartoon debut had competition, and the Fleischers get left out of the story.
- **Watch for:** Define "first" on air.

### 12. The Golden Gate Bridge's Forgotten Designer
- **Inspired by:** The Engineering That Built the World, "Battle of the Bridges" (Joseph Strauss).
- **Credited:** Joseph Strauss, chief engineer.
- **Who was first:** Charles Alton Ellis. Strauss hired him in 1921 and made him vice president in charge of bridge design. Ellis directed the thousands of calculations, stress sheets and specifications. The bridge district's own site says Strauss never officially recognized him, that Strauss later fired him, and that "the record clearly demonstrates that he deserves significant credit."
- **Sources:** Found in search: Golden Gate Bridge Highway and Transportation District, "Joseph Strauss" (https://www.goldengate.org/bridge/history-research/bridge-construction/joseph-strauss/), "Bridge Design" (https://www.goldengate.org/bridge/history-research/bridge-construction/bridge-design/), and "Engineering the Design" (https://www.goldengate.org/exhibits/engineering-the-design/); Britannica, "Golden Gate Bridge" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Golden-Gate-Bridge). Need an independent second source on Ellis (expect Smithsonian or a university). I could not confirm any 2007 recognition of Ellis, so leave that out.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The district site is official but is one source.
- **Why it's a good video:** The most famous bridge in America, and its main designer was written out of the story.
- **Watch for:** See myth watch.

### 13. The Mouse Walt Didn't Draw: Ub Iwerks
- **Inspired by:** How Disney Built America, Season 1, Episode 1, "The Birth of Mickey."
- **Credited:** Walt Disney.
- **Who was first:** Ub Iwerks drew and animated Mickey in 1928 after Disney lost Oswald the Lucky Rabbit to a distributor. Britannica says Mickey was drawn by Iwerks in the early years and was created by both men.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Britannica, "Mickey Mouse" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mickey-Mouse); Smithsonian NMAH, "Mickey Mouse turns 90" (https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/mickey-mouse-turns-90); Britannica, Today in History, May 15 (https://www.britannica.com/today-in-history/May-15-Mickey-Mouses-weird-first-draft). Both major sources already share credit, so the "Iwerks drew him" point needs a stronger source, such as the Academy or a Disney archive statement.
- **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Why it's a good video:** The face of the company was drawn by the man most people have never heard of.
- **Watch for:** See myth watch.

### 14. Fritos Started as a $100 Recipe
- **Inspired by:** The Food That Built America, "The Chips That Took Over the Snack World" (a listed episode title; coverage of Fritos not confirmed).
- **Credited:** C.E. Doolin and the Frito Company, which launched Fritos in 1932.
- **Who was first:** Gustavo Olguin, a San Antonio restaurant owner. Doolin bought his recipe, a hand-operated potato ricer, and 19 customer accounts for $100.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Texas State Historical Association, Handbook of Texas, "Doolin, Charles Elmer" (https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/doolin-charles-elmer-c-e); National Park Service timeline, "1932 Fritos Corn Chips" (https://www.nps.gov/features/shen/2019/ccc/lesson-1/timeline/1932-1.html); Smithsonian NMAH, "Snack Engineering" (https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/food/online/new-and-improved/snack-nation/snack-engineering). Need a second source that names Olguin and the $100 price.
- **Confidence:** High for the purchase. Do not say Olguin "invented the corn chip," since fried corn-dough snacks are much older.
- **Why it's a good video:** A direct example of a brand founder who bought someone else's invention.
- **Watch for:** A search for a different claim (Rebecca Webb Carranza as a tortilla-chip pioneer) found nothing, so that claim is not used.

### 15. Kodak's Film Had a Rival Inventor: Hannibal Goodwin
- **Inspired by:** The Mega-Brands That Built America (Kodak is among the brands; the episode title is not confirmed).
- **Credited:** George Eastman and Kodak, who made celluloid roll film from 1889.
- **Who was first:** Hannibal Goodwin, a clergyman in Newark. He filed his celluloid film application on May 2, 1887. US patent 610,861 was granted on September 13, 1898.
- **Sources:** Found in search: Britannica, "Hannibal Goodwin" (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hannibal-Goodwin); Library of Congress record (https://www.loc.gov/item/2014695499); Google Patents, US 610,861 (https://patents.google.com/patent/US610861A/en); Britannica, "Eastman Kodak Company" (https://www.britannica.com/money/Eastman-Kodak-Company).
