# 100 Episodes: "Somebody Did It First"

Prepared 2026-10-02. One hundred episode ideas. Each has a working title, a cold-open hook, and a fun fact verified against two or more strong sources (museums, journals, national libraries, Britannica, Smithsonian, IEEE, patents and similar; never Wikipedia or content farms).

## How this list was built

- **48 episodes were already researched:** the launch episode, the strongest 37 of the original 65 (fit 4–5, including two reworked angles), and the 10 new topics from the first pass. Their fun facts are the verified lines from `episode-research.md` and `launch-episode-fact-notes.md`.
- **52 episodes are new**, researched today. Sixty candidates went through the same two-strong-sources check:
  - Penicillin (Duchesne before Fleming) was **cut**, because a peer-reviewed paper calls that story a fable.
  - Eight others passed but were weaker or duplicative, so they sit in the **reserve list** at the bottom.
- **Method:** all checks came from search-engine extracts. This container can't open web pages directly. Every "Confirmed with hedge" entry lists, in its detail, exactly what must not be overstated on air.
- **Order:** the order below is a suggested release order. It starts with the launch, then rotates through six categories, strongest first within each, so viewers don't get six Edison-era inventors in a row.

**Category counts:** Technology & Media 22 · Food & Drink 19 · Science & Medicine 17 · Culture & Society 17 · Everyday Things 13 · Math, Space & Exploration 12.

- **Actually first:** Alexander Gordon of Aberdeen published *A Treatise on the Epidemic Puerperal Fever of Aberdeen* in 1795, based on the 1789–92 epidemic. He argued the disease was carried from patient to patient by the attendants and told doctors and midwives to wash and fumigate their clothes. Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. made the same case in Boston in 1843, cited Gordon, and published "The Contagiousness of Puerperal Fever."
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** Gordon admitted in print, "I myself was the means of carrying the infection to a great number of women." He said that just by knowing which midwife would deliver a woman, he could predict who would get sick.
- **Hook (cold-open line):** Fifty years before Semmelweis, a Scottish doctor confessed in print that he had been the one carrying a deadly fever from mother to mother.
- **Hedges needed on air:** Semmelweis's specific contribution was chlorinated handwashing with hard death-rate data showing it worked. Gordon recommended washing and fumigating clothing. Frame Gordon as "first to say doctors were spreading it," not "first to invent handwashing." Holmes reached his conclusion before Semmelweis but did not run an intervention trial.
- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: Gordon's 1795 treatise, both quotes, his washing advice, Holmes in 1843, and that Holmes cited Gordon (Royal College of Physicians of Edinburgh, Harvard's digitized treatise, ASU Embryo Project, PubMed review, University of Iowa library). Unknown: whether Semmelweis knew of either man.
- **Sources:**
  - Royal College of Physicians of Edinburgh, The History of Germ Theory in the College Collections (https://www.rcpe.ac.uk/heritage/heritage-blog/history-germ-theory-college-collections): Gordon's "I myself was the means..." quote
  - PubMed, Alexander Gordon, puerperal sepsis, and modern theories of infection control: Semmelweis in perspective (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20334850/): Gordon first to prove contagion and to advocate hygiene, 1795
  - University of Iowa Hardin Library, Alexander Gordon: puerperal fever (https://blog.lib.uiowa.edu/needtoknow/2018/09/05/alexander-gordon-puerperal-fever-september-2018-notes-from-the-john-martin-rare-book-room-hardin-library/): the "foretell" quote; half a century before Holmes and Semmelweis

Unknown: the exact date Barbier's system arrived at the Institute (1821 is the usual date).

- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: the patent title and 1970 issue date; the wooden-shell prototype; 9 Dec 1968 demo; Alto 1973; PARC visit Dec 1979. Single-source: no royalties / lump sum. Unknown: the exact amount Apple paid SRI.

- **Credited:** Statistician Stephen Stigler (Univ. of Chicago), whose 1980 "law of eponymy" says "no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer."
- **Actually first:** Sociologist Robert K. Merton had already described the pattern (his 1957 work on priority disputes). Stigler wrote his paper for a festschrift honoring Merton and openly credited him, so the law obeys itself.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed (meta/bonus episode)
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** "Stephen Stigler wrote his law for a book honoring the sociologist Robert Merton, and said the idea was really Merton's. A law saying discoveries are never named after their discoverer is named after the wrong person, on purpose."

  1. **Hubble's law → Lemaître.** In 2018 the International Astronomical Union voted to recommend calling it the "Hubble–Lemaître law." Belgian priest Georges Lemaître published the expansion in 1927, two years before Hubble (78% of 4,060 electronic votes in favor). Sources: Nature (https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-018-07234-y); Science (https://www.science.org/content/article/move-over-hubble-discovery-expanding-cosmos-assigned-little-known-belgian-astronomer).

  3. Also in this batch: the Gaussian curve (de Moivre) and Halley's Comet (Chinese records).
- **Hedges needed on air:** It's a joke "law," not a statistical finding; say "tongue-in-cheek." Historian Helge Kragh called the IAU's background notes "bad history," so frame Lemaître as "published first," not "Hubble stole it." Calling Plimpton 322 "trigonometry" is the UNSW authors' reading and is debated; say "a table of Pythagorean triples."
- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: the 1980 paper in Merton's festschrift, Stigler's crediting of Merton, the IAU 2018 vote, Plimpton 322's date and triples. Inferred: the exact Merton 1957 paper title, which comes from secondary sources only.

  - Nature and Science (above): Hubble–Lemaître
  - Smithsonian, UNSW, Britannica (above): Plimpton 322
- **Why it works:** A natural season finale or channel-trailer episode that explains why the channel exists, and the law proves itself.

### 80. Wegener's Big Idea Was 300 Years Old — Fit 4/5
- **Credited:** Alfred Wegener's continental drift (1912) is widely taught as the origin of the idea that continents move.
- **Actually first:** Abraham Ortelius, the Flemish mapmaker, suggested in his *Thesaurus Geographicus* (1596) that the Americas were "torn away from Europe and Africa" by earthquakes and floods. In 1858 Antonio Snider-Pellegrini published "before and after" maps that showed the Americas and Africa joined and then separated.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** In 1596 a mapmaker looked at the Atlantic coastlines and wrote that "the vestiges of the rupture reveal themselves" if you put a world map in front of you and look carefully at the coasts. That was more than 300 years before Wegener.
- **Hook (cold-open line):** Every kid notices that South America and Africa fit like puzzle pieces, and so did a map seller in 1596.
- **Hedges needed on air:** Ortelius and Snider-Pellegrini guessed from the shape of the coasts. Ortelius blamed earthquakes and floods. - **Credited:** In Britain, Frank Whittle is credited as the inventor of the jet engine.

Working independently, Hans von Ohain in Germany designed the HeS 3B engine that powered the Heinkel He 178 on the world's first turbojet-powered flight on 27 August 1939. The first British jet, the Gloster E.28/39, flew on 15 May 1941.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** "The first jet plane in history flew in Germany on 27 August 1939, just days before World War II began and nearly two years before Britain's first jet got off the ground."
- **Hook (cold-open line):** "The man who patented the jet engine watched someone else fly one first."
- **Hedges needed on air:** This is a two-way story. Whittle was first to patent (1930) and first to run an engine (April 1937). Von Ohain was first to fly. Present them as independent inventors, not as a case of Whittle being wrongly credited. Whittle letting his patent lapse in 1935 because he couldn't pay the £5 renewal fee appeared in a search extract without a clearly attributed strong source, so verify it before using it. The "days before WWII" line rests on the well-known 1 September 1939 invasion of Poland, which was not separately sourced this pass.
- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: He 178 flew 27 Aug 1939; Gloster E.28/39 flew 15 May 1941; Whittle's 1930 patent; Whittle's engine first ran 12 Apr 1937. Inferred: "days before WWII" (date arithmetic). Single or unattributed: the £5 patent-lapse anecdote.
- **Sources:**
  - Britannica: He 178 (https://www.britannica.com/technology/He-178): first jet flight 27 Aug 1939; Gloster E.28/39 on 15 May 1941
  - Smithsonian (How Things Fly): When did we see the first jet plane? (https://howthingsfly.si.edu/ask-an-explainer/when-did-we-see-first-jet-plane-what-plane-was-it): He 178 as first jet
  - Smithsonian NASM: Heinkel (von Ohain) HeS 3B Turbojet Engine (https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/heinkel-von-ohain-hes-3b-turbojet-engine-reproduction/nasm_A19810039000): HeS 3B powered the 27 Aug 1939 flight
  - Britannica: Sir Frank Whittle (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Frank-Whittle): 1930 patent
  - IMechE: Marking the 75th anniversary of Whittle's first jet engine (https://www.imeche.org/news/news-article/12-04-30/Marking_the_75th_anniversary_of_Whittle_s_first_jet_engine.aspx): 12 April 1937 first run
  - Britannica: Hans Joachim Pabst von Ohain (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hans-Joachim-Pabst-von-Ohain): independent conception
- **Why it works:** A real race between two inventors on opposite sides of a world war, with a precise date for the first flight.

### Da Vinci Never Drew a Bicycle — Fit 3/5
- **Credited:** Leonardo da Vinci. A bicycle sketch "found" in his Codex Atlanticus in the 1970s led to claims that he designed a bike around 1490–93.
- **Actually first:** Karl Drais rode his two-wheeled, foot-pushed "Laufmaschine" (running machine) on June 12, 1817, starting from Mannheim. The sketch has been called a modern forgery added during the Codex's 20th-century restoration.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge (borderline: the forgery side needs another strong source)
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** The first bicycle had no pedals. On his first ride in 1817, Karl Drais pushed it along with his feet for roughly 14 kilometers, about 9 miles, out of Mannheim.
- **Hook (cold-open line):** "There's a famous da Vinci bicycle sketch. The problem is that it was drawn in pencil about 450 years after he died."
- **Hedges needed on air:** Britannica says the drawing "was discovered to be a forgery added in the 1960s." The main scholarly case (Hans-Erhard Lessing, 1997 cycling-history conference) dates it to about 1967–74. Italian scholars led by Augusto Marinoni defended it as authentic, so say "most historians now consider it a fake." The pencil and graphite-date argument came only from non-strong pages (pryordodge.com, capovelo), so don't use it as fact without a better source. **The cold-open hook above relies on it; replace it if it can't be sourced.** Drais didn't invent pedals. Those came in the 1860s.
- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: the Drais ride on June 12, 1817 (Britannica, Technoseum state museum, Smithsonian). Single strong source: the forgery verdict (Britannica, plus Lessing's paper, which I saw only secondhand). Unknown: who drew it.
- **Sources:**
  - Britannica — Bicycle (https://www.britannica.com/technology/bicycle). Supports the da Vinci sketch as a forgery added in the 1960s.
  - Britannica — Karl von Drais de Sauerbrun (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karl-von-Drais-de-Sauerbrun) and Draisienne (https://www.britannica.com/technology/draisienne). Supports 1817, the 14 km (9 mi) ride, and the 1818 Paris exhibition.
  - Technoseum (Mannheim state museum) — Freiherr von Drais und die Geschichte des Fahrrades (https://www.technoseum.de/fileadmin/media/pdf/mupaed-material/Schulheft_2R%C3%A4der_200Jahre.pdf). Supports the first ride from Mannheim on June 12, 1817.
  - Smithsonian — Draisine (https://www.si.edu/collections/snapshot/draisine). Supports Drais's machine.
  - Lead only, not strong: Pryor Dodge — The Leonardo da Vinci Bicycle Hoax (https://pryordodge.com/The_Bicycle_Leonardo.html). Supports Lessing's 1997 paper, Marinoni's 1974 announcement, and the pencil argument.
- **Why it works:** Viewers "know" da Vinci drew a bicycle. Taking that away gives the episode its twist.
- **Before script lock:** find a second strong source for the forgery, such as Lessing's own book or paper, or a museum statement. If none turns up, cut this one and use the ATM reserve instead.

### The Ancient Holy-Water Vending Machine — Fit 3/5
- **Credited:** Coin-operated vending machines are usually traced to the early 1880s in London, where machines sold postcards at railway stations and post offices.
- **Actually first:** Around the 1st century CE, Hero (Heron) of Alexandria described a coin-operated dispenser in his *Pneumatica*. A coin dropped into a slot landed on a balance-beam lever, which lifted a plug and let out a measured squirt of holy water until the coin slid off.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** The vending machine is about 1,800 years older than the candy bar. Hero of Alexandria described a temple device where your coin tipped a little lever and paid for a squirt of holy water.
- **Hook (cold-open line):** "The first vending machine didn't sell snacks or soda. It sold holy water, about 2,000 years ago."
- **Hedges needed on air:** This is a "3" because no famous person is wrongly credited, just a general belief that vending machines are modern. Hero *described* the device and may not have invented it, so say "described" or "Hero's design." One Britannica student page says Hero reported such machines in Egyptian temples "in about 215 BC." That's a confusing claim, so don't use the date. The "people were taking more holy water than they paid for" motive appeared in an extract without a clear source, so don't use it. Hero's dates are about 10–70 CE, so say "1st century."
- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: the coin-operated device in Hero's *Pneumatica* (Britannica's Pneumatica entry lists "coin-operated machines," and Britannica Students describes the mechanism; the LOC holds the 1851 English translation, primary). Note that both Britannica entries come from one publisher, so independence is weak; the LOC primary text is the independent check, and I saw its record but not the page itself. Confirmed: 1880s London postcard machines (Britannica). Unknown: whether any such machine was actually built or used.
- **Sources:**
  - Britannica — Pneumatica (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pneumatica). Supports *Pneumatica* describing coin-operated machines.
  - Britannica Students — Vending machine (https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/vending-machine/277563). Supports the coin, lever, and plug mechanism for holy water.
  - Library of Congress — The pneumatics of Hero of Alexandria, from the original Greek (https://www.loc.gov/item/07041532/). Primary text (Woodcroft translation).
  - Britannica — Heron of Alexandria (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Heron-of-Alexandria). Supports his dates and works.
  - Britannica — Vending machine (https://www.britannica.com/money/vending-machine). Supports the first modern coin machines in early-1880s London dispensing postcards.
- **Why it works:** The gap between "pay to pray" in ancient Egypt and Victorian postcard machines is very sayable.
- **Before script lock:** check the actual Pneumatica entry in the LOC translation (it's the "sacrificial vessel which flows only when money is introduced" device) to make the second source fully independent.

### The Machine Only Gamblers Loved: Who Really Built the First ATM — Fit 4/5
- **Credited:** John Shepherd-Barron (De La Rue) and Barclays. The first cash machine went in at Barclays Enfield, north London, on 27 June 1967, and obituaries called him "the inventor of the ATM."
- **Actually first:** Luther George Simjian's Bankograph. He filed the patent on June 30, 1960, and it was granted Feb 26, 1963. City Bank of New York (now Citi) ran a trial in a lobby, about 1961 per secondary sources, and dropped it after about six months for lack of demand. It took deposits (cash, checks, coins in envelopes) but did **not** dispense cash.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** "Six years before London got its cash machine, a New York bank tested a machine that took deposits. It was pulled after about six months, and its inventor reportedly said the only people using it were prostitutes and gamblers who didn't want to face a teller."
- **Hook (cold-open line):** "The first bank machine in America didn't hand out a single dollar. And according to its inventor, its best customers were gamblers."
- **Hedges needed on air:** It depends on what counts as an "ATM." Simjian's machine was deposit-only. Barclays 1967 really is the first *cash-dispensing* machine. The "prostitutes and gamblers" line is Simjian's reported remark, not a bank record. The exact 1961 install date came only from Wikipedia-tier and secondary sources. Don't claim there were no other 1960s rivals. That was not checked.
- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: the Bankograph existed (1960 filing), had a New York bank trial and was removed after about 6 months; Barclays Enfield 27 June 1967; Shepherd-Barron/De La Rue. Inferred: 1961 install year. Unknown: other early-1960s claimants (not researched).
- **Sources:**
  - Smithsonian Magazine, "The ATM is Dead. Long Live the ATM!" (https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/atm-dead-long-live-atm-180953838/): Bankograph in a New York bank lobby, the prostitutes/gamblers remark, Shepherd-Barron's 1967 machine
  - Lemelson-MIT Program, "Luther Simjian" (https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/luther-simjian): Bankograph 1960, City Bank trial, six months, little demand, prostitutes/gamblers
  - Barclays Group Archives, "Cash machines" (https://www.archive.barclays.com/items/show/5415): Enfield 27 June 1967 first cash machine, Reg Varney first customer
  - Historic England, "London Bank" Enfield listing (https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/news/enfield-bank-listed/): site of first cashpoint, 1967
- **Why it works:** Everyone uses ATMs, and "the first one was a flop that only gamblers used" is an irresistible twist.

### The First Selfie Was Taken in 1839 — Fit 3/5
- **Credited:** The smartphone/social-media era. "Selfie" feels like a 2010s invention.
- **Actually first:** Robert Cornelius, a Philadelphia lamp-shop worker and amateur chemist, made a daguerreotype self-portrait outdoors behind his family's store in October or November 1839. That was months after Daguerre's August 1839 announcement. It is held by the Library of Congress.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** "On the back of his 1839 self-portrait, Robert Cornelius wrote: 'The first light picture ever taken.'"

- **Hedges needed on air:** The Library of Congress calls it "believed to be the earliest extant American portrait photo." "First selfie" is a popular label (Smithsonian uses it, with "many believe"). Say "often called the first selfie." His inscription was a boast, not proven fact. Exposure-time figures conflict between sources (about a minute vs 10–15 minutes), so leave them out.
- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: Cornelius self-portrait, Oct/Nov 1839, the inscription, LOC holding, made outdoors at the family store. Inferred: "first selfie anywhere" (no earlier self-portrait was checked, e.g., in Europe). Unknown: exact exposure time.

  - Library of Congress, "Robert Cornelius. Self-portrait. 1839" exhibition page (https://www.loc.gov/exhibitions/images-from-americas-library/about-this-exhibition/icons/robert-cornelius-self-portrait-1839/): Oct/Nov 1839, made outdoors
