# 100 Episodes: "Somebody Did It First"

Prepared 2026-10-02. One hundred episodes, each with a finished recording script (see `scripts/README.md`), a cold-open hook, and a fun fact verified against two or more strong sources (museums, journals, national libraries, Britannica, Smithsonian, IEEE, patents and similar; never Wikipedia or content farms).

**Updated 2026-10-02 after the script fact-checks (Re-check A to D).** Every correction those rounds made to a fact was folded back in here: about 110 lines across the two research files, plus 16 fun facts. Where this file and a script's `scripts/notes/` file ever disagree, the notes file is the later record.

## How this list was built

- **48 episodes were already researched:** the launch episode, the strongest 37 of the original 65 (fit 4–5, including two reworked angles), and the 10 new topics from the first pass. Their fun facts are the verified lines from `episode-research.md` and `launch-episode-fact-notes.md`.
- **52 episodes are new**, researched today. Sixty candidates went through the same two-strong-sources check:
  - Penicillin (Duchesne before Fleming) was **cut**, because a peer-reviewed paper calls that story a fable.
  - Eight others passed but were weaker or duplicative, so they sit in the **reserve list** at the bottom.
- **Method:** all checks came from search-engine extracts. This container can't open web pages directly. Every "Confirmed with hedge" entry lists, in its detail, exactly what must not be overstated on air.
- **Titles and hooks:** these now match the final, fact-checked scripts. Some titles were changed during script review where the old one claimed more than the research supports.
- **Order:** the order below is a suggested release order. It starts with the launch, then rotates through six categories, strongest first within each, so viewers don't get six Edison-era inventors in a row.

**Category counts:** Technology & Media 22 · Food & Drink 19 · Science & Medicine 17 · Culture & Society 17 · Everyday Things 13 · Math, Space & Exploration 12.

### 12. Pascal Wasn't First: The Triangle That Is 600 Years Older Than Pascal — Fit 5/5
- **Credited:** Blaise Pascal. The triangle carries his name from his *Traité du triangle arithmétique* (written 1654, published 1665).
- **Actually first:** Al-Karaji (probably born in Karaj, Persia, but holding an official post and writing his main works in Baghdad) worked out the triangle around 1000 CE. Jia Xian (China, mid-11th century) laid out the coefficients as a triangle, up to n = 6. Yang Hui printed it in 1261 in *Xiangjie Jiuzhang Suanfa* and said he took it from Jia Xian. Omar Khayyam (11th–12th c.) also studied it.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** "In China it's called Yang Hui's triangle. But in his 1261 book Yang Hui said he got it from Jia Xian, who had worked it out about two centuries earlier. So even the Chinese name goes to the guy who passed it along."
- **Hook (cold-open line):** "Every math student learns Pascal's triangle. Pascal was born almost six hundred years after someone else first drew it."
- **Hedges needed on air:** Jia Xian's own book is lost; we know his triangle through Yang Hui. Al-Karaji's work also survives mainly through later writers. Don't say Pingala "drew the triangle". That claim wasn't checked this pass, so leave India out or say "Indian scholars studied related counting rules". Pascal's real contribution, a systematic treatise with proofs and probability uses, should be acknowledged.
- **Confirmed vs. inferred vs. unknown:** Confirmed: Jia Xian (11th c.), Yang Hui 1261 crediting Jia Xian, al-Karaji c. 1000, Pascal's treatise 1654/1665. Inferred: the "Tartaglia's triangle" (Italy) and "Khayyam's triangle" (Iran) names, which only MacTutor supports. Unknown: the Pingala/Halayudha (India) lineage.
- **Sources:**
  - Britannica — Yang Hui (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Yang-Hui): Yang Hui popularized it; known as Yang Hui's triangle; Jia Xian earlier
  - Britannica — Binomial theorem (https://www.britannica.com/science/binomial-theorem): al-Karaji c. 1000; Jia Xian's triangle; Pascal 1665 "reinvented"

### 13. 3,000 Years Before Goodyear: The Real Inventors of Rubber — Fit 5/5

- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge

Goodyear's 1839 discovery still stands as the industrial process. The Hancock patent-race detail is single-source (Science Museum) and should be phrased softly.

### 16. Heinz Didn't Invent Ketchup - It Started as Fish Sauce — Fit 5/5
- **Credited:** H.J. Heinz. The popular belief is that Heinz (tomato ketchup, 1876) invented ketchup.
- **Actually first:** A fermented fish sauce from Southern China called *kê-tsiap* (Hokkien) gave the condiment its name. Ketchup was reaching England by the late 1600s: the word is already in English print then (the OED's earliest citations run from 1682, with "catchup" in B.E.'s 1690 canting dictionary). The earliest English ketchup recipe usually cited is Eliza Smith's *The Compleat Housewife* (1727), an anchovy-based "katchup". Mushroom, walnut and oyster ketchups were common in 1700s Britain. The first published tomato ketchup recipe usually cited is by Philadelphia scientist James Mease, in 1812. Heinz started selling ketchup in 1876, about 64 years later.
- **Verdict:** Confirmed with hedge
- **Verified fun fact (on-air line):** "The word ketchup traces back to a fermented fish sauce from southern China, called kê-tsiap in the Hokkien dialect. The tomato version historians usually start with is from 1812, decades before Heinz bottled a drop."
- **Hook (cold-open line):** "The ketchup on your fries started life as fish sauce, and Heinz showed up about 64 years after the earliest tomato recipe historians usually cite."
- **Hedges needed on air:** Say Mease's 1812 recipe is "the earliest published tomato ketchup recipe historians usually cite," not "the first ever." Weak sites claim an 1801 tomato "catsup" recipe in Sandy Addison's *Sugar House Book*, which I could not verify in any strong source. Say ketchup "comes from" or "traces to" the Hokkien word; the exact path from Asia to Britain is generally explained as British traders bringing it home, which is inferred. Heinz's real contribution was commercializing, standardizing and promoting ketchup (glass bottles, the pure-food push), so credit him for that. ### 97. Before Morse: The Telegraph That Caught a Killer — Fit 4/5
